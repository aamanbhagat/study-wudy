## 1. What it is — in plain English

Imagine you have a tiny, flexible rubber band floating in a fluid, like water or air. This rubber band isn't just a passive observer; it's actually made up of the same fluid particles, so it moves and deforms with the flow.

Now, imagine you try to measure how much the fluid "spins" or "rotates" around this rubber band. This "spinning amount" is what physicists call **circulation**. Think of it like a tiny whirlpool's strength measured along the path of your rubber band.

Kelvin's circulation theorem tells us something remarkable: if the fluid is "ideal" (meaning it's perfectly smooth with no stickiness, and its density only depends on pressure, and any external pushes are simple forces like gravity), then this "spinning amount" or circulation around our special rubber band **will never change over time**. It stays constant, even as the fluid moves, swirls, and the rubber band stretches or shrinks.

So, in essence, it's a conservation law: under specific ideal conditions, the "twist" or "vortex strength" associated with a closed loop of fluid particles is a permanent feature of that loop.

## 2. Why it matters — real-world applications

Kelvin's circulation theorem, while based on ideal fluid conditions, provides fundamental insights into many real-world phenomena and forms the basis for more complex models.

1.  **Aerospace Engineering (Lift Generation):** The lift generated by an aircraft wing is directly related to the circulation around it, as described by the Kutta-Joukowski theorem. Kelvin's theorem helps explain how this circulation is established and maintained. When a wing starts moving, it generates a "starting vortex" that is shed from the trailing edge. By Kelvin's theorem, the circulation around the wing (which is a material circuit in the fluid) must be equal and opposite to the circulation of this starting vortex, ensuring the total circulation in the fluid remains zero if it started from rest. This understanding is crucial for designing efficient airfoils and understanding phenomena like wingtip vortices. Companies like **Boeing** and **Airbus** use principles derived from circulation theory in their aerodynamic design software.

2.  **Meteorology and Oceanography (Hurricanes and Ocean Gyres):** The persistence and intensity of large-scale atmospheric and oceanic vortices, such as hurricanes, typhoons, and major ocean currents (like the North Atlantic Gyre), can be qualitatively understood through Kelvin's theorem. While real atmospheric and oceanic flows are viscous and non-barotropic, the theorem explains why large-scale rotational motions, once established, tend to be remarkably stable and long-lived, especially in regions where viscous effects are relatively small compared to inertial forces. The **National Hurricane Center** uses models that implicitly account for circulation dynamics.

3.  **Turbulence Research:** While turbulence itself is characterized by chaotic, dissipative vortex structures, Kelvin's theorem provides a baseline for understanding the behavior of coherent vortex structures within turbulent flows. It helps explain why large eddies can persist for some time before breaking down, and how energy might be transferred between different scales of motion. Researchers at institutions like **NASA Ames Research Center** study vortex dynamics in turbulent flows for applications ranging from aircraft design to re-entry vehicles.

4.  **Hydraulic Engineering (Turbine and Pump Design):** The efficiency of hydraulic machinery like pumps, turbines, and propellers is heavily dependent on the circulation patterns they induce in the fluid. Understanding how circulation is generated, maintained, and how it interacts with solid boundaries is critical for optimizing their design to maximize energy transfer and minimize losses. For instance, **General Electric (GE)** and **Siemens Energy** design large-scale hydroturbines and industrial pumps where circulation control is a key design factor.

## 3. Prerequisites — what you must know first

To fully grasp Kelvin's circulation theorem, you should have a solid understanding of the following concepts:

*   **Vector Calculus:**
    *   **Gradient ($\nabla$):** A vector field pointing in the direction of the greatest rate of increase of a scalar field.
    *   **Divergence ($\nabla \cdot$):** A scalar field representing the magnitude of a source or sink at a given point in a vector field.
    *   **Curl ($\nabla \times$):** A vector field representing the infinitesimal rotation of a vector field.
    *   **Line Integrals ($\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$):** Integrating a vector field along a curve, often representing work done or circulation.
    *   **Surface Integrals ($\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$):** Integrating a vector field over a surface.
    *   **Stokes' Theorem:** Relates the line integral of a vector field around a closed curve to the surface integral of the curl of the field over any surface bounded by that curve.

*   **Fluid Kinematics:**
    *   **Velocity Field ($\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t)$):** A vector field describing the velocity of fluid particles at every point in space and time.
    *   **Material Derivative ($\frac{D}{Dt}$):** Describes the rate of change of a property of a specific fluid particle as it moves through space; also known as the substantial or total derivative.
    *   **Lagrangian vs. Eulerian Descriptions:** Lagrangian tracks individual fluid particles, while Eulerian describes fluid properties at fixed points in space. Kelvin's theorem deals with a Lagrangian concept (a material loop).

*   **Fluid Dynamics:**
    *   **Euler Equations of Motion:** The fundamental equations describing the motion of an inviscid (frictionless) fluid.
    *   **Navier-Stokes Equations:** The general equations of motion for viscous fluids; Euler equations are a special case.
    *   **Inviscid Flow:** A flow where viscous (frictional) forces are negligible.
    *   **Incompressible Flow:** A flow where the density of the fluid remains constant.
    *   **Barotropic Flow:** A flow where the pressure is solely a function of density ($p = f(\rho)$). This implies that surfaces of constant pressure coincide with surfaces of constant density.
    *   **Conservative Body Forces:** Forces that can be expressed as the negative gradient of a scalar potential ($\mathbf{f}_b = -\nabla\Phi$), such as gravity.

*   **Thermodynamics (briefly):**
    *   **Isentropic Process:** A thermodynamic process where entropy remains constant. Barotropic flows are often, but not always, associated with isentropic conditions for ideal gases.

## 4. The core idea — step by step

Let's break down Kelvin's circulation theorem into its fundamental components, building intuition along the way.

### Step 1: What is Circulation ($\Gamma$)?

*   **Plain-English Statement:** Circulation is a measure of the "net rotation" or "swirl" of a fluid around a closed loop. It quantifies how much the fluid tends to flow *along* the path of the loop.

*   **Small Concrete Example:** Imagine a small, closed path (like a circle) drawn in a river. If the river flows uniformly, the circulation around that circle is zero because the flow going "with" one side of the circle is canceled by the flow going "against" the other side. However, if there's a whirlpool, and you draw a circle around its center, the fluid velocity will be largely tangential to your circle, and the circulation will be significant and non-zero.

*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:** Circulation, denoted by $\Gamma$ (Gamma), around a closed curve $C$ is defined as the line integral of the fluid velocity vector $\mathbf{u}$ along that curve:

    $$ \Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$

    Here, $\mathbf{u}$ is the fluid velocity vector at a point on the curve, and $d\mathbf{l}$ is an infinitesimal vector element tangent to the curve $C$ and pointing in the direction of integration.

*   **What Could Go Wrong:** A common mistake is to confuse circulation with vorticity ($\boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u}$). While related by Stokes' Theorem ($\Gamma = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{u}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iint_S \boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$), circulation is an integral property over a *closed curve*, while vorticity is a *point property* of the fluid, representing the local angular velocity of a fluid particle. Circulation is the *total* "spin" enclosed by the loop, while vorticity is the *density* of spin at a point.

### Step 2: What is a Material Loop $C(t)$?

*   **Plain-English Statement:** A material loop (or material circuit) is a closed curve that is always composed of the *same* fluid particles. As the fluid moves, these particles move and deform, and the loop moves and deforms along with them. It's like drawing a closed shape on a balloon and then inflating or twisting the balloon – the shape changes, but it's always the *same* rubber making up the shape.

*   **Small Concrete Example:** Imagine you place a ring of tiny, neutrally buoyant markers (like dye particles) in a fluid. As the fluid flows, these markers will move and their arrangement will change, but they will always form a closed loop, albeit a deforming one. Kelvin's theorem applies to *this specific, moving, deforming loop* of fluid particles.

*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:** If a curve $C(t)$ is a material loop, it means that every point $\mathbf{x}(t)$ on the curve moves with the local fluid velocity $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}(t), t)$. That is, $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t)$ for particles on the curve. This is a Lagrangian description of the curve.

*   **What Could Go Wrong:** Applying the theorem to a loop that is fixed in space, or one that is arbitrarily chosen at different times. Kelvin's theorem *only* holds for loops that are "made of" the same fluid particles. If you're observing a fixed region, fluid particles are constantly entering and leaving the region, and circulation is generally *not* conserved.

### Step 3: The Conditions for Conservation

*   **Plain-English Statement:** For the circulation to be conserved, the fluid needs to be "ideal" in a few specific ways: it can't be sticky (no viscosity), its density must only depend on its pressure (barotropic), and any external forces acting on it must be "simple" or "conservative" (like gravity, which can be derived from a potential).

*   **Small Concrete Example:**
    *   **Inviscid:** Water is a good approximation of an inviscid fluid in many large-scale flows, while honey or oil are highly viscous. Kelvin's theorem wouldn't apply well to honey.
    *   **Barotropic:** Air in the atmosphere is often approximated as barotropic if its temperature variations are linked directly to pressure changes (e.g., in an adiabatic process). A fluid where density depends on both pressure and temperature independently (like saltwater where salinity also affects density) is generally not barotropic.
    *   **Conservative Body Forces:** Gravity is the classic example. The force on a fluid particle due to gravity depends only on its position, not its velocity or path. An electromagnetic force that depends on the particle's velocity would generally *not* be conservative.

*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:** The fluid must satisfy these conditions:
    1.  **Inviscid:** The fluid has zero viscosity ($\mu = 0$). This means there are no internal frictional forces between fluid layers. The Navier-Stokes equations simplify to the Euler equations.
    2.  **Barotropic:** The pressure $p$ is a function of density $\rho$ only, i.e., $p = f(\rho)$. This implies that $\nabla p = (\frac{dp}{d\rho})\nabla\rho$. For a barotropic fluid, the term $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p$ can be written as $\nabla \int \frac{dp}{\rho}$.
    3.  **Conservative Body Forces:** Any external body forces $\mathbf{f}_b$ (per unit mass) acting on the fluid can be expressed as the negative gradient of a scalar potential $\Phi$, i.e., $\mathbf{f}_b = -\nabla\Phi$. Gravity ($\mathbf{g} = -\nabla(gz)$) is the most common example.

*   **What Could Go Wrong:** Ignoring any of these conditions. If the fluid is viscous, or if density variations are independent of pressure (e.g., due to temperature or salinity gradients), or if non-conservative forces are present, then Kelvin's theorem does not hold, and circulation will generally *not* be conserved.

### Step 4: The Statement of the Theorem

*   **Plain-English Statement:** If you pick a closed loop of fluid particles (a material loop) in a fluid that is perfectly smooth (inviscid), where density only depends on pressure (barotropic), and where external pushes are simple forces like gravity (conservative body forces), then the "spinning amount" (circulation) around that loop will *never change* as the fluid flows and the loop deforms. It's a constant value for that specific loop.

*   **Small Concrete Example:** Imagine a large, calm lake. If you somehow create a small, perfectly circular ring of fluid that has a slight initial spin (circulation), and the lake water is approximated as ideal, then that ring of fluid, even as it moves, stretches, and deforms due to other currents, will always maintain that same amount of "spin." If it started with zero spin, it will always have zero spin.

*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:** For a material loop $C(t)$ in an inviscid, barotropic fluid under conservative body forces, the material derivative of the circulation is zero:

    $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = 0 $$

    This means $\Gamma = \text{constant}$ for any given material loop.

*   **What Could Go Wrong:** Forgetting that this applies to a *material* loop, not an arbitrary, fixed loop in space. Also, forgetting *any* of the three crucial conditions (inviscid, barotropic, conservative forces).

### Step 5: Derivation Overview (Intuition)

*   **Plain-English Statement:** To prove this, we start with the definition of circulation for a material loop and see how it changes over time. We use a special mathematical tool (Leibniz's theorem for line integrals) to handle the fact that our loop is moving and deforming. Then, we substitute the fluid's equation of motion (Euler's equation) into the expression. Finally, by applying the conditions (inviscid, barotropic, conservative forces), we show that all the terms that *could* cause a change in circulation cancel out, leaving zero.

*   **Formal/Mathematical Version (Conceptual Steps):**
    1.  Start with the definition of circulation: $\Gamma(t) = \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$.
    2.  Apply the material derivative to this integral. For a material curve $C(t)$, the material derivative of the circulation is given by:
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_{C(t)} \left( \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \cdot d\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{u} \cdot d\left(\frac{D\mathbf{l}}{Dt}\right) \right) $$
        (This is a form of Reynolds Transport Theorem for line integrals, or more specifically, the material derivative of a line integral.)
    3.  Recall Euler's equation for an inviscid fluid under body forces $\mathbf{f}_b$:
        $$ \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{f}_b $$
    4.  Substitute Euler's equation into the expression for $\frac{D\Gamma}{Dt}$.
    5.  Use the fact that for a material element $d\mathbf{l}$, $\frac{D(d\mathbf{l})}{Dt} = (d\mathbf{l} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}$. This is a bit advanced, but it essentially means how the segment stretches and rotates.
    6.  Combine terms and use vector identities. Specifically, the term $\oint_C \mathbf{u} \cdot d\left(\frac{D\mathbf{l}}{Dt}\right)$ simplifies to $\oint_C \mathbf{u} \cdot (d\mathbf{l} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}$. Using the identity $\mathbf{u} \cdot (d\mathbf{l} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = \frac{1}{2} d(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}) - (\mathbf{u} \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{u})) \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ and some manipulation, we arrive at:
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_C \left( -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{f}_b \right) \cdot d\mathbf{l} + \oint_C \frac{1}{2}\nabla(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}) \cdot d\mathbf{l} - \oint_C (\mathbf{u} \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{u})) \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
        This is getting complicated, let's simplify the intuition for the derivation. The key simplification comes from Euler's equation directly.
        A more direct way to look at the change in circulation is:
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_{C(t)} \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \cdot d\mathbf{l} + \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{u} \cdot \frac{D(d\mathbf{l})}{Dt} $$
        Using $\frac{D(d\mathbf{l})}{Dt} = (d\mathbf{l} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}$ and the identity $\mathbf{u} \cdot (d\mathbf{l} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = d(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^2) - d\mathbf{l} \cdot (\mathbf{u} \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{u}))$.
        So,
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_C \left( \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} - \mathbf{u} \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{u}) \right) \cdot d\mathbf{l} + \oint_C d\left(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^2\right) $$
        The last term $\oint_C d(\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^2)$ is zero because it's an exact differential integrated over a closed loop.
        So,
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_C \left( \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} - \mathbf{u} \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{u}) \right) \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
        Now, substitute Euler's equation: $\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{f}_b$.
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_C \left( -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{f}_b - \mathbf{u} \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{u}) \right) \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
        Now, apply the conditions:
        *   **Conservative Body Forces:** $\mathbf{f}_b = -\nabla\Phi$. So, $\oint_C \mathbf{f}_b \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \oint_C -\nabla\Phi \cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ (integral of a gradient over a closed loop is zero).
        *   **Barotropic Fluid:** $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p = \nabla \int \frac{dp}{\rho}$. So, $\oint_C -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \oint_C -\nabla \left( \int \frac{dp}{\rho} \right) \cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ (integral of a gradient over a closed loop is zero).
        *   The remaining term is $\oint_C -(\mathbf{u} \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{u})) \cdot d\mathbf{l}$. This term is not generally zero. *This is where I need to be careful with the derivation.*
        Let's restart the derivation intuition from a more standard approach.

        The fundamental derivation starts with the material derivative of circulation:
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \frac{D}{Dt} \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
        Using a form of Reynolds Transport Theorem for a line integral (or by direct calculation), this expands to:
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_{C(t)} \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} \right) \cdot d\mathbf{l} + \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{u} \cdot \left( \frac{\partial (d\mathbf{l})}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla) d\mathbf{l} \right) $$
        No, this is incorrect. The correct form for the material derivative of circulation is:
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_{C(t)} \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
        This is a crucial simplification for a material loop! If the curve is material, then $d\mathbf{l}$ is always moving with the fluid, so the change in $\mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is solely due to the change in $\mathbf{u}$ for the particles that form $d\mathbf{l}$. This is a key insight.

        Now substitute Euler's equation: $\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{f}_b$.
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_{C(t)} \left( -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{f}_b \right) \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
        Now apply the conditions:
        1.  **Conservative Body Forces:** $\mathbf{f}_b = -\nabla\Phi$.
            So, $\oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{f}_b \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \oint_{C(t)} -\nabla\Phi \cdot d\mathbf{l}$. Since the line integral of a gradient of a scalar field over a closed loop is zero (as $\nabla\Phi \cdot d\mathbf{l} = d\Phi$), this term is zero.
            $$ \oint_{C(t)} -\nabla\Phi \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\oint_{C(t)} d\Phi = 0 $$
        2.  **Barotropic Fluid:** $p = f(\rho)$. This implies $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p = \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dp}{d\rho}\nabla\rho$. This can be written as $\nabla \left( \int \frac{dp}{\rho} \right)$. Let $P(\rho) = \int \frac{dp}{\rho}$. Then $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p = \nabla P$.
            So, $\oint_{C(t)} -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \oint_{C(t)} -\nabla P \cdot d\mathbf{l}$. Again, the line integral of a gradient of a scalar field over a closed loop is zero.
            $$ \oint_{C(t)} -\nabla P \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\oint_{C(t)} dP = 0 $$
        Since both terms on the right-hand side are zero under these conditions, we conclude:
        $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = 0 $$
        This is the formal derivation. The intuition is that the forces that *could* change the circulation (pressure gradients and body forces) are "nice" enough (conservative, or derivable from a potential) that their net effect around any closed loop is zero.

## 5. Worked examples — multiple, with every step shown

### Example 1: Fluid Initially at Rest

**Problem:** Consider an inviscid, barotropic fluid that is initially everywhere at rest ($\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, 0) = \mathbf{0}$). Show that the circulation around any material loop in this fluid remains zero for all subsequent times, assuming only conservative body forces act on it.

**Given:**
*   Fluid is inviscid.
*   Fluid is barotropic ($p=f(\rho)$).
*   Body forces are conservative ($\mathbf{f}_b = -\nabla\Phi$).
*   Initial velocity field $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, 0) = \mathbf{0}$.
*   We want to show $\Gamma(t) = 0$ for all $t$ for any material loop $C(t)$.

**Solution:**

1.  **Recall Kelvin's Circulation Theorem:**
    Under the given conditions (inviscid, barotropic fluid, conservative body forces), Kelvin's Circulation Theorem states that the material derivative of circulation $\Gamma$ around any material loop $C(t)$ is zero:
    $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = 0 $$
    *This is the fundamental principle we will use.*

2.  **Interpret the Theorem:**
    The statement $\frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = 0$ means that the circulation $\Gamma$ for a specific material loop $C(t)$ is constant with respect to time.
    $$ \Gamma(t) = \text{constant} $$
    *This tells us that if we know the circulation at one point in time, we know it for all time for that material loop.*

3.  **Calculate Initial Circulation:**
    The problem states that the fluid is initially everywhere at rest. This means that at time $t=0$, the velocity field $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, 0)$ is zero for all $\mathbf{x}$.
    $$ \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, 0) = \mathbf{0} $$
    The definition of circulation around a closed loop $C(t)$ is:
    $$ \Gamma(t) = \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
    At $t=0$, for any arbitrary material loop $C(0)$, the circulation is:
    $$ \Gamma(0) = \oint_{C(0)} \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, 0) \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
    Substitute the initial velocity:
    $$ \Gamma(0) = \oint_{C(0)} \mathbf{0} \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
    The dot product of any vector with the zero vector is zero, and the integral of zero is zero.
    $$ \Gamma(0) = 0 $$
    *We've found the initial value of circulation for any material loop.*

4.  **Apply Conservation:**
    Since Kelvin's Theorem states that $\Gamma(t)$ is constant for a material loop, and we found $\Gamma(0) = 0$, it must be that for all subsequent times $t$, the circulation for that same material loop remains zero.
    $$ \Gamma(t) = \Gamma(0) = 0 $$
    *This directly follows from the conservation principle.*

5.  **Conclusion:**
    Therefore, the circulation around any material loop in this fluid remains zero for all subsequent times.

    $$ \boxed{\Gamma(t) = 0 \quad \text{for all } t} $$

**Reflection:** This example highlights the power of Kelvin's theorem. If a fluid starts from rest and meets the ideal conditions, no amount of pressure gradients or conservative body forces can ever induce circulation. This is why you can't create a whirlpool in a perfectly still, ideal fluid just by applying gravity or by pushing on it from the outside (unless you introduce non-conservative forces or viscosity, or an initial disturbance).

### Example 2: Circulation in a Uniformly Rotating Fluid

**Problem:** A large tank of an inviscid, incompressible (and therefore barotropic) fluid is rotating uniformly as a rigid body with angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \Omega \hat{\mathbf{k}}$. Consider a circular material loop of radius $R_0$ that is initially centered on the axis of rotation ($z$-axis) and lies in the $xy$-plane. Find the circulation around this loop at any time $t$.

**Given:**
*   Fluid is inviscid.
*   Fluid is incompressible (density $\rho = \text{constant}$), which implies it is barotropic ($p=f(\rho)$ is trivially true if $\rho$ is constant).
*   No external body forces (or conservative and uniform, so they integrate to zero).
*   Initial fluid velocity is rigid-body rotation: $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \boldsymbol{\Omega} \times \mathbf{x}$, where $\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \Omega \hat{\mathbf{k}}$.
*   Initial material loop $C(0)$ is a circle of radius $R_0$ in the $xy$-plane, centered at the origin.

**Solution:**

1.  **Check Conditions for Kelvin's Theorem:**
    *   **Inviscid:** Given.
    *   **Barotropic:** Given as incompressible, so density is constant. This means $p$ can be any function, and $\rho$ is constant, so $p$ is trivially a function of $\rho$. Thus, it is barotropic.
    *   **Conservative Body Forces:** Assuming no body forces or only conservative ones (like gravity, which would be uniform and integrate to zero around a horizontal loop). So, Kelvin's theorem applies.
    *   **Material Loop:** The problem specifies a material loop.

    Since all conditions are met, Kelvin's Circulation Theorem holds:
    $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = 0 \quad \implies \quad \Gamma(t) = \text{constant} $$
    *This means we only need to calculate the initial circulation.*

2.  **Define the Velocity Field:**
    The velocity field for rigid-body rotation is $\mathbf{u} = \boldsymbol{\Omega} \times \mathbf{x}$.
    Given $\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \Omega \hat{\mathbf{k}}$ and $\mathbf{x} = x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + z\hat{\mathbf{k}}$.
    $$ \mathbf{u} = (\Omega \hat{\mathbf{k}}) \times (x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + z\hat{\mathbf{k}}) $$
    $$ \mathbf{u} = \Omega x (\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{i}}) + \Omega y (\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{j}}) + \Omega z (\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{k}}) $$
    Recall $\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{i}} = \hat{\mathbf{j}}$, $\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{j}} = -\hat{\mathbf{i}}$, and $\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{k}} = \mathbf{0}$.
    $$ \mathbf{u} = \Omega x \hat{\mathbf{j}} - \Omega y \hat{\mathbf{i}} $$
    So, $\mathbf{u} = -\Omega y \hat{\mathbf{i}} + \Omega x \hat{\mathbf{j}}$.
    *This is the velocity vector at any point $(x,y,z)$ in the fluid.*

3.  **Parameterize the Initial Material Loop $C(0)$:**
    The loop $C(0)$ is a circle of radius $R_0$ in the $xy$-plane, centered at the origin.
    We can parameterize this loop using polar coordinates:
    $$ \mathbf{x}(s) = R_0 \cos s \hat{\mathbf{i}} + R_0 \sin s \hat{\mathbf{j}} + 0 \hat{\mathbf{k}} $$
    where $s$ varies from $0$ to $2\pi$.
    The differential line element $d\mathbf{l}$ is:
    $$ d\mathbf{l} = \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{ds} ds = (-R_0 \sin s \hat{\mathbf{i}} + R_0 \cos s \hat{\mathbf{j}}) ds $$
    *This defines the path and direction of integration for our initial loop.*

4.  **Calculate the Initial Circulation $\Gamma(0)$:**
    Substitute $\mathbf{u}$ and $d\mathbf{l}$ into the circulation definition:
    $$ \Gamma(0) = \oint_{C(0)} \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
    At a point $(x,y)$ on $C(0)$, $x = R_0 \cos s$ and $y = R_0 \sin s$.
    So, $\mathbf{u}$ on $C(0)$ is:
    $$ \mathbf{u} = -\Omega (R_0 \sin s) \hat{\mathbf{i}} + \Omega (R_0 \cos s) \hat{\mathbf{j}} $$
    Now, calculate the dot product $\mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$:
    $$ \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = (-\Omega R_0 \sin s \hat{\mathbf{i}} + \Omega R_0 \cos s \hat{\mathbf{j}}) \cdot (-R_0 \sin s \hat{\mathbf{i}} + R_0 \cos s \hat{\mathbf{j}}) ds $$
    $$ \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = [ (-\Omega R_0 \sin s)(-R_0 \sin s) + (\Omega R_0 \cos s)(R_0 \cos s) ] ds $$
    $$ \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = [ \Omega R_0^2 \sin^2 s + \Omega R_0^2 \cos^2 s ] ds $$
    $$ \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \Omega R_0^2 (\sin^2 s + \cos^2 s) ds $$
    Using the identity $\sin^2 s + \cos^2 s = 1$:
    $$ \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \Omega R_0^2 ds $$
    Now integrate this around the loop from $s=0$ to $s=2\pi$:
    $$ \Gamma(0) = \int_0^{2\pi} \Omega R_0^2 ds $$
    $$ \Gamma(0) = \Omega R_0^2 [s]_0^{2\pi} $$
    $$ \Gamma(0) = \Omega R_0^2 (2\pi - 0) $$
    $$ \Gamma(0) = 2\pi \Omega R_0^2 $$
    *This is the initial circulation for the material loop.*

5.  **Apply Conservation to Find Circulation at Any Time $t$:**
    Since Kelvin's theorem states that $\Gamma(t)$ is constant for a material loop under these conditions:
    $$ \Gamma(t) = \Gamma(0) $$
    $$ \Gamma(t) = 2\pi \Omega R_0^2 $$
    *The circulation remains constant.*

6.  **Alternative using Stokes' Theorem (for initial circulation):**
    For a planar loop, Stokes' Theorem states $\Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{u}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} dS$.
    First, calculate the vorticity $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u}$.
    $\mathbf{u} = -\Omega y \hat{\mathbf{i}} + \Omega x \hat{\mathbf{j}}$.
    $$ \nabla \times \mathbf{u} = \left| \begin{matrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ -\Omega y & \Omega x & 0 \end{matrix} \right| $$
    $$ \nabla \times \mathbf{u} = \hat{\mathbf{i}}(0-0) - \hat{\mathbf{j}}(0-0) + \hat{\mathbf{k}}(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\Omega x) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-\Omega y)) $$
    $$ \nabla \times \mathbf{u} = \hat{\mathbf{k}}(\Omega - (-\Omega)) = 2\Omega \hat{\mathbf{k}} $$
    So, the vorticity is uniform and equal to $2\Omega \hat{\mathbf{k}}$.
    For the circular loop in the $xy$-plane, the normal vector $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ is $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$.
    $$ \Gamma(0) = \iint_S (2\Omega \hat{\mathbf{k}}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{k}} dS $$
    $$ \Gamma(0) = \iint_S 2\Omega dS $$
    Since $2\Omega$ is a constant, it can be pulled out of the integral. The integral $\iint_S dS$ is simply the area of the surface $S$, which is $\pi R_0^2$.
    $$ \Gamma(0) = 2\Omega (\pi R_0^2) = 2\pi \Omega R_0^2 $$
    This matches the result from direct line integration, confirming the initial circulation.

**Final Answer:** The circulation around the material loop at any time $t$ is:
$$ \boxed{\Gamma(t) = 2\pi \Omega R_0^2} $$

**Reflection:** This example demonstrates that if a fluid already possesses circulation (like in rigid-body rotation), Kelvin's theorem ensures that this circulation is conserved for any material loop under ideal conditions. The value of the circulation depends on the initial angular velocity and the initial area enclosed by the loop. Even if the loop deforms, its associated circulation remains the same.

### Example 3: Non-Conservation due to Non-Barotropic Effects

**Problem:** Consider a 2D, inviscid fluid where the density $\rho$ is not only a function of pressure $p$ but also of temperature $T$, i.e., $\rho = \rho(p, T)$. Assume there are no body forces. Can Kelvin's circulation theorem be applied to this flow? If not, show why.

**Given:**
*   2D, inviscid fluid.
*   Density $\rho = \rho(p, T)$, meaning it's *not* barotropic.
*   No body forces ($\mathbf{f}_b = \mathbf{0}$).

**Solution:**

1.  **Recall the Conditions for Kelvin's Theorem:**
    Kelvin's Circulation Theorem holds for a material loop $C(t)$ if the fluid is:
    *   Inviscid
    *   Barotropic ($p=f(\rho)$)
    *   Subject to conservative body forces ($\mathbf{f}_b = -\nabla\Phi$)

2.  **Check Given Conditions Against Requirements:**
    *   **Inviscid:** Given as true.
    *   **Barotropic:** Given as $\rho = \rho(p, T)$, which explicitly states that density depends on both pressure and temperature. This means the fluid is **not barotropic**.
    *   **Conservative Body Forces:** Given as no body forces, so $\mathbf{f}_b = \mathbf{0}$, which is a conservative force (potential $\Phi = \text{constant}$). This condition is met.

3.  **Identify the Failing Condition:**
    The fluid is not barotropic. This violates a fundamental condition for Kelvin's Circulation Theorem.

4.  **Show Why Non-Barotropic Flow Prevents Conservation (Derivation Snippet):**
    Start with the material derivative of circulation (as derived in Step 5 of the core idea):
    $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_{C(t)} \left( -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{f}_b \right) \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
    Since there are no body forces, $\mathbf{f}_b = \mathbf{0}$, so the term $\oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{f}_b \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is zero.
    The equation simplifies to:
    $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_{C(t)} -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
    For Kelvin's theorem to hold, this integral must be zero. We know that $\oint_{C(t)} -\nabla P \cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ if $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p$ can be written as the gradient of a scalar function $P$. This is only possible if the fluid is barotropic, i.e., $p = f(\rho)$, which allows us to define $P = \int \frac{dp}{\rho}$.

    However, if the fluid is *not* barotropic, then $p$ is not solely a function of $\rho$. In this case, $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p$ cannot generally be expressed as the gradient of a scalar function $P(\rho)$.
    More rigorously, consider the integrand $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p$. By Stokes' Theorem, the line integral $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is zero if and only if $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ (for a simply connected region).
    Let's compute the curl of the integrand:
    $$ \nabla \times \left( \frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \right) $$
    Using the vector identity $\nabla \times (\phi \mathbf{A}) = \phi (\nabla \times \mathbf{A}) + (\nabla \phi) \times \mathbf{A}$, where $\phi = 1/\rho$ and $\mathbf{A} = \nabla p$:
    $$ \nabla \times \left( \frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \right) = \frac{1}{\rho} (\nabla \times \nabla p) + (\nabla \frac{1}{\rho}) \times \nabla p $$
    The curl of a gradient is always zero, so $\nabla \times \nabla p = \mathbf{0}$.
    $$ \nabla \times \left( \frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \right) = (\nabla \frac{1}{\rho}) \times \nabla p $$
    $$ \nabla \times \left( \frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \right) = -\frac{1}{\rho^2} (\nabla \rho) \times \nabla p $$
    For the integral $\oint_{C(t)} -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ to be zero for any arbitrary loop, the integrand $-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p$ must be a conservative vector field, meaning its curl must be zero.
    So, we need $-\frac{1}{\rho^2} (\nabla \rho) \times \nabla p = \mathbf{0}$. This implies $(\nabla \rho) \times \nabla p = \mathbf{0}$.
    The condition $(\nabla \rho) \times \nabla p = \mathbf{0}$ means that the gradient of density ($\nabla \rho$) must be parallel to the gradient of pressure ($\nabla p$). This happens if and only if surfaces of constant density are parallel to surfaces of constant pressure.
    If surfaces of constant density are parallel to surfaces of constant pressure, then $\rho$ must be a function of $p$ only (or vice versa), which is the definition of a barotropic fluid.
    However, the problem states that $\rho = \rho(p, T)$. This means that $\rho$ can change independently of $p$ if $T$ changes. In such a fluid, the surfaces of constant pressure and constant density are generally *not* parallel. For example, in a stratified atmosphere, pressure decreases with height, but temperature can vary independently, leading to situations where surfaces of constant pressure are not parallel to surfaces of constant density.
    Therefore, $(\nabla \rho) \times \nabla p \neq \mathbf{0}$ in general for a non-barotropic fluid.

5.  **Conclusion:**
    Since the fluid is not barotropic, the term $\oint_{C(t)} -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is generally **not zero**.
    Therefore, $\frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} \neq 0$, and Kelvin's Circulation Theorem **cannot be applied** to state that circulation is conserved.

    $$ \boxed{\text{Kelvin's Circulation Theorem cannot be applied because the fluid is not barotropic.}} $$

**Reflection:** This example highlights the critical importance of the barotropic condition. Non-barotropic effects, where pressure and density surfaces are not aligned, introduce a mechanism for circulation to change. This is crucial in geophysical fluid dynamics, where buoyancy forces (related to density variations) and temperature gradients play a significant role in generating or destroying circulation, for example, in the formation of weather systems.

### Example 4: Initial Vorticity and Circulation in a 2D Incompressible Flow

**Problem:** A 2D, inviscid, incompressible fluid has a velocity field given by $\mathbf{u}(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 2xy\hat{\mathbf{j}}$. There are no body forces. Consider a square material loop $C$ with vertices at $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(1,1)$, and $(0,1)$ at $t=0$. Find the circulation around this loop at any later time $t$.

**Given:**
*   2D, inviscid, incompressible fluid.
*   Velocity field $\mathbf{u}(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 2xy\hat{\mathbf{j}}$.
*   No body forces ($\mathbf{f}_b = \mathbf{0}$).
*   Initial material loop $C(0)$ is a square with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(1,1)$, $(0,1)$.

**Solution:**

1.  **Check Conditions for Kelvin's Theorem:**
    *   **Inviscid:** Given.
    *   **Incompressible:** Given. For an incompressible fluid, $\rho = \text{constant}$, which means it is barotropic ($p=f(\rho)$ is trivially true).
    *   **Conservative Body Forces:** Given as no body forces, which is conservative.
    *   **Material Loop:** The problem specifies a material loop.

    All conditions are met. Therefore, Kelvin's Circulation Theorem applies, meaning $\Gamma(t) = \text{constant}$ for this material loop. We only need to calculate the initial circulation.

2.  **Calculate Initial Circulation $\Gamma(0)$ using Stokes' Theorem:**
    For a 2D flow in the $xy$-plane, the circulation is given by $\Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$. By Stokes' Theorem, this is equal to $\iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{u}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{k}} dS$, where $S$ is the surface enclosed by $C$ and $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ is the normal vector to the $xy$-plane.
    First, find the vorticity $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u}$:
    $$ \mathbf{u} = u\hat{\mathbf{i}} + v\hat{\mathbf{j}} = (x^2 - y^2)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 2xy\hat{\mathbf{j}} $$
    $$ \boldsymbol{\omega} = \left( \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right) \hat{\mathbf{k}} $$
    Calculate the partial derivatives:
    $$ \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-2xy) = -2y $$
    $$ \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2 - y^2) = -2y $$
    Now substitute these into the vorticity equation:
    $$ \boldsymbol{\omega} = (-2y - (-2y))\hat{\mathbf{k}} $$
    $$ \boldsymbol{\omega} = (0)\hat{\mathbf{k}} = \mathbf{0} $$
    *The vorticity of this flow field is identically zero everywhere.*

3.  **Interpret Zero Vorticity:**
    A flow field with zero vorticity ($\nabla \times \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$) is called an irrotational flow.

4.  **Calculate $\Gamma(0)$ for an Irrotational Flow:**
    Since $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \mathbf{0}$, the circulation integral becomes:
    $$ \Gamma(0) = \iint_S (\mathbf{0}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{k}} dS $$
    $$ \Gamma(0) = \iint_S 0 \, dS = 0 $$
    *The initial circulation around the square loop is zero.*

5.  **Apply Conservation:**
    Because Kelvin's theorem holds and the initial circulation is zero, the circulation around this material loop must remain zero for all subsequent times.
    $$ \Gamma(t) = \Gamma(0) = 0 $$

6.  **Verify Incompressibility (Optional but good practice):**
    For a 2D incompressible flow, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$.
    $$ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} $$
    $$ \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2 - y^2) = 2x $$
    $$ \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-2xy) = -2x $$
    $$ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 2x + (-2x) = 0 $$
    The flow is indeed incompressible.

**Final Answer:** The circulation around the material loop at any later time $t$ is:
$$ \boxed{\Gamma(t) = 0} $$

**Reflection:** This example demonstrates that if a flow is initially irrotational (zero vorticity everywhere), and it meets the conditions for Kelvin's theorem, then the circulation around any material loop will always remain zero. This is a powerful result: you cannot create "spin" from an initially "spin-free" ideal fluid without violating one of the theorem's conditions. This flow field $\mathbf{u}(x,y) = (x^2 - y^2)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 2xy\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ is actually a potential flow, as its vorticity is zero everywhere. It represents a specific type of deformation flow.

## 6. Common mistakes and traps

1.  **Applying to non-material loops:** The most frequent error is assuming Kelvin's theorem applies to a fixed loop in space or a loop that doesn't track specific fluid particles. Remember, the theorem is inherently Lagrangian.
2.  **Ignoring the inviscid condition:** Students often forget that viscosity, no matter how small, can dissipate or generate circulation. Real fluids are always viscous, so the theorem is an idealization.
3.  **Ignoring the barotropic condition:** This is a subtle but critical trap. If density depends on more than just pressure (e.g., temperature, salinity), then pressure surfaces and density surfaces may not be parallel, and circulation is generally not conserved.
4.  **Ignoring the conservative body force condition:** If non-conservative forces (like electromagnetic forces in MHD or some forms of frictional forces) are present, they can change the circulation.
5.  **Confusing circulation with vorticity:** While related by Stokes' Theorem, circulation is an integral quantity over a closed curve, representing the total "spin" within that curve, whereas vorticity is a differential (point) quantity representing the local angular velocity of a fluid particle.
6.  **Assuming circulation is always zero:** Kelvin's theorem states that circulation is *conserved*, not that it is always zero. If a material loop starts with non-zero circulation (e.g., in a rotating fluid), it will maintain that non-zero value. It's only zero if the initial circulation was zero.

## 7. Textbook-precise explanation

Kelvin's Circulation Theorem is a fundamental principle in fluid dynamics, stating the conditions under which the circulation around a material fluid loop is conserved.

Let $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t)$ be the velocity field of a fluid, and let $C(t)$ be a closed material curve (or material circuit) moving with the fluid. The circulation $\Gamma$ around $C(t)$ is defined as the line integral of the velocity field along the curve:

$$ \Gamma(t) = \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$

Kelvin's Circulation Theorem states that for an **inviscid**, **barotropic** fluid under the influence of **conservative body forces**, the material derivative of the circulation around any material loop $C(t)$ is zero:

$$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = 0 $$

This implies that $\Gamma(t) = \text{constant}$ for a given material loop.

**Derivation:**

1.  **Material Derivative of Circulation:** The material derivative of the circulation around a material curve $C(t)$ is given by:
    $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \frac{D}{Dt} \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \oint_{C(t)} \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
    This step is a specific application of the Reynolds Transport Theorem for a line integral, which simplifies significantly for a material curve.

2.  **Euler's Equation of Motion:** For an inviscid fluid, the equation of motion (Euler's equation) is:
    $$ \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{f}_b $$
    where $\rho$ is the fluid density, $p$ is the pressure, and $\mathbf{f}_b$ is the body force per unit mass.

3.  **Substitution into Circulation Equation:** Substitute Euler's equation into the expression for $\frac{D\Gamma}{Dt}$:
    $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_{C(t)} \left( -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{f}_b \right) \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
    $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_{C(t)} -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \cdot d\mathbf{l} + \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{f}_b \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$

4.  **Application of Conditions:**
    *   **Conservative Body Forces:** If $\mathbf{f}_b$ is a conservative body force, it can be expressed as the negative gradient of a scalar potential $\Phi$, i.e., $\mathbf{f}_b = -\nabla\Phi$. The line integral of a gradient of a scalar field over a closed loop is always zero:
        $$ \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{f}_b \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \oint_{C(t)} -\nabla\Phi \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\oint_{C(t)} d\Phi = 0 $$
    *   **Barotropic Fluid:** If the fluid is barotropic, pressure $p$ is solely a function of density $\rho$, i.e., $p = f(\rho)$. Under this condition, the term $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p$ can be expressed as the gradient of a scalar function $P(\rho) = \int \frac{dp}{\rho}$. That is, $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p = \nabla P$. Similar to the conservative body force term, the line integral of a gradient of a scalar field over a closed loop is zero:
        $$ \oint_{C(t)} -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \oint_{C(t)} -\nabla P \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\oint_{C(t)} dP = 0 $$
        (Alternatively, by Stokes' Theorem, $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$. The curl of $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p$ is $\nabla \times (\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p) = -\frac{1}{\rho^2}(\nabla\rho \times \nabla p)$. For this to be zero, $\nabla\rho$ must be parallel to $\nabla p$, which is the definition of a barotropic fluid.)

5.  **Conclusion:** Since both integral terms are zero under the specified conditions, we have:
    $$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = 0 $$

This theorem is a cornerstone of classical fluid dynamics, particularly in the study of vortex motion and potential flow. It implies that if a fluid is initially irrotational (i.e., $\Gamma=0$ for all reducible loops), it remains irrotational under these ideal conditions.

If you put a tiny, spinning toy (representing circulation) inside a segment of this Jell-O loop, that specific Jell-O loop will always contain the same "spin" as it moves and deforms, as long as the Jell-O doesn't get sticky (viscous), doesn't change density weirdly (barotropic), and no strange forces act on it (conservative). The key is that it's the *same Jell-O* (material loop).

    2.  **Definition of Circulation:** $\Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$.
    3.  **The Four Critical Conditions:** Inviscid, Barotropic, Conservative Body Forces, Material Loop.

3.  **Spaced-Repetition Schedule:**
    *   **Review 1:** After 1 day
    *   **Review 2:** After 3 days

    *   **Review 4:** After 16 days
    *   **Review 5:** After 35 days
    *   *Self-test yourself by trying to state the theorem, its conditions, and sketch the derivation.*

4.  **First-Principles Re-derivation Pathway:**
    If you forget the theorem, you can rebuild it by following these steps:
    1.  **Start with the definition of circulation:** $\Gamma(t) = \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$.
    2.  **Apply the material derivative:** Remember that for a material loop, $\frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_{C(t)} \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$. (This is the trickiest step to recall precisely, but intuitive if you think about tracking specific particles).
    3.  **Substitute Euler's Equation:** Replace $\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt}$ with $(-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \mathbf{f}_b)$.
    4.  **Break the integral into two parts:** $\oint_{C(t)} -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \cdot d\mathbf{l} + \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{f}_b \cdot d\mathbf{l}$.
    5.  **Apply the conditions to each integral:**
        *   For conservative body forces ($\mathbf{f}_b = -\nabla\Phi$), the integral $\oint -\nabla\Phi \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is zero (integral of an exact differential over a closed loop).
        *   For a barotropic fluid ($p=f(\rho)$), $\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p$ can be written as $\nabla P$ for some scalar $P$. Thus, $\oint -\nabla P \cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is also zero.
    6.  **Conclude:** Since both terms are zero, $\frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = 0$.
