## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Electromagnetic waves are self-sustaining oscillations of E and B fields that satisfy the wave equation obtained by taking curls of Maxwell’s source-free equations in vacuum.**

Maxwell’s four equations already contain the possibility of waves. When charge density ρ and current density J are zero, the curl of Faraday’s law combined with the Ampère-Maxwell law produces a second-order partial differential equation whose solutions travel at finite speed c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀). The same equation appears for both E and B, so the fields must oscillate together and perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

The derivation therefore consists of vector calculus identities plus the two homogeneous Maxwell equations; no new postulates are added. Once the wave equation exists, plane-wave solutions, polarization, energy transport, and radiation all follow directly.

> [!NOTE]
> The single deepest insight is that the displacement-current term μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t is what closes the loop between E and B, allowing the fields to “bootstrap” each other and propagate without any medium.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
SpaceX’s Starlink satellites transmit at 10–40 GHz; the phased-array antennas are sized using the exact wavelength λ = c/f that emerges from this derivation. Any mismatch between predicted and actual c produces pointing errors of kilometres at GEO distances.

LIGO’s 4 km arms detect gravitational waves by measuring picometre changes in laser phase; the 1064 nm light itself is an electromagnetic wave whose vacuum propagation speed is fixed by μ₀ε₀. Calibration papers cite the Maxwell-derived wave equation to bound systematic errors below 10⁻²³ strain.

Semiconductor EUV lithography at 13.5 nm relies on plasma sources whose radiated fields are solutions of the same inhomogeneous wave equation; ASML’s optical design software solves the vector Helmholtz equation that descends directly from the source-free case derived here.

Pulsar timing arrays measure nanohertz gravitational waves by monitoring millisecond pulsar signals; the radio pulses are electromagnetic waves whose group velocity in the interstellar plasma is again set by the same c, allowing dispersion-measure corrections that preserve timing residuals at the 100 ns level.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Write the source-free Maxwell equations
In vacuum, ρ = 0 and J = 0, so the four equations collapse to two curl equations and two divergence equations. The divergence equations later force the fields to be transverse; the curl equations generate the dynamics.

### Step 2 — Take the curl of Faraday’s law
Start with ∇ × E = −∂B/∂t. Apply the curl operator to both sides and interchange the order of differentiation on the right-hand side to obtain ∇ × (∇ × E) = −∂(∇ × B)/∂t.

### Step 3 — Substitute Ampère-Maxwell law
Replace ∇ × B with μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t. The left side becomes ∇ × (∇ × E) = −μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t². This is the pivotal substitution that introduces the second time derivative.

### Step 4 — Invoke the vector identity
Use ∇ × (∇ × E) = ∇(∇·E) − ∇²E. In free space ∇·E = 0, so the identity simplifies to −∇²E = −μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t², or ∇²E − μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t² = 0.

### Step 5 — Repeat for B and identify wave speed
The identical procedure applied to the magnetic field yields ∇²B − μ₀ε₀ ∂²B/∂t² = 0. Comparing with the standard wave equation ∇²ψ − (1/c²)∂²ψ/∂t² = 0 immediately shows c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀).

### Step 6 — Plane-wave solutions and transversality
Assume solutions of the form E = E₀ exp[i(k·r − ωt)]. The wave equation forces ω = ck together with k · E₀ = 0 and k · B₀ = 0, proving that both fields are perpendicular to the propagation direction and to each other.

> [!WARNING]
> If you forget to set ∇·E = 0 before applying the vector identity, an extra longitudinal term survives and the wave equation is incorrect.

## 5. Worked examples — har step show karo

**Example 1 — Verify the wave equation for a plane wave**  
*Given:* E = E₀ cos(kz − ωt) x̂ with ω = ck.  
*Find:* Show that it satisfies ∇²E − μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t² = 0.  
Compute ∂²E/∂z² = −k² E.  
Compute ∂²E/∂t² = −ω² E.  
Substitute: −k² E + μ₀ε₀ ω² E = 0.  
Because ω = ck and c² = 1/(μ₀ε₀), the coefficients cancel.  
**Final answer:** The equation holds identically.  
*Reflection:* This example confirms consistency but does not derive c; the derivation must still come from Maxwell’s equations.

**Example 2 — Derive the relation B₀ = E₀/c**  
*Given:* Plane wave E = E₀ exp[i(kz − ωt)] x̂.  
*Find:* Amplitude of accompanying B.  
From ∇ × E = −∂B/∂t, the only non-zero component yields ik E₀ = iω B₀, hence B₀ = E₀/c.  
*Why:* Faraday’s law directly links the spatial derivative of E to the time derivative of B.  
**Final answer:** |B₀| = |E₀|/c.  
*Reflection:* The factor 1/c is required by dimensional consistency and by the impedance of free space.

**Example 3 — Show transversality from Gauss’s law**  
*Given:* Same plane wave.  
*Find:* k · E₀.  
∇·E = ik Eₓ = 0 implies k Eₓ = 0. Since k ≠ 0, Eₓ = 0.  
*Why:* Divergence-free condition forbids longitudinal components for propagating waves.  
**Final answer:** k ⊥ E₀.  
*Reflection:* This step uses the homogeneous Maxwell equations that were not needed for the wave equation itself.

**Example 4 — Energy flux for a sinusoidal wave**  
*Given:* E and B amplitudes from Example 2.  
*Find:* Time-averaged Poynting vector magnitude.  
S = (1/μ₀) E × B, |S_avg| = (E₀ B₀)/(2μ₀) = E₀²/(2μ₀ c).  
*Why:* The factor 1/2 arises from averaging cos² over one period.  
**Final answer:** ⟨S⟩ = E₀²/(2 Z₀) where Z₀ = √(μ₀/ε₀).  
*Reflection:* Radiation pressure and solar-sail calculations begin from this expression.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap | Why it happens | How to avoid it |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| Forgetting displacement current | Students treat Ampère’s law as ∇ × B = μ₀ J only | Always write the full Ampère-Maxwell law before substituting |
| Using ∇² in non-Cartesian coordinates without care | The identity ∇ × (∇ × V) = ∇(∇·V) − ∇²V holds only in Cartesian or with extra curvature terms | Stay in Cartesian until comfortable with curvilinear forms |
| Assuming E and B can be parallel | Intuitive picture of “light wiggling” is vague | Enforce k · E = 0 and E · B = 0 at the plane-wave step |
| Sign error in Faraday vs Ampère | Mixing the minus sign in ∇ × E = −∂B/∂t | Write both curl equations side-by-side before taking curls |
| Treating c as input rather than output | Historical habit of inserting speed of light by hand | Derive c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) explicitly; never insert it earlier |
| Ignoring that ρ = 0 and J = 0 must hold everywhere | Near-field or antenna problems violate this | State the vacuum assumption clearly at the start of every derivation |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
In any region where ρ = 0 and J = 0, Maxwell’s equations imply that each Cartesian component of E and of B satisfies the homogeneous wave equation
$$
\nabla^2 \psi - \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial t^2} = 0,
$$
where the propagation speed is exactly c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀). (Griffiths, *Introduction to Electrodynamics*, 4e, §9.2.1, equations 9.19–9.21.)
