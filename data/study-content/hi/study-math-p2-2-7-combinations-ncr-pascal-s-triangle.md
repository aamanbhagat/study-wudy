## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Combinations (nCr) count the number of ways to choose r distinct items from n items when order does not matter, and Pascal’s triangle is the visual arrangement of these values where each entry equals the sum of the two entries directly above it.**

Aap already know that permutations care about order, but combinations ignore order completely. The formula therefore divides the permutation count by r! to remove the internal arrangements of the chosen items. This same quantity appears as the r-th entry in the n-th row of Pascal’s triangle because every path that reaches that position must come from one of the two positions immediately above it.

The central insight is that the recursive addition rule in Pascal’s triangle is mathematically identical to the identity C(n,r) = C(n-1,r-1) + C(n-1,r). Once you see this identity, both the formula and the triangle become two views of the same counting process.

> [!NOTE]
> The single most useful “aha” is that every number in Pascal’s triangle is already a combination; you never need to memorise separate rows—you simply read C(n,r) directly from the triangle.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Counting without order
When you must pick r objects out of n and the sequence inside the selection does not matter, every group of r objects is counted r! times in the corresponding permutation count.  
Concrete example: choosing 2 letters from {A,B,C}. The ordered pairs are AB, BA, AC, CA, BC, CB—six of them—yet the unordered sets are only {A,B}, {A,C}, {B,C}.  
Formal statement:  
$$C(n,r)=\frac{P(n,r)}{r!}=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}.$$  
> [!WARNING]  
> If you forget to divide by r! you will over-count by exactly the number of ways to arrange the chosen items.

### Step 2 — The boundary conditions
C(n,0) must equal 1 because there is exactly one way to choose nothing. Likewise C(n,n)=1. These two facts become the leftmost and rightmost 1’s in every row of Pascal’s triangle.  
Formal statement:  
$$C(n,0)=1,\qquad C(n,n)=1.$$

### Step 3 — The additive recurrence
Any selection of r items from n items either includes the n-th item or it does not.  
- If it includes the n-th item, you still need to choose r−1 from the first n−1.  
- If it excludes the n-th item, you choose all r from the first n−1.  
Hence the Pascal addition rule appears automatically.  
Formal statement:  
$$C(n,r)=C(n-1,r-1)+C(n-1,r).$$

### Step 4 — Building Pascal’s triangle row by row
Start with row 0: 1.  
Each subsequent row is generated by placing 1 at both ends and filling interior entries via the recurrence above. The entry in position r of row n is exactly C(n,r).

### Step 5 — Symmetry identity
Because choosing r items to keep is the same as choosing n−r items to discard, the triangle is symmetric:  
$$C(n,r)=C(n,n-r).$$

### Step 6 — Closed-form verification
Substituting the factorial definition into the recurrence and using algebraic cancellation confirms that the binomial coefficient formula satisfies the same recurrence and boundary conditions, proving uniqueness.

## 5. Worked examples — har step show karo

**Example 1 — Simple two-item choice**  
*Given:* n=5, r=2.  
*Find:* C(5,2).  
Step 1: Write the formula → C(5,2)=5!/(2!·3!).  
Step 2: Expand 5!=120, 2!=2, 3!=6 → 120/(2·6)=10.  
*Why* each move: we replace the symbols with their numeric values and cancel common factors.  
**10**

*Reflection:* The calculation is short, yet it already shows why order cancellation is mandatory; without the 2! the answer would wrongly be 60.

**Example 2 — Using Pascal’s triangle**  
*Given:* Build row 6 of Pascal’s triangle.  
*Find:* All C(6,r) for r=0 to 6.  
Row 5 is 1 5 10 10 5 1.  
Add adjacent pairs: 1, 1+5=6, 5+10=15, 10+10=20, 10+5=15, 5+1=6, 1.  
**Row 6: 1 6 15 20 15 6 1**

*Reflection:* You obtain six distinct binomial coefficients without ever writing a factorial.

**Example 3 — Larger value with symmetry**  
*Given:* C(12,9).  
*Find:* Its value.  
Apply symmetry first: C(12,9)=C(12,3).  
C(12,3)=12!/(3!·9!)= (12×11×10)/(3×2×1)=220.  
**220**

*Reflection:* Symmetry reduces arithmetic; always check whether r or n−r is smaller.

**Example 4 — Word problem with combinations**  
*Given:* A committee of 4 people must be formed from 7 men and 5 women; we want exactly 2 women.  
*Find:* Number of possible committees.  
Choose 2 women out of 5 → C(5,2).  
Choose 2 men out of 7 → C(7,2).  
Total = C(5,2)×C(7,2)=10×21=210.  
**210**

*Reflection:* The multiplication principle appears once the two independent choices are settled; each sub-choice is itself a combination.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                        | Why it happens                              | How to avoid it                              |
|-----------------------------|---------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Using P(n,r) instead of C(n,r) | Students forget order is irrelevant         | Always ask “does sequence inside the group matter?” |
| Writing C(n,r)=n!/r!        | Missing the (n−r)! term                     | Keep the full three-factor denominator visible |
| Off-by-one row indexing     | Thinking row n starts at C(n,0) yet counting from 0 | Label rows explicitly 0 through n            |
| Treating C(n,r) as ordered when r=1 | Intuition fails for trivial cases           | Verify boundary cases C(n,1)=n               |
| Calculator overflow on large n | Direct factorial computation                | Cancel factors before multiplying            |
| Ignoring symmetry           | Unnecessary computation                     | Compare r with n−r at the start              |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
A combination of n objects taken r at a time is the number of r-element subsets of an n-element set and is given by  
$$ \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} \quad (0\le r\le n), $$  
where the binomial coefficient is defined to be zero when r<0 or r>n. These numbers satisfy the Pascal identity  
$$ \binom{n}{r} = \binom{n-1}{r-1} + \binom{n-1}{r} $$  
with boundary conditions \(\binom{n}{0}=\binom{n}{n}=1\). (Rosen, *Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications*, 8e, §6.3)

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture a pyramid of oranges; every orange rests on exactly two oranges below it. The number of oranges at each spot is C(n,r).  
2. **What to overlearn** — The closed formula, the additive recurrence, and the symmetry identity C(n,r)=C(n,n-r).  
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review after 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.  
4. **First-principles fallback** — If the formula disappears, rebuild from the story: “total ordered selections divided by internal arrangements” or from the two-case split that produces the Pascal recurrence.
