## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Forces on a rocket in flight consist of thrust acting along the body axis, aerodynamic forces resolved into axial and normal components in the body frame, and gravitational force acting vertically downward in the inertial frame.**

Thrust arises from momentum expulsion at the nozzle and pushes the vehicle forward along its long axis. Gravity always pulls the center of mass toward Earth’s center with magnitude \(mg\), independent of orientation. Aerodynamic forces appear only inside the atmosphere: the axial component opposes motion along the body axis while the normal component acts perpendicular to that axis and is produced by any angle of attack.

These three forces together determine the translational acceleration of the center of mass and the rotational moments that must be countered by the guidance system. In vacuum the aerodynamic terms vanish, leaving only thrust and gravity; inside the atmosphere all three must be carried simultaneously in the equations of motion.

> [!NOTE]
> The single deepest insight is that aerodynamic forces are most naturally expressed in the body frame (axial/normal) while gravity is expressed in the inertial frame; every useful flight-mechanics equation therefore requires an explicit rotation matrix between the two frames.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
SpaceX recovers Falcon 9 first stages by throttling thrust, modulating angle of attack, and balancing axial drag against gravity during entry; the guidance algorithm solves the force balance at 100 Hz to keep peak heating and loads within limits. NASA’s Artemis I trajectory design explicitly accounts for the normal-force spike that occurs when the SLS stack encounters high-dynamic-pressure winds aloft, because that spike produces bending moments that the thrust-vector-control system must cancel.

Modern sounding-rocket campaigns at Andøya Space Center use real-time axial-force estimates derived from onboard accelerometers to trigger parachute deployment only after drag has decelerated the vehicle below a prescribed threshold. Hypersonic boost-glide vehicles such as those under development by the U.S. Air Force rely on sustained normal-force generation at small angles of attack to produce the lift-to-drag ratio needed for cross-range maneuvers while gravity continuously curves the trajectory downward.

## 3. Mental prerequisites

| Concept                  | Why you need it here |
|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Newton’s second law \(\vec{F}=m\vec{a}\) | Supplies the direct link from net force vector to center-of-mass acceleration in inertial space. |
| Body-axis versus inertial reference frames | Aerodynamic forces are measured in body axes; gravity is measured in inertial axes; the two must be reconciled by a direction-cosine matrix. |
| Definition of angle of attack \(\alpha\) | Normal force is proportional to \(\alpha\) at small angles; without this kinematic variable the aerodynamic model cannot be written. |

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Thrust is a body-axis force of known magnitude and fixed direction
Thrust is generated by expelling mass rearward; the reaction force therefore lies exactly along the nozzle centerline, which for a conventional rocket coincides with the vehicle longitudinal axis.  
Example: a Merlin engine at full throttle produces 845 kN directed forward along the body axis regardless of the rocket’s pitch attitude.  
The vector statement in body coordinates is  
\[
\vec{T}^b = \begin{bmatrix} T \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
> [!WARNING]
> Treating thrust as an inertial-frame vector leads to incorrect moment arms when the vehicle is at angle of attack.

### Step 2 — Gravity is an inertial-frame force whose line of action passes through the center of mass
Gravity acts on every particle; the net effect is a single force \(mg\) directed toward Earth’s center. In a locally flat-Earth inertial frame the gravity vector is simply  
\[
\vec{W}^i = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -mg \end{bmatrix}.
\]
> [!WARNING]
> Omitting the distinction between body and inertial frames when adding gravity produces fictitious side forces that violate Newton’s laws.

### Step 3 — Aerodynamic forces are resolved into axial and normal components in the body frame
The total aerodynamic force vector lies in the plane defined by the velocity vector and the body axis. Its projection along the body axis is the axial force \(A\) (primarily pressure + skin-friction drag); its projection perpendicular to the body axis is the normal force \(N\) (primarily lift).  
In body axes:  
\[
\vec{F}_a^b = \begin{bmatrix} -A \\ N \end{bmatrix}.
\]
> [!WARNING]
> Using wind-axis drag and lift without rotating them into body axes omits the coupling that produces pitching moments.

### Step 4 — The net force in inertial coordinates is obtained by rotating the body-frame forces and adding gravity
The direction-cosine matrix \(C^{i/b}\) transforms body vectors into inertial components. The translational equation therefore reads  
\[
m \ddot{\vec{r}}^i = C^{i/b} \bigl( \vec{T}^b + \vec{F}_a^b \bigr) + \vec{W}^i.
\]
This is the textbook statement reached after the preceding four steps.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Vertical ascent at burnout**  
*Given:* 5000 kg rocket, thrust 60 kN along body axis, no atmosphere, vertical flight.  
*Find:* initial upward acceleration.  
Step 1: Net force = \(T - mg = 60000 - 5000 \times 9.81\).  
*Why:* only thrust and gravity act.  
Step 2: \(a = 60000 / 5000 - 9.81 = 2.19\) m s⁻².  
*Why:* divide by mass after subtracting weight.  
**2.19 m s⁻² upward**  

*Reflection:* The example isolates gravity subtraction; any later angled case merely adds the same term after rotation.

**Example 2 — Steady climb at constant 10° angle of attack**  
*Given:* axial force 800 N, normal force 1200 N, thrust 25 kN, mass 1200 kg.  
*Find:* net axial acceleration in body frame.  
Step 1: Body-frame net force \(F_x^b = T - A = 25000 - 800\).  
*Why:* axial components add directly.  
Step 2: \(a_x^b = 24200 / 1200 = 20.17\) m s⁻².  
**20.17 m s⁻²**  

*Reflection:* Normal force does not appear because we asked only for axial acceleration.

**Example 3 — Force transformation at angle of attack**  
*Given:* \(A = 1200\) N, \(N = 300\) N, \(\alpha = 5^\circ\).  
*Find:* wind-axis drag.  
Step 1: Drag = \(A\cos\alpha + N\sin\alpha\).  
*Why:* projection of body forces onto velocity vector.  
Step 2: \(D = 1200 \times 0.9962 + 300 \times 0.0872 \approx 1221\) N.  
**1221 N**  

*Reflection:* Demonstrates why body-frame coefficients must be rotated before comparison with wind-tunnel data.

**Example 4 — Full inertial acceleration during gravity turn**  
*Given:* mass 3000 kg, \(T = 40\) kN, \(A = 1500\) N, \(N = 800\) N, flight-path angle 30°, body angle 35°.  
*Find:* inertial acceleration components.  
Step 1: Rotate body forces: \(F_x^i = (T - A)\cos 35^\circ - N\sin 35^\circ\).  
*Why:* direction-cosine matrix for 2-D case.  
Step 2: Add gravity term \(-mg\sin 30^\circ\).  
Step 3: Divide by mass.  
Numerical result: \(a_x^i = 9.12\) m s⁻², \(a_z^i = -3.41\) m s⁻².  
**\( \vec{a}^i = (9.12, -3.41) \) m s⁻²**

*Reflection:* Combines all three forces and the required frame rotation; the arithmetic is longer but each line follows directly from Step 4.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap | Why it happens | How to avoid it |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| Adding normal force directly to inertial y-component | Forgetting the rotation matrix | Always write \(C^{i/b}\) explicitly before summing vectors |
| Treating thrust magnitude as constant when chamber pressure drops | Confusing vacuum thrust with sea-level thrust | Use \(T = \dot{m}v_e + (p_e - p_a)A_e\) at each altitude |
| Sign error on axial force (drag is negative) | Inconsistent body-axis convention | Adopt “positive x forward, positive axial force rearward” once and for all |
| Neglecting that gravity has zero moment arm about CM | Thinking weight produces torque | Realize gravity acts at CM; only offset masses or thrust misalignment produce moments |
| Using dynamic pressure with body velocity instead of air-relative velocity | Ignoring wind | Compute \(\alpha\) from \(\vec{v}_{air} = \vec{v}_{body} - \vec{v}_{wind}\) |
| Assuming normal force is zero at \(\alpha = 0\) for symmetric rockets | Overlooking manufacturing misalignments | Retain a small bias term or measure it during wind-tunnel calibration |
| Forgetting that axial force includes base drag that changes with nozzle pressure ratio | Treating aerodynamics as independent of thrust | Couple the aerodynamic and propulsion models at the base |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
In a body-fixed coordinate system whose origin is at the instantaneous center of mass, the external force vector is
\[
\vec{F}^b = \vec{T}^b + \vec{F}_a^b(\alpha,M,q_\infty) + C^{b/i}\vec{W}^i,
\]
where \(\vec{T}^b = (T,0,0)^\top\), \(\vec{F}_a^b = (-A(\alpha,M,q_\infty),N(\alpha,M,q_\infty),0)^\top\), and \(\vec{W}^i = (0,-mg,0)^\top\). The inertial acceleration of the center of mass then satisfies
\[
m \ddot{\vec{r}}^i = C^{i/b} \vec{F}^b.
\]
This decomposition is stated formally in Zipfel, *Modeling and Simulation of Aerospace Vehicle Dynamics*, 2e, §3.3.

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture the rocket as a weather-vane arrow: thrust shoots out the tail, gravity hangs a weight from the center, and the air pushes sideways on the vane (normal) while also slowing its forward slide (axial).  
2. **What to overlearn** — \( \vec{F}^b = \vec{T}^b + (-A,N)^\top + C^{b/i}\vec{W}^i \) and the two-axis rotation matrix for a 2-D gravity turn.  
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.  
4. **First-principles fallback** — Re-derive from Newton’s second law by writing the three force vectors in their natural frames and inserting the coordinate transformation.
