## 1. The one-sentence answer
**The true anomaly \(\nu\) is recovered from the eccentric anomaly \(E\) by a half-angle tangent formula that corrects for the displacement of the focus from the ellipse center.**

An ellipse has two foci; the central body sits at one focus. The true anomaly measures the angle at that focus from periapsis to the spacecraft. The eccentric anomaly instead measures an angle at the geometric center of the ellipse, using an auxiliary circle whose radius equals the semi-major axis. Because these two reference points differ, the angles are not identical; a trigonometric mapping is required.

The mapping arises directly from equating the polar radius expressed in both anomalies and solving the resulting identity. Once the half-angle form is obtained, quadrant-correct evaluation yields the physical true anomaly between \(0\) and \(2\pi\).

> [!NOTE]
> The square-root factor \(\sqrt{(1+e)/(1-e)}\) stretches the auxiliary-circle angle exactly enough to move the measurement point from the center to the occupied focus; forgetting the factor is the single most common source of numerical error in preliminary orbit calculations.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
SpaceX’s Starlink constellation planning software converts eccentric anomalies generated by its mean-anomaly propagators into true anomalies before computing line-of-sight vectors to ground stations; the conversion appears in every daily contact-plan update.

ESA’s Juice mission trajectory team uses the identical relation inside its flight-dynamics software to translate numerical solutions of Kepler’s equation (which naturally yields \(E\)) into true anomalies required for Jupiter gravity-assist targeting.

NASA’s General Mission Analysis Tool (GMAT) implements the half-angle formula in its “TrueAnomaly” report field so that attitude-control engineers can command thruster firings at precise true-anomaly milestones during lunar transfer.

Atmospheric drag models for low-Earth-orbit debris, such as those run by LeoLabs, require true anomaly to locate the perigee-relative position where density peaks; eccentric anomaly alone produces range errors of several kilometres.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Locate the two angles on the same ellipse
The true anomaly is measured at the focus; the eccentric anomaly is measured at the centre. Draw the auxiliary circle of radius \(a\) centred at the ellipse centre. Project the spacecraft position perpendicular to the major axis onto this circle; the angle from periapsis to that projection is \(E\).

### Step 2 — Write the Cartesian coordinates of the spacecraft
In the auxiliary circle the abscissa is \(a\cos E\). Because the ellipse is compressed by \(\sqrt{1-e^2}\) along the minor axis, the actual coordinates relative to the centre become
\[
x = a(\cos E - e), \quad y = a\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin E.
\]
The offset \(-ae\) appears because the focus lies a distance \(ae\) from the centre.

### Step 3 — Express the same point in polar coordinates centred at the focus
Shift the origin to the focus. The radius vector from the focus satisfies
\[
r = a(1-e\cos E).
\]
The angle of this vector from periapsis is the true anomaly \(\nu\), so
\[
x = r\cos\nu - ae, \quad y = r\sin\nu.
\]

### Step 4 — Equate the two coordinate expressions
Equating the \(x\) expressions yields
\[
a(\cos E - e) = r\cos\nu - ae.
\]
Substitute \(r = a(1-e\cos E)\) and simplify to obtain the cosine relation
\[
\cos\nu = \frac{\cos E - e}{1 - e\cos E}.
\]

### Step 5 — Repeat for the sine component and divide
The \(y\) expressions give
\[
\sin\nu = \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin E}{1 - e\cos E}.
\]
Dividing the sine by the cosine produces
\[
\tan\nu = \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin E}{\cos E - e}.
\]

### Step 6 — Convert to half-angle form
Apply the Weierstrass substitution \(\tan(\nu/2) = t\) and the corresponding identities for \(\sin E\) and \(\cos E\). Algebraic rearrangement collapses the expression to the compact textbook formula
\[
\tan\frac{\nu}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\frac{E}{2}.
\]
This is the required mapping; the square-root prefactor encodes the focus offset.

> [!WARNING]
> Using the two-argument arctangent of the sine and cosine pair is mandatory; the single-argument \(\arctan\) of the half-angle expression alone loses quadrant information and yields errors of \(\pi\) radians.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Circular limit check**  
*Given:* \(e=0\), \(E=\pi/2\).  
*Find:* \(\nu\).  
Because \(e=0\) the auxiliary circle and focus coincide, so \(\nu=E\).  
Substitute into the formula:
\[
\tan\frac{\nu}{2}=\sqrt{1}\tan\frac{\pi}{4}=1 \implies \frac{\nu}{2}=\pi/4 \implies \nu=\pi/2.
\]
**Final answer**  
\(\nu=\pi/2\)  

*Reflection:* The factor collapses to unity, confirming consistency with the circle.

**Example 2 — Periapsis verification**  
*Given:* \(E=0\), arbitrary \(e<1\).  
*Find:* \(\nu\).  
\[
\tan\frac{\nu}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan 0=0 \implies \nu=0.
\]
**Final answer**  
\(\nu=0\)  

*Reflection:* Both anomalies are zero at periapsis by definition.

**Example 3 — Moderate eccentricity**  
*Given:* \(e=0.5\), \(E=60^\circ= \pi/3\).  
*Find:* \(\nu\).  
\[
\tan\frac{E}{2}=\tan 30^\circ=1/\sqrt{3}.
\]
\[
\tan\frac{\nu}{2}=\sqrt{3}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}=1 \implies \frac{\nu}{2}=45^\circ \implies \nu=90^\circ.
\]
**Final answer**  
\(\nu=90^\circ\)  

*Reflection:* The mapping stretches the angle exactly to quadrature at this particular eccentricity.

**Example 4 — Quadrant III case**  
*Given:* \(e=0.3\), \(E=240^\circ\).  
*Find:* \(\nu\).  
Half-angle: \(E/2=120^\circ\), \(\tan 120^\circ=-\sqrt{3}\).  
\[
\tan\frac{\nu}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1.3}{0.7}}(-\sqrt{3})\approx1.357(-\sqrt{3})\approx-2.351.
\]
Two-argument arctangent of the corresponding sine and cosine pair returns \(\nu\approx 256.4^\circ\).  
**Final answer**  
\(\nu\approx 256.4^\circ\)  

*Reflection:* The negative tangent correctly signals the third quadrant only when quadrant logic is applied after the half-angle step.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                                      | How to avoid it                                      |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Using \(\nu=\arccos((\cos E-e)/(1-e\cos E))\) alone | Arccos returns only [0,π]; loses quadrant II/III    | Always compute both sin and cos, then apply atan2    |
| Forgetting the \(\sqrt{(1+e)/(1-e)}\) factor | Treating the auxiliary circle as if the focus were centred | Derive the factor once from the coordinate shift     |
| Evaluating at \(E=\pi\) with floating-point e | Denominator 1-e cos E becomes zero or negative      | Add a small epsilon guard or use the half-angle form |
| Confusing mean anomaly M with E   | Both are measured from centre; names are similar    | Keep M for Kepler iteration; E is the geometric angle|
| Degree/radian mismatch in code    | Language libraries default to radians               | Convert explicitly before calling trig functions     |
| Negative eccentricity input       | Typo or imported hyperbolic data                    | Assert 0 ≤ e < 1 before conversion                   |
| Using single-precision tan(E/2) near E=π | tan diverges at 180°                                | Switch to the sin/cos pair form near that point      |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let \(0\leq e<1\) and let \(E\) be any real number. The true anomaly \(\nu\) corresponding to the eccentric anomaly \(E\) on an ellipse of eccentricity \(e\) is the unique angle satisfying
\[
\cos\nu=\frac{\cos E-e}{1-e\cos E},\qquad\sin\nu=\frac{\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin E}{1-e\cos E},
\]
with \(\nu\) chosen in \([0,2\pi)\) so that both identities hold simultaneously. Equivalently,
\[
\tan\frac{\nu}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\frac{E}{2}
\]
provided the two-argument arctangent is used to restore the correct quadrant. (See Curtis, *Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students*, 4e, §3.7, Eq. 3.29–3.30.)

## 9. The memory technique

1. **The hook** — Picture the auxiliary circle as a rubber band stretched sideways until the focus sits at the right place; the stretch factor is exactly \(\sqrt{(1+e)/(1-e)}\).
2. **What to overlearn** — The half-angle formula and the two-argument arctangent rule.
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.
4. **First-principles fallback** — Return to the coordinate definitions \(x=a(\cos E-e)\), \(y=a\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin E\) and re-derive the tangent ratio.
