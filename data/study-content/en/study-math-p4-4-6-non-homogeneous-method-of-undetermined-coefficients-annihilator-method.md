## 1. The one-sentence answer
**The annihilator method determines a particular solution \(y_p\) to a constant-coefficient non-homogeneous linear ODE \(L(y)=g(x)\) by constructing a differential operator \(A\) that kills \(g\), solving the auxiliary homogeneous equation \(AL(y)=0\), and isolating the new terms that arise.**

The forcing function \(g(x)\) belongs to a finite-dimensional vector space closed under differentiation. Any such function is annihilated by a constant-coefficient operator whose characteristic polynomial has roots precisely at the frequencies or growth rates present in \(g\). Applying that operator to both sides of the original equation produces a higher-order homogeneous equation whose general solution contains every solution of the original homogeneous problem plus additional independent solutions. Those additional solutions supply the exact functional form needed for \(y_p\); the coefficients are then fixed by substitution.

Because the method works entirely inside the algebra of differential operators, it converts an inhomogeneous problem into a purely algebraic one of root extraction and linear algebra, without ever requiring variation of parameters or direct integration against a Green’s function.

> [!NOTE]
> The annihilator enlarges the solution space in a controlled way; the particular solution is exactly the projection of the enlarged kernel onto the new root factors.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
In the design of active suspension controllers for Tesla Model S vehicles, the road-surface input is modeled as a sum of sinusoids; the annihilator method yields the exact steady-state deflection of the quarter-car model, allowing engineers to tune damping ratios without numerical simulation at every frequency.

NASA’s Europa Clipper mission uses constant-coefficient models of the spacecraft’s attitude dynamics driven by periodic thruster firings; the annihilator technique supplies closed-form particular solutions that are inserted into the Kalman filter covariance propagation, reducing on-board computational load.

In analog filter design at Texas Instruments, the voltage across a loaded RLC circuit with a polynomial-times-exponential input is solved via annihilators; the resulting expressions become the transfer-function numerator coefficients that are hard-coded into the filter’s DSP firmware.

Quantum-control pulses for superconducting qubits at Rigetti Computing are approximated by low-degree polynomials; the annihilator method gives the exact integrated Bloch-vector trajectory, enabling analytic fidelity estimates that guide pulse-shaping algorithms.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Write the equation in operator form
Any constant-coefficient linear ODE can be written \(L(D)y=g(x)\), where \(D=d/dx\) and \(L\) is a polynomial in \(D\).  
Example: \(y''-3y'+2y=e^{3x}\) becomes \((D^2-3D+2)y=e^{3x}\).  
Formally, \(L(D)=\sum_{k=0}^n a_k D^k\).  
> [!WARNING] Treating the operator as a mere symbol without remembering it acts on functions leads to incorrect multiplicity counts later.

### Step 2 — Identify an annihilator for the forcing term
A minimal annihilator \(A(D)\) satisfies \(A(D)g=0\) and has the lowest possible order.  
For \(g(x)=e^{rx}p(x)\cos(\omega x)\) or \(\sin(\omega x)\), the annihilator is \((D-r)^m[(D-r)^2+\omega^2]^k\) where \(m\) and \(k\) match the polynomial degree and trigonometric power.  
Formally, \(A(D)g\equiv0\).

### Step 3 — Apply the annihilator to both sides
\(A(D)L(D)y=A(D)g=0\), so \(y\) satisfies the homogeneous equation \((AL)(D)y=0\).  
The characteristic polynomial of \(AL\) is the product of the factors from \(L\) and from \(A\).

### Step 4 — Solve the enlarged homogeneous equation
Factor the new characteristic polynomial and write the general solution as the sum of the original homogeneous solution \(y_h\) plus new terms \(y_\text{new}\) generated by the roots of \(A\).

### Step 5 — Extract the candidate particular solution
Delete from \(y_\text{new}\) any functions already present in \(y_h\); the remainder is the undetermined-coefficient form for \(y_p\).  
Formally, \(y_p\) is a linear combination of the basis functions belonging to the kernel of \(A\) but not to the kernel of \(L\).

### Step 6 — Substitute and equate coefficients
Insert \(y_p\) into the original equation, collect like terms, and solve the resulting linear system for the undetermined coefficients.  
The final particular solution is therefore obtained by purely algebraic operations.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Pure exponential**  
*Given:* \(y''-3y'+2y=e^{3x}\).  
*Find:* a particular solution.  
Apply \(L(D)=D^2-3D+2\). The annihilator of \(e^{3x}\) is \(A(D)=D-3\).  
Then \((D-3)(D^2-3D+2)y=0\) yields characteristic equation \((r-3)(r-1)(r-2)=0\).  
New root \(r=3\) produces term \(c\,e^{3x}\).  
Because \(e^{3x}\) is absent from \(y_h=c_1e^x+c_2e^{2x}\), set \(y_p=Ae^{3x}\).  
Differentiate twice and substitute:  
\((9A-9A+2A)e^{3x}=e^{3x}\) \(\implies 2A=1\) \(\implies A=\frac12\).  
**\(y_p=\frac12 e^{3x}\)**  
*Reflection:* The method recovers the classic undetermined-coefficient guess automatically.

**Example 2 — Polynomial of degree 2**  
*Given:* \(y''+y=x^2\).  
*Find:* \(y_p\).  
Annihilator \(A(D)=D^3\).  
Enlarged characteristic equation \(r^2+1=r^3(r^2+1)=0\) adds roots \(0\) (multiplicity 3).  
New terms: \(A+Bx+Cx^2\).  
\(y_h\) contains none of these, so \(y_p=A+Bx+Cx^2\).  
Substitute: \(2C+(A+Bx+Cx^2)=x^2\) \(\implies C=1\), \(B=0\), \(A=0\).  
**\(y_p=x^2-2\)**  
*Reflection:* The constant term appears because differentiation lowers degree.

**Example 3 — Resonance with sine**  
*Given:* \(y''+4y=\sin(2x)\).  
*Find:* \(y_p\).  
Annihilator \(A(D)=(D^2+4)\).  
Enlarged operator has double factor \((D^2+4)\), producing new terms \(x(A\cos2x+B\sin2x)\).  
Substitute and equate coefficients yields \(A=0\), \(B=-\frac18\).  
**\(y_p=-\frac18 x\sin2x\)**  
*Reflection:* The extra factor of \(x\) is forced by the resonance already present in \(y_h\).

**Example 4 — Product forcing**  
*Given:* \(y''-y'=x e^x\).  
*Find:* \(y_p\).  
Annihilator of \(x e^x\) is \((D-1)^2\).  
Enlarged roots add multiplicity at \(r=1\).  
\(y_h\) already contains \(e^x\), so candidate becomes \(y_p=x(A+Bx)e^x\).  
After substitution and simplification the system gives \(A=-1\), \(B=\frac12\).  
**\(y_p=x(\frac12 x-1)e^x\)**  
*Reflection:* Multiplicity counting must compare the annihilator order with the existing homogeneous multiplicity.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                                      | How to avoid it                                      |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Forgetting to raise multiplicity when resonance occurs | Operator multiplication adds exponents directly     | Compare the annihilator factor with the factor already in \(L\) before writing \(y_p\). |
| Using a non-minimal annihilator   | Over-annihilating introduces superfluous constants  | Choose the lowest-order operator whose kernel contains \(g\). |
| Treating \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) separately | Their annihilator is irreducible quadratic          | Always use \((D^2+\omega^2)\) as a single block.     |
| Omitting the polynomial degree in the annihilator | Polynomial of degree \(m\) needs multiplicity \(m+1\) | Count the highest power of \(x\) present in \(g\).   |
| Substituting \(y_p\) into the enlarged equation instead of the original | The enlarged equation is satisfied by many extra functions | Always return to \(L(y_p)=g\).                       |
| Sign error when moving terms across the operator | Differential operators do not commute with variable coefficients | Keep all coefficients constant; verify by direct differentiation. |
| Assuming the method works for variable-coefficient \(L\) | Annihilators rely on constant coefficients          | Verify that every coefficient in \(L\) is constant before starting. |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let \(L(D)\) be a monic constant-coefficient differential operator of order \(n\) and let \(g\) be a smooth function annihilated by a constant-coefficient operator \(A(D)\) of order \(m\). If \(A\) and \(L\) share no common factors, then a particular solution of \(L(D)y=g\) exists of the form
\[
y_p=\sum c_k\phi_k(x),
\]
where \(\{\phi_k\}\) is any basis for \(\ker A\setminus\ker L\). When common factors occur, each shared root of multiplicity \(\mu\) in \(L\) and \(\nu\) in \(A\) contributes an extra factor of \(x^\mu\) to the corresponding basis functions. (Boyce & DiPrima, *Elementary Differential Equations*, 11e, §4.5.)

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture an “annihilator ray gun” that vaporizes the forcing function; the debris left in the enlarged kernel is exactly the shape you need for \(y_p\).
2. **What to overlearn** — The annihilator for \(x^k e^{rx}\cos\omega x\) is \((D-r)^{k+1}[(D-r)^2+\omega^2]\); multiplicity arithmetic when factors overlap.
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.
4. **First-principles fallback** — Re-derive the characteristic polynomial of \(AL\) from scratch and count the difference in root multiplicities.

## 10. What this unlocks
Mastery of the annihilator method supplies the fastest route to closed-form particular solutions for any constant-coefficient linear system driven by the classical forcing families; it therefore becomes the default first attack in control theory, circuit analysis, and structural dynamics before numerical or Laplace-transform methods are invoked.

- Laplace-transform methods for discontinuous or impulsive forcing
- Variation of parameters for variable-coefficient or non-annihilable \(g\)
- Green’s-function constructions that generalize the same kernel-difference idea
- State-space realization of forced linear systems
