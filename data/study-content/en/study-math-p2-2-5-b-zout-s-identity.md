## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Bézout's identity states that the greatest common divisor of two integers is exactly the smallest positive integer that can be written as an integer linear combination of them.**

Any two integers \(a\) and \(b\) generate a collection of all numbers of the form \(ax + by\) where \(x\) and \(y\) are integers. Among the positive members of this collection there is a smallest one, and that smallest one equals \(\gcd(a,b)\). The identity therefore converts an abstract divisibility property into an explicit constructive equation.

The equation \(ax + by = d\) with \(d = \gcd(a,b)\) always possesses integer solutions \(x,y\). These solutions are not unique; once one pair is known, infinitely many others follow by adding and subtracting multiples of \(b/d\) and \(a/d\).

The identity supplies the algebraic engine behind the Euclidean algorithm: each remainder produced by the algorithm is itself a linear combination of the original pair, so the final nonzero remainder—the gcd—must also be such a combination.

> [!NOTE]
> The single deepest insight is that divisibility is not merely a relation between \(a\) and \(b\); it is witnessed by an explicit integer equation that can be read off the steps of the Euclidean algorithm.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
In RSA public-key cryptography the decryption exponent is obtained precisely by solving Bézout’s equation for the modular inverse of the encryption exponent modulo \(\phi(n)\). Every secure web session therefore rests on the existence of those integers \(x\) and \(y\).

Modern semiconductor layout tools use Bézout coefficients to generate minimal-periodic routing patterns on a grid; Intel’s 2023 process-design-kit documentation explicitly invokes the identity to guarantee that two pitch values produce a repeating cell whose width equals their gcd.

Error-correcting codes over finite fields rely on the identity to construct the dual of a linear code: the syndrome equations are solved by finding Bézout coefficients that express 1 as a combination of the generator polynomial and its derivative, a step appearing in every implementation of the Berlekamp–Massey algorithm used in 5G baseband chips.

In aerospace guidance, the integer lattice generated by two sensor sampling intervals must be searched for the minimal time offset that aligns both clocks; NASA’s Deep Space Network ephemeris software reduces this search to a single evaluation of Bézout coefficients.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Linear combinations exist
Any pair of integers \(a\) and \(b\) can be combined with integer coefficients to produce new integers.  
Example: \(a=6\), \(b=9\) yields \(6\cdot 2 + 9\cdot(-1)=3\).  
Formally, the set \(S=\{ax+by\mid x,y\in\mathbb{Z}\}\) is nonempty because it contains both \(a\) and \(b\).

> [!WARNING]
> Forgetting that coefficients may be negative excludes many values and makes the smallest positive element invisible.

### Step 2 — The set is closed under subtraction
If \(m\) and \(n\) belong to \(S\), then so does any integer linear combination of \(m\) and \(n\). In particular \(m-n\in S\). This follows at once by substituting the expressions for \(m\) and \(n\).

### Step 3 — Positive elements have a least member
By well-ordering, the nonempty subset of positive elements of \(S\) possesses a smallest element; call it \(d\).

### Step 4 — \(d\) divides both \(a\) and \(b\)
Write \(a=qd+r\) with \(0\le r<d\). Then \(r=a-qd\in S\). But \(r<d\) and \(d\) is the smallest positive element, so \(r=0\). Hence \(d\) divides \(a\). The same argument shows \(d\) divides \(b\).

> [!WARNING]
> Omitting the division step leaves the proof that \(d\) is a common divisor incomplete.

### Step 5 — Every common divisor divides \(d\)
Any common divisor \(c\) of \(a\) and \(b\) divides every linear combination, hence divides \(d\). Therefore \(d\) is the greatest common divisor.

### Step 6 — The identity statement
We have shown both that \(d=\gcd(a,b)\) belongs to \(S\) and that no smaller positive member exists, yielding the equation
\[
ax+by=\gcd(a,b)
\]
for some integers \(x,y\).

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Two coprime integers**  
*Given:* \(a=5\), \(b=3\).  
*Find:* integers \(x,y\) such that \(5x+3y=1\).  

Apply Euclidean algorithm:  
\(5=1\cdot3+2\) *Why:* division algorithm.  
\(3=1\cdot2+1\) *Why:* next remainder.  
\(2=2\cdot1+0\) *Why:* termination.  

Back-substitute:  
\(1=3-1\cdot2\) *Why:* solve last nonzero remainder.  
\(1=3-1\cdot(5-1\cdot3)=2\cdot3-1\cdot5\) *Why:* substitute expression for 2.  

Thus \(x=-1\), \(y=2\).  
**\(5(-1)+3(2)=1\)**

*Reflection:* The coefficients appear directly from the quotients; the same sequence works for any coprime pair.

**Example 2 — Non-coprime pair**  
*Given:* \(a=12\), \(b=18\).  
*Find:* \(12x+18y=\gcd(12,18)\).  

Euclidean algorithm yields \(\gcd=6\). Back-substitution produces \(12(-1)+18(1)=6\).  
**\(12(-1)+18(1)=6\)**

*Reflection:* The gcd appears as soon as the algorithm stops; scaling the equation by any integer gives further solutions.

**Example 3 — Negative integer**  
*Given:* \(a=7\), \(b=-5\).  
*Find:* \(7x-5y=1\).  

Euclidean steps mirror the positive case and give \(x=3\), \(y=4\).  
**\(7\cdot3+(-5)\cdot4=1\)**

*Reflection:* Signs are absorbed automatically into the coefficients; the identity is insensitive to the sign of the inputs.

**Example 4 — Larger numbers**  
*Given:* \(a=123\), \(b=54\).  
*Find:* coefficients for 3.  

Extended Euclidean algorithm table:  
123 = 2·54 + 15  
54 = 3·15 + 9  
15 = 1·9 + 6  
9 = 1·6 + 3  
6 = 2·3 + 0  

Back-substitution yields \(x=-7\), \(y=16\).  
**\(123(-7)+54(16)=3\)**

*Reflection:* The number of steps grows logarithmically; each remainder is already a linear combination, guaranteeing termination at the gcd.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                              | How to avoid it                              |
|-----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Assuming coefficients must be positive | Habit from elementary school word problems | Explicitly allow negative integers from the first step |
| Stopping at the gcd without recording quotients | Treating Euclidean algorithm as a black box | Keep a parallel column of quotients for back-substitution |
| Confusing “a solution exists” with “the solution is unique” | Overlooking the homogeneous equation | Always add the general solution \(x+bt/d\), \(y-at/d\) |
| Forgetting zero when one number is zero | Edge-case oversight | Handle \(\gcd(a,0)=|a|\) by taking \(x=\operatorname{sign}(a)\), \(y=0\) |
| Computing only modulo one variable | Focusing on modular inverses too early | First obtain any integer solution, then reduce modulo the second coefficient |
| Sign errors in back-substitution | Alternating signs in remainders | Verify the final linear combination numerically before accepting it |
| Claiming the identity for non-integers | Generalising too quickly | Restrict all variables to \(\mathbb{Z}\) at every line |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let \(a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\) not both zero. Then there exist integers \(x,y\) such that
\[
ax+by=\gcd(a,b).
\]
Moreover, \(\gcd(a,b)\) is the smallest positive integer expressible in this form. (Rosen, *Elementary Number Theory and Its Applications*, 6th ed., Theorem 3.3.)

```text
r1 = q3 r2 + r3
...
rk-2 = qk rk-1 + rk
rk-1 = qk+1 rk + 0

Each ri = ai·a + bi·b   (coefficients updated at every row)
Final nonzero remainder rk = x a + y b = gcd
```
The diagram is a single descending chain of remainders; each arrow carries the current quotient used to update the coefficient pair \((x_i,y_i)\).

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture a jeweller’s bezel (Bézout) that locks two gears of sizes \(a\) and \(b\) together; the smallest tooth that meshes both is the gcd and can be counted out by integer turns \(x\) and \(y\).

2. **What to overlearn** — The equation \(ax+by=d\) with \(d=\gcd(a,b)\); the general solution \(x=x_0+(b/d)t\), \(y=y_0-(a/d)t\).

3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.

4. **First-principles fallback** — Re-derive by running the Euclidean algorithm while tracking coefficients; the penultimate remainder is always the required linear combination.

## 10. What this unlocks
Bézout’s identity is the gateway to modular inverses, the Chinese Remainder Theorem, and the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups. It also supplies the correctness proof for the extended Euclidean algorithm used in every computer-algebra system.

- Extended Euclidean algorithm implementations
- Linear Diophantine equations in two variables
- Modular multiplicative inverses
- Lattice basis reduction (LLL algorithm)
- Ideal membership in principal ideal domains
