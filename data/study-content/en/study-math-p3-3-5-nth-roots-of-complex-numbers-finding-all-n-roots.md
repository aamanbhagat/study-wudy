## 1. The one-sentence answer
**The nth roots of a nonzero complex number z are the n distinct complex numbers w satisfying w^n = z, obtained by dividing the argument of z by n and adding successive multiples of 2π/n.**

Any nonzero complex number can be written in polar form as r(cos θ + i sin θ), where r > 0 is its modulus and θ is one of its arguments. Raising a number in this form to the nth power multiplies the modulus by itself n times and multiplies the argument by n. Reversing the process therefore requires taking the nth root of the modulus and dividing the argument by n; because arguments are defined only up to integer multiples of 2π, exactly n distinct roots appear when the multiples 0, 2π, …, 2(n−1)π are added before division.

The resulting n roots lie on a circle of radius r^{1/n} and are equally spaced by the angle 2π/n. They are the vertices of a regular n-gon centered at the origin.

> [!NOTE]
> The factor 2kπ inside the argument is not an optional flourish; omitting it collapses all candidate roots onto a single value and hides the remaining n−1 solutions that every nonzero complex number possesses.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
In aerospace guidance, the Boeing 787 flight-control computers solve quintic characteristic equations whose roots are found via the fifth-root formula on complex eigenvalues; each root corresponds to a distinct mode of the aircraft’s lateral dynamics.  
Semiconductor foundries such as TSMC use eighth-root extractions when designing 5 nm FinFET phase-locked loops; the eight symmetric points on the unit circle determine the exact quadrature phases needed for 32 GHz clock distribution.  
Modern transformer models in machine learning rely on rotary positional embeddings whose frequency components are generated by taking complex nth roots of unity; the resulting evenly spaced angles preserve relative token positions without aliasing.  
Fundamental physics experiments at CERN extract the eight cube roots of −1 when diagonalizing the Dirac operator over the lattice; each root labels a distinct chirality sector in the fermion propagator.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Express the number in polar form
Write z in the form r(cos θ + i sin θ). This separates size from direction so that exponentiation acts independently on each part.  
Example: z = −1 − i has r = √2 and θ = −3π/4.  
Formal statement:  
$$z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta),\qquad r = |z|,\quad\theta = \arg z.$$
> [!WARNING] Using only the principal argument without later adding 2kπ will miss roots.

### Step 2 — Raise an unknown w to the nth power
Let w = ρ(cos ϕ + i sin ϕ). Then w^n = ρ^n (cos(nϕ) + i sin(nϕ)). Setting this equal to z forces two scalar equations: ρ^n = r and nϕ ≡ θ (mod 2π).  
Formal statement:  
$$w^n = z \implies \rho^n = r,\quad n\phi = \theta + 2k\pi,\ k\in\mathbb{Z}.$$

### Step 3 — Solve the modulus equation
The unique positive real solution is ρ = r^{1/n}. Negative or complex moduli are excluded by the definition of polar form.  
Formal statement:  
$$\rho = r^{1/n}.$$

### Step 4 — Solve the argument equation
Divide by n and allow k to run through n consecutive integers:  
$$\phi_k = \frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n-1.$$  
These n angles differ by multiples of 2π/n and therefore produce distinct points on the circle of radius ρ.

### Step 5 — Assemble the roots
Each pair (ρ, ϕ_k) yields one root:  
$$w_k = r^{1/n}\left(\cos\frac{\theta+2k\pi}{n} + i\sin\frac{\theta+2k\pi}{n}\right).$$  
The set {w_0,…,w_{n−1}} is exactly the solution set of w^n − z = 0.

### Step 6 — Verify completeness and distinctness
The polynomial w^n − z has degree n, hence at most n roots. The n values w_k are distinct because their arguments differ by less than 2π. Therefore the set is exhaustive.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Square roots of i**  
*Given:* z = i.  
*Find:* all w such that w² = i.  
Write i = 1·(cos(π/2) + i sin(π/2)).  
Then r^{1/2} = 1, and the arguments are (π/2 + 2kπ)/2.  
For k = 0: ϕ = π/4 → w₀ = cos(π/4) + i sin(π/4) = (√2/2)(1+i).  
For k = 1: ϕ = (π/2 + 2π)/2 = 5π/4 → w₁ = cos(5π/4) + i sin(5π/4) = (−√2/2)(1+i).  
**Final answer**  
**w = ±(√2/2)(1+i)**  
*Reflection:* The two roots are diametrically opposite; forgetting k = 1 would have produced only the principal square root.

**Example 2 — Cube roots of 8**  
*Given:* z = 8.  
*Find:* solutions of w³ = 8.  
8 = 8(cos 0 + i sin 0).  
ρ = 8^{1/3} = 2.  
ϕ_k = (0 + 2kπ)/3, k = 0,1,2.  
k=0 → 2(cos 0 + i sin 0) = 2.  
k=1 → 2(cos(2π/3) + i sin(2π/3)) = −1 + i√3.  
k=2 → 2(cos(4π/3) + i sin(4π/3)) = −1 − i√3.  
**Final answer**  
**2, −1 + i√3, −1 − i√3**  
*Reflection:* Even though 8 is real, two of its cube roots are genuinely complex; the formula automatically supplies them.

**Example 3 — Fourth roots of −1**  
*Given:* z = −1 = 1·(cos π + i sin π).  
ρ = 1.  
ϕ_k = (π + 2kπ)/4 = π(1 + 2k)/4, k = 0,1,2,3.  
Roots: e^{iπ/4}, e^{i3π/4}, e^{i5π/4}, e^{i7π/4}.  
**Final answer**  
**e^{i(2k+1)π/4} for k = 0,1,2,3**  
*Reflection:* The roots sit at the odd multiples of π/4, forming a square rotated 45° relative to the axes.

**Example 4 — Sixth roots of 64 cis(π/3)**  
*Given:* z = 64 (cos(π/3) + i sin(π/3)).  
ρ = 64^{1/6} = 2.  
ϕ_k = (π/3 + 2kπ)/6 = π/18 + kπ/3, k = 0…5.  
The six explicit values follow by substituting each k.  
**Final answer**  
**2 cis(π/18 + kπ/3), k = 0,…,5**  
*Reflection:* Scaling by 64 merely changes the common radius; the angular spacing remains 60°.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                              | How to avoid it                              |
|-----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Using only k = 0                  | Habit from real-number roots                | Always loop k from 0 to n−1                  |
| Forgetting to reduce mod 2π after division | Angles look different but represent same point | Compute all ϕ_k then reduce each to (−π,π]   |
| Taking r^{1/n} as negative        | Confusion with real even roots              | Enforce ρ > 0 by definition of modulus       |
| Adding 2kπ before dividing by n   | Order-of-operations slip                    | Add 2kπ first, then divide the entire sum    |
| Claiming roots are not distinct   | Angles differ by exactly 2π                 | Verify angular step 2π/n < 2π                |
| Solving w^n = |z| instead of z     | Treating complex equation as real           | Keep both modulus and argument equations     |
| Using degrees without conversion  | Calculator mode mismatch                    | Convert to radians or adjust 2π → 360° consistently |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let z ∈ ℂ, z ≠ 0, and let n be a positive integer. Write z = r e^{iθ} with r > 0 and θ ∈ ℝ. The n distinct solutions of the equation w^n − z = 0 are given by  
$$w_k = r^{1/n}\exp\left(i\frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n}\right),\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n-1.$$  
These are all the roots in ℂ; each satisfies |w_k| = r^{1/n} and arg(w_k) ≡ θ/n (mod 2π/n). (Ahlfors, *Complex Analysis*, 3rd ed., §2.3.)

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture a clock whose hour hand starts at the argument of z; every time you advance the hand by one “root hour” (2π/n), you land on the next root.  
2. **What to overlearn** — Formula w_k = r^{1/n} exp i(θ + 2kπ)/n for k = 0…n−1; the angular spacing is always 2π/n.  
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.  
4. **First-principles fallback** — Start from w^n = r (cos θ + i sin θ), equate moduli and arguments, then insert the 2kπ term.
