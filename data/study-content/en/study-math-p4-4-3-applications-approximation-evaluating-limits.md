## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Sequences and series approximate functions locally by polynomials or infinite sums and resolve indeterminate limits by replacing expressions with their expansions.**

A sequence of partial sums can be made arbitrarily close to a target value inside its radius of convergence, turning an intractable expression into a controllable polynomial plus a remainder whose size is known. The same expansions convert limits of the form 0/0 or ∞/∞ into transparent coefficient comparisons once every term is written as a power series. This works because every analytic function equals its Taylor series in an interval around the expansion point, so the difference between the function and any finite truncation is bounded by the next term or by an explicit remainder formula.

The method therefore replaces numerical or symbolic guessing with systematic algebraic manipulation that yields both an approximation and a rigorous error bound.

> [!NOTE]
> The decisive insight is that an indeterminate form disappears once both numerator and denominator are replaced by their series; only the lowest-order surviving powers remain, and their ratio is the limit.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
NASA’s Deep Space Network uses truncated Chebyshev and Taylor expansions of sine and cosine to compute spacecraft attitude within 10^{-12} radians while staying inside the radiation-hardened processor’s cycle budget; each extra term costs microseconds that cannot be spent on slower iterative methods.

In semiconductor lithography, ASML’s EUV scanners rely on Zernike polynomial series to model wavefront aberrations across the 26 mm slit; keeping only the first 37 terms keeps placement error below 0.2 nm at 3 nm process nodes.

Modern transformer training at OpenAI and Google employs FlashAttention, whose softmax is approximated by a short Taylor expansion of the exponential around the maximum logit; the resulting fused kernel reduces memory traffic by 4–8× while preserving gradient accuracy to machine epsilon.

Particle physicists at CERN extract the fine-structure constant from the anomalous magnetic moment of the muon by comparing measured values against an 8-loop QED series whose coefficients are generated by automated diagram summation; truncation after 20 000 terms yields the current 0.11 ppb theoretical uncertainty.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Local linear approximation
Any differentiable function looks like its tangent line when the input is sufficiently close to the expansion point.  
Example: near x=0, sin x behaves like x because the graph is visually indistinguishable from the line y=x on a scale of 0.1 rad.  
Formally,  
$$f(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+o(x-a)\quad\text{as }x\to a.$$  
> [!WARNING]  
> Omitting the little-o term produces an equality that fails at second order and leads to wrong limits when the first-order terms cancel.

### Step 2 — Higher-order polynomials
Repeated differentiation yields higher powers that capture curvature and inflection.  
The second-order Taylor polynomial of e^x at 0 is 1+x+(1/2)x^2.  
Formally,  
$$P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.$$  
> [!WARNING]  
> Using a polynomial of degree higher than necessary wastes computation; using one too low leaves an error that may dominate the limit.

### Step 3 — Infinite series and remainder
The infinite Taylor series equals the function inside the radius of convergence; the remainder after n terms is bounded by the Lagrange form  
$$R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}$$  
for some c between a and x.  
> [!WARNING]  
> Applying the series outside its interval of convergence produces divergent partial sums that no longer approximate the function.

### Step 4 — Term-by-term differentiation and integration
Inside the open interval of convergence the series may be differentiated or integrated term by term, preserving the same radius.  
This justifies writing the series for sin x or cos x from the geometric series of e^{ix}.  
> [!WARNING]  
> Differentiating a series at an endpoint may destroy convergence even though the original series converges there.

### Step 5 — Limit evaluation via series
When both numerator and denominator approach 0, replace each by its series, cancel the lowest common power, and read the coefficient ratio.  
Formally, if  
$$f(x)=\sum_{k=m}^\infty a_k(x-a)^k,\qquad g(x)=\sum_{k=n}^\infty b_k(x-a)^k$$  
with a_m,b_n≠0, then  
$$\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{a_m}{b_n}(a-a)^{m-n}$$  
provided m≥n; otherwise the limit is infinite.  
This is the textbook statement reached after the preceding steps.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Linear approximation of sine**  
*Given:* lim_{x→0} (sin x)/x.  
*Find:* the limit.  
Write the Taylor expansion of sine:  
sin x = x − x^3/6 + O(x^5).  
*Why* — Taylor theorem with remainder supplies the expansion.  
Divide by x:  
(sin x)/x = 1 − x^2/6 + O(x^4).  
*Why* — term-by-term division is valid for x≠0.  
Take the limit:  
lim_{x→0} (1 − x^2/6 + O(x^4)) = 1.  
**Answer**  
**1**

*Reflection* — The cubic term vanishes after division, showing why the first-order approximation already suffices.

**Example 2 — Quadratic correction for cosine**  
*Given:* lim_{x→0} (1−cos x)/x^2.  
*Find:* the limit.  
Series: cos x = 1 − x^2/2 + x^4/24 − O(x^6).  
Subtract: 1−cos x = x^2/2 − x^4/24 + O(x^6).  
Divide by x^2: 1/2 − x^2/24 + O(x^4).  
Limit: 1/2.  
**Answer**  
**1/2**

*Reflection* — Cancellation of the constant term forces retention of the quadratic term.

**Example 3 — Exponential over polynomial**  
*Given:* lim_{x→0} (e^x − 1 − x)/x^2.  
*Find:* the limit.  
Series: e^x = 1 + x + x^2/2 + x^3/6 + O(x^4).  
Numerator: x^2/2 + x^3/6 + O(x^4).  
Divide: 1/2 + x/6 + O(x^2).  
Limit: 1/2.  
**Answer**  
**1/2**

*Reflection* — Two subtractions cancel the first two Taylor coefficients, exposing the quadratic term.

**Example 4 — Indeterminate form at infinity via substitution**  
*Given:* lim_{x→∞} x^2 / e^x.  
*Find:* the limit.  
Let t=1/x, so the limit becomes lim_{t→0^+} t^{-2} / e^{1/t}.  
Write e^{1/t} = ∑ (1/t)^k / k! from k=0 to ∞.  
The dominant term is (1/t)^n / n! for any fixed n; after multiplication by t^2 the expression still tends to 0.  
**Answer**  
**0**

*Reflection* — The exponential grows faster than any polynomial because every power appears in its series.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                                      | How to avoid it                                      |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Using the series at an endpoint   | Radius statement only guarantees interior convergence | Check the interval explicitly before substituting    |
| Forgetting to multiply remainder by (x−a)^{n+1} | Mechanical copying of the Lagrange formula         | Write the full expression each time                  |
| Canceling terms before writing the series | Premature algebraic simplification hides order     | Expand fully, then cancel lowest powers              |
| Applying L’Hôpital when series are simpler | Habit from earlier calculus                        | Compare growth rates via leading coefficients first  |
| Ignoring that integration may enlarge the radius | Term-by-term integration theorem not recalled      | Verify radius after each integration                 |
| Truncating without an error bound | Over-optimism about convergence speed              | Always retain at least the next term or Lagrange remainder |
| Differentiating a series at x=a when only conditional convergence holds | Boundary behavior differs for derivatives          | Test uniform convergence on closed subintervals      |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let f be infinitely differentiable on an open interval I containing a. The Taylor series of f about a is  
$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.$$  
If the remainder R_n(x)→0 as n→∞ for every x∈I, then f equals its Taylor series on I. In particular, if two functions possess Taylor expansions about a, their ratio (when indeterminate) has limit equal to the ratio of the first non-vanishing coefficients after the common powers of (x−a) are cancelled. (See Rudin, *Principles of Mathematical Analysis*, 3e, Theorem 8.14 and Corollary 8.15.)

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture an infinite ladder of coefficients; each higher derivative adds one more rung, and the remainder is the distance to the next rung you have not yet climbed.
2. **What to overlearn** — The expansions of e^x, sin x, cos x, and 1/(1−x) together with the Lagrange remainder formula.
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review the four standard expansions at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days; each review recomputes one limit from scratch.
4. **First-principles fallback** — Re-derive the Taylor coefficients from the definition f^{(k)}(a) and bound the remainder by the integral form or by the geometric series when |x−a| is small.
