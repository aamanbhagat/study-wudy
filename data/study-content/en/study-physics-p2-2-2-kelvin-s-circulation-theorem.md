## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Kelvin's circulation theorem states that the circulation around any closed material curve moving with an inviscid fluid under conservative body forces remains constant in time.**

Circulation measures the net “swirl” of velocity along a loop. In an ideal fluid the loop is made of the same fluid particles at every instant, so its shape stretches and rotates exactly as the flow dictates. Because pressure and body forces do no net work around a closed path and viscosity is absent, no mechanism exists to change the total swirl.

The result follows directly from the Euler equation once it is integrated around the moving contour and the material derivative is taken. The theorem therefore converts a local differential statement about momentum into a global integral invariant.

> [!NOTE]
> The theorem is the fluid-mechanical analogue of angular-momentum conservation: once the fluid is ideal, vorticity can be rearranged but never created or destroyed along material curves.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
Aircraft designers at Boeing and Airbus rely on the theorem to guarantee that the circulation (hence lift) generated by a wing remains constant along a streamline tube in the absence of viscosity, allowing rapid estimation of induced drag from wake vortices.

NASA’s Langley Research Center uses Kelvin’s theorem in large-eddy simulations of rocket plumes to track the persistence of tip vortices shed from booster fins; the constancy of circulation supplies an exact integral check on numerical dissipation.

In astrophysical fluid codes that model accretion disks around black holes, the theorem explains why initially weak poloidal magnetic fields are wound into strong toroidal fields without resistive loss, because circulation of the ideal plasma is frozen.

Oceanographers at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution apply the result to mesoscale eddies: the circulation of an eddy is conserved as it migrates across the Gulf Stream, permitting prediction of its lifetime and trajectory from satellite altimetry.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Circulation as net rotation
Circulation simply adds the tangential velocity component around any closed path; a positive value means the flow rotates in the same sense as the path traversal.  
Consider a rigid-body rotation v = ω × r inside a circle of radius R: the integral equals 2πR²ω.  
$$ \Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{v} \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$  
> [!WARNING]  
> If the path is fixed in space rather than material, the theorem does not apply; fluid particles slip across the contour and circulation can change.

### Step 2 — Material curve and its evolution
A material curve consists of the same fluid particles forever; its infinitesimal line elements dl obey the kinematic relation D(dl)/Dt = (dl · ∇)v.  
A small square patch of fluid is deformed into a parallelogram by a shear flow; each side stretches or rotates according to the local velocity gradient.  
$$ \frac{D}{Dt}(d\mathbf{l}) = d\mathbf{l} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{v} $$  
> [!WARNING]  
> Treating dl as constant leads to an incorrect time derivative that misses the stretching term.

### Step 3 — Time derivative of circulation
Differentiate Γ under the integral sign while accounting for the motion of the contour.  
After inserting the evolution law for dl and rearranging, the integrand becomes the material acceleration dotted with dl plus a perfect differential.  
$$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = \oint_C \frac{D\mathbf{v}}{Dt} \cdot d\mathbf{l} + \oint_C \mathbf{v} \cdot \frac{D(d\mathbf{l})}{Dt} $$  
> [!WARNING]  
> Omitting the second integral produces a spurious source term that violates the theorem.

### Step 4 — Substitute Euler’s equation
For an inviscid fluid the acceleration is −(1/ρ)∇p − ∇Φ. Both pressure and conservative body-force terms integrate to zero around any closed loop.  
The remaining integrand vanishes identically, proving DΓ/Dt = 0.  
$$ \frac{D\Gamma}{Dt} = -\oint_C \frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p \cdot d\mathbf{l} - \oint_C \nabla\Phi \cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0 $$  
> [!WARNING]  
> If density is not barotropic (p = p(ρ)), the pressure term need not vanish and the theorem fails.

### Step 5 — Textbook statement via Stokes’ theorem
By Stokes’ theorem, Γ equals the flux of vorticity ω = ∇ × v through any surface bounded by the curve. Constancy of Γ therefore implies that vorticity flux is conserved on material surfaces.  
$$ \frac{D}{Dt}\int_S \boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0 $$  
This is the final, rigorous form of Kelvin’s circulation theorem.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Uniform flow past a fixed circle**  
*Given:* Irrotational flow u = U ê_x around a stationary circular contour.  
*Find:* DΓ/Dt.  
Step 1: v is constant along the circle, so Γ = 0.  
*Why:* Symmetry cancels contributions from opposite elements.  
Step 2: Euler’s equation holds with ∇p balanced by convective acceleration; no viscosity.  
*Why:* The flow is ideal.  
Step 3: The pressure integral around a closed path is zero.  
*Why:* Gradient theorem.  
**Γ remains zero; DΓ/Dt = 0.**  
*Reflection:* Trivial case confirms the theorem reduces to zero when vorticity is absent.

**Example 2 — Rankine vortex**  
*Given:* Solid-body rotation inside radius a, irrotational outside; material circle initially inside.  
*Find:* Circulation after the circle is stretched radially by a straining flow.  
Step 1: Inside, ω = 2Ω, Γ = πa²·2Ω.  
*Why:* Direct integration of v_θ = Ωr.  
Step 2: Material stretching preserves area for incompressible flow, hence preserves Γ.  
*Why:* Incompressibility enforces zero divergence.  
**Final circulation equals initial value.**  
*Reflection:* Demonstrates conservation even when the curve deforms.

**Example 3 — Baroclinic generation (violation case)**  
*Given:* Horizontal density gradient across a material loop.  
*Find:* DΓ/Dt.  
Step 1: ∮ (1/ρ)∇p · dl ≠ 0 when isobars and isopycnals intersect.  
*Why:* The line integral of a non-gradient field survives.  
**DΓ/Dt equals the baroclinic torque integral.**  
*Reflection:* Shows the barotropic restriction explicitly.

**Example 4 — Lift on an airfoil**  
*Given:* Steady 2-D flow about an airfoil; far-field velocity U.  
*Find:* Circulation required for Kutta condition.  
Step 1: Kelvin’s theorem implies that a starting vortex of opposite circulation is shed so total Γ remains zero.  
*Why:* Initial fluid at rest has Γ = 0.  
Step 2: Bound circulation Γ = π c U sin α (thin airfoil).  
*Why:* Kutta condition fixes rear stagnation point.  
**Total circulation of airfoil plus starting vortex is identically zero.**  
*Reflection:* Explains why lift appears only after the starting vortex leaves.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                              | How to avoid it                              |
|-----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Applying theorem to fixed contours | Confusion between Eulerian and Lagrangian views | Always verify the curve consists of the same particles |
| Ignoring baroclinic torque        | Overlooking density variation               | Check whether p = p(ρ) before invoking the theorem |
| Forgetting the stretching term in D(dl)/Dt | Treating line elements as fixed vectors     | Derive the kinematic relation explicitly     |
| Using the theorem with viscosity  | Assuming “small” viscosity is harmless      | Confirm Reynolds number → ∞ or add viscous diffusion term |
| Confusing circulation with vorticity at a point | Both involve “rotation”                     | Remember circulation is an integral; vorticity is local |
| Applying to compressible shocks   | Shocks introduce non-conservative effects   | Restrict domain to smooth isentropic regions |
| Sign errors in path orientation   | Right-hand rule ambiguity                   | Fix consistent orientation with Stokes’ theorem |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Kelvin’s circulation theorem (Batchelor, *An Introduction to Fluid Dynamics*, 1967, §5.5): Let C(t) be a closed material curve in an inviscid fluid whose density is barotropic and whose body force is conservative. Then  
$$ \frac{D}{Dt}\oint_{C(t)}\mathbf{u}\cdot d\mathbf{x}=0. $$  
Equivalently, the flux of vorticity through any material surface is invariant.

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture a smoke ring: the same fluid particles always form the ring; Kelvin says the ring’s total twist never changes.  
2. **What to overlearn** — DΓ/Dt = 0 for barotropic, inviscid flow; Γ ≡ ∮ v · dl.  
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.  
4. **First-principles fallback** — Start from Euler’s equation, integrate around a material contour, apply the transport theorem for line integrals.

## 10. What this unlocks
Kelvin’s theorem is the gateway to all subsequent ideal-fluid vorticity theorems.  
- Helmholtz’s three vorticity theorems follow at once.  
- The frozen-in property of magnetic field lines in ideal MHD is formally identical.  
- Crocco’s theorem and the derivation of Bernoulli’s equation along streamlines both rely on the same circulation argument.  
- Modern vortex methods in computational aerodynamics conserve discrete circulation exactly because of this invariance.
