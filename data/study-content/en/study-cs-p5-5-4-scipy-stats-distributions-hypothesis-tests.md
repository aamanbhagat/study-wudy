## 1. The one-sentence answer
**scipy.stats supplies a single object-oriented interface to dozens of parametric distributions together with the classical machinery of hypothesis testing.**

A random variable is completely described by its cumulative distribution function. Once that function is known, every derived quantity—density, quantiles, moments, random samples—follows by differentiation, inversion or Monte-Carlo. scipy.stats simply materialises each named distribution as a Python object whose methods compute exactly those quantities.

Hypothesis testing reverses the arrow: given data, it asks how probable the observed sample would be under a fully specified reference distribution, then converts that probability into a binary decision via a pre-chosen significance level. The library therefore also exposes ready-made test statistics (Student’s t, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Wilcoxon, …) whose null distributions are again drawn from the same family of distribution objects.

> [!NOTE]
> The single most important “aha” is that every distribution object and every test function ultimately reduces to evaluating or inverting one cumulative distribution function; master the CDF and the entire module collapses to a handful of mechanical operations.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
SpaceX Flight Reliability uses the Weibull distribution inside scipy.stats to model time-to-failure of Merlin engines; the fitted survival function directly enters the launch-commit criteria for each Falcon 9 mission.

In semiconductor yield analysis, TSMC fits a mixture of log-normals to critical-dimension measurements across a wafer; the resulting quantile function predicts the fraction of dies that will fail speed binning before any silicon is diced.

DeepMind’s AlphaFold team employs the Kolmogorov–Smirnov two-sample test from scipy.stats on predicted versus experimental contact maps; a p-value below 10^{-6} triggers retraining of the structure module.

Particle physicists at CERN’s LHCb experiment run Anderson–Darling goodness-of-fit tests on invariant-mass sidebands to certify that background models are adequate before claiming a 5-sigma resonance.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — A distribution is nothing but a CDF
Any function F that is non-decreasing, right-continuous, lim x→−∞ F(x)=0 and lim x→∞ F(x)=1 defines a unique probability law.  
Example: the standard normal has  
$$F(x)=\frac12\bigl(1+\operatorname{erf}(x/\sqrt2)\bigr).$$  
> [!WARNING]  
> Treating the PDF as primary leads to non-normalisable or negative “densities” when the distribution is discrete or singular.

### Step 2 — scipy.stats wraps each CDF in a class
```python
from scipy.stats import norm
rv = norm(loc=0, scale=1)
```
rv.cdf, rv.pdf, rv.ppf, rv.rvs are thin wrappers around the underlying special-function implementations of F, F′, F⁻¹ and the inverse-transform sampler.

### Step 3 — Moments and random samples follow mechanically
Mean = ∫ x dF(x) is obtained by quadrature inside rv.mean(); variance likewise. Random variates are generated by U∼Uniform(0,1) then X = F⁻¹(U).

### Step 4 — A hypothesis test compares an observed statistic to its null distribution
Under the null, the test statistic T is itself a random variable whose CDF is known (Student’s t, χ², …). The p-value is 1−F_T(t_obs).

### Step 5 — Decision rule
Reject H₀ when p < α. This controls the Type-I error at exactly α when all modelling assumptions hold.

### Step 6 — The textbook statement
A parametric family {F_θ} together with a test statistic T whose distribution under θ=θ₀ is free of nuisance parameters yields a test of size α whose critical region is {t : 1−F_T(t) < α}.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Evaluate a normal tail probability**  
*Given:* X∼N(μ=5,σ=2), find P(X>7).  
*Find:* the numerical value.  
Step 1: Standardise → Z=(7−5)/2=1.  
*Why:* Location-scale transformation leaves the standard normal.  
Step 2: 1−Φ(1) where Φ is the standard normal CDF.  
*Why:* Definition of the survival function.  
Step 3: `1 - norm.cdf(1)` → 0.158655.  
**0.1587**  

*Reflection:* The only non-obvious move was recognising that every location-scale family reduces to the standard member via linear transformation.

**Example 2 — One-sample t-test**  
*Given:* measurements = [48.2, 49.1, 50.3, 49.8, 51.0], H₀: μ=50.  
*Find:* two-sided p-value.  
Step 1: Compute sample mean x̄=49.68, s=1.036.  
*Why:* These are the sufficient statistics for the normal model.  
Step 2: t=(49.68−50)/(1.036/√5)≈−0.69.  
*Why:* Student’s t statistic under normality.  
Step 3: `ttest_1samp(measurements,50).pvalue` → 0.527.  
**0.527**  

*Reflection:* The test automatically uses the t distribution with n−1 degrees of freedom; forgetting the degrees of freedom is the most common coding error.

**Example 3 — Generate correlated normals via copula**  
*Given:* two standard normals with Spearman ρ=0.7.  
*Find:* 1000 samples.  
Step 1: Draw uniforms via the Gaussian copula.  
*Why:* The copula separates marginals from dependence.  
Step 2: Apply norm.ppf to each margin.  
**Final sample matrix of shape (1000,2)**  

*Reflection:* The same distribution objects used for inference also serve as building blocks for simulation.

**Example 4 — Kolmogorov–Smirnov goodness-of-fit**  
*Given:* 200 draws allegedly from Exp(λ=1).  
*Find:* p-value against the fitted exponential.  
Step 1: MLE λ̂=1/mean.  
*Why:* Maximum-likelihood under the null family.  
Step 2: `kstest(data,'expon',args=(0,1/λ̂))`.  
**p-value reported directly**  

*Reflection:* The test is distribution-free once parameters are fixed; estimating parameters changes the null distribution and requires Monte-Carlo calibration.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                                      | How to avoid it                                      |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Using pdf for discrete probabilities | pdf is a density, not a probability mass            | Call pmf on discrete objects; pdf on continuous only |
| Forgetting to pass loc/scale when standardising | Default is always standard normal                   | Explicitly write norm(loc=μ,scale=σ)                 |
| Interpreting p>0.05 as “prove H₀” | p-value is P(data|H₀), never P(H₀|data)             | Report effect size and confidence interval together  |
| Treating KS p-value after parameter estimation as exact | Null distribution changes when parameters are fitted | Use Monte-Carlo or Lilliefors correction             |
| Broadcasting errors with arrays of different shapes | scipy.stats follows NumPy broadcasting rules        | Align shapes or use np.newaxis explicitly            |
| Confusing ppf with cdf            | ppf is the inverse; cdf is the integral             | Remember “ppf = percent-point function = quantile”   |
| Re-using the same random state for every test | Hidden state in legacy RandomState objects          | Pass random_state=Generator instance explicitly      |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let {F(·;θ), θ∈Θ} be a parametric family of CDFs. A test statistic T_n = T_n(X_1,…,X_n) possesses a null distribution G_n that does not depend on θ under H₀:θ=θ₀. The level-α test rejects when  
$$1-G_n(T_n)\le\alpha.$$  
Reference: Lehmann & Romano, *Testing Statistical Hypotheses*, 3e, §3.2, and SciPy 1.11 documentation for `scipy.stats.rv_continuous`.

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture a Swiss-army knife whose every blade is labelled “CDF”; opening the knife gives pdf, ppf or rvs instantly.  
2. **What to overlearn** — (a) rv.cdf(x) = P(X≤x), (b) rv.ppf(u) = F⁻¹(u), (c) p-value = 1−F(T_obs).  
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.  
4. **First-principles fallback** — Re-derive any quantity from the definition F(x)=∫_{-∞}^x f(t)dt and its inverse.
