## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Planetary orbits under an inverse-square central force are conic sections with the force center at one focus.**

Gravity pulls every planet toward the Sun with strength falling exactly as \(1/r^2\). Newton showed that any trajectory obeying this single rule must lie on an ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola whose focus coincides with the Sun. Bound orbits close on themselves and are therefore ellipses; the other two conics describe escape or fly-by paths. The geometric properties of the ellipse—two foci, constant sum of distances—turn out to be direct algebraic consequences of the force law rather than an arbitrary fit to data.

Kepler reached the same conclusion decades earlier by painstaking analysis of Tycho Brahe’s naked-eye observations. He replaced the ancient prejudice of circular orbits with the statement that Mars traces an ellipse. The mathematical link between the physical force and the curve remained hidden until Newton supplied it.

> [!NOTE]
> The single fact that the force is inverse-square forces every orbit to be a conic; change the exponent by even a tiny amount and the curve ceases to be any conic section at all.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
SpaceX’s Falcon 9 and Starship trajectories are designed as elliptical transfer orbits whose parameters are computed from the vis-viva equation that follows directly from the conic-section solution. NASA’s Parker Solar Probe uses the same ellipse mathematics to set perihelion distances inside 10 solar radii while guaranteeing safe return to Earth. GPS satellites broadcast ephemerides expressed as Keplerian elements—semi-major axis, eccentricity, argument of perigee—because the underlying orbits remain ellipses to first order. ESA’s Gaia mission maps stellar positions by fitting observed arcs to the identical family of conics, allowing micro-arcsecond astrometry. In semiconductor manufacturing, electron-beam lithography systems steer beams along elliptical paths generated by the same inverse-square mathematics that governs planetary motion, ensuring sub-nanometer overlay accuracy.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Central force implies planar motion
A force always directed toward a fixed point exerts no torque about that point. Angular momentum about the Sun is therefore conserved, locking the velocity vector into a fixed plane. All subsequent motion is confined to that plane.

### Step 2 — Polar coordinates centered at the Sun
Place the Sun at the origin. The position of the planet is \((r,\theta)\). The radial acceleration contains both \(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\) and the gravitational acceleration \(-GM/r^2\).

### Step 3 — Angular momentum supplies the substitution
Define specific angular momentum \(h = r^2\dot\theta =\) constant. Then \(\dot\theta = h/r^2\). Differentiate \(u=1/r\) with respect to \(\theta\) to obtain the Binet substitution \(\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u = GM/h^2\).

### Step 4 — The orbit equation integrates immediately
The linear non-homogeneous equation has general solution
\[
u = \frac{GM}{h^2} + A\cos(\theta-\theta_0).
\]
Back-substituting \(r=1/u\) yields
\[
r = \frac{h^2/GM}{1+e\cos\theta},
\]
where \(e = Ah^2/GM\).

### Step 5 — The polar equation is the standard conic
The equation above is precisely the polar form of a conic section with focus at the origin and eccentricity \(e\). When total energy is negative, \(e<1\) and the curve is an ellipse.

### Step 6 — Textbook statement of Kepler’s first law
Every planet moves in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Recover the semi-latus rectum**
*Given:* \(h=1\), \(GM=1\), \(e=0\).
*Find:* the constant radius.
\[
r=\frac{1}{1+0}=1.
\]
*Why:* The denominator reduces to 1 when eccentricity vanishes, leaving a circle whose radius equals the semi-latus rectum \(h^2/GM\).

**Example 2 — Identify the curve from eccentricity**
*Given:* \(e=0.6\), periapsis distance 1.
*Find:* the type of conic.
Because \(e<1\) the trajectory is an ellipse. The semi-latus rectum \(p=h^2/GM=1+e=1.6\), so \(a=p/(1-e^2)=2.5\).

**Example 3 — Convert polar equation to Cartesian**
*Given:* \(r=\frac{2}{1+0.5\cos\theta}\).
Multiply through by the denominator:
\[
r+0.5r\cos\theta=2 \implies r+0.5x=2.
\]
Square and substitute \(r^2=x^2+y^2\):
\[
(x^2+y^2)=(2-0.5x)^2.
\]
Expand and collect terms to obtain the standard ellipse equation
\[
\frac{(x+0.4)^2}{3.84}+\frac{y^2}{3.2}=1.
\]

**Example 4 — Energy fixes eccentricity**
*Given:* specific energy \(\mathcal{E}=-GM/(2a)\). Show \(e=\sqrt{1+2\mathcal{E}h^2/(GM)^2}\).
Substitute \(\mathcal{E}\) into the constant term of the orbit equation; the algebra yields exactly the displayed formula for \(e\).

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap | Why it happens | How to avoid it |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| Placing the coordinate origin at the ellipse center instead of the focus | Textbooks often draw the geometric center; physics places the force at a focus | Always start the derivation with the force center at the pole. |
| Treating \(e\) as a free geometric parameter | Students forget \(e\) is fixed by energy and angular momentum | Compute \(e=\sqrt{1+2\mathcal{E}h^2/GM^2}\) before drawing the orbit. |
| Confusing semi-latus rectum with semi-major axis | Both appear in the polar equation | Remember \(p=a(1-e^2)\). |
| Forgetting the orbit is planar | Three-dimensional intuition intrudes | Invoke torque = 0 once, then restrict all vectors to the plane. |
| Using the wrong sign in the force law | Sign error in radial acceleration produces a repulsive rather than attractive orbit | Keep the gravitational term negative: \(-GM/r^2\). |
| Assuming every ellipse has the Sun at the geometric center | Visual symmetry misleads | Label the empty focus explicitly on every diagram. |
| Neglecting the constant of integration \(\theta_0\) | Orientation appears arbitrary | Fix \(\theta_0\) by the observed argument of periapsis. |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let a particle of negligible mass move under the Newtonian acceleration \(\ddot{\mathbf{r}}=-GM\mathbf{r}/r^3\). Then its trajectory is a conic section whose focus lies at the origin. If the specific energy \(\mathcal{E}<0\), the trajectory is an ellipse with the origin at one focus. (Goldstein, Poole & Safko, *Classical Mechanics*, 3rd ed., §3.7, Theorem 3.7.1.)

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture the Sun “tugging” the planet from one focus while an invisible twin focus keeps the string length constant; the inverse-square law is what makes the string taut.
2. **What to overlearn** — Polar orbit equation \(r = p/(1+e\cos\theta)\), relation \(e=\sqrt{1+2\mathcal{E}h^2/GM^2}\), and \(p=a(1-e^2)\).
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.
4. **First-principles fallback** — Re-derive the Binet equation from angular-momentum conservation and integrate; the cosine term appears automatically.

## 10. What this unlocks
The identification of orbits with conics supplies the language for all subsequent orbital mechanics. The next concepts are: Kepler’s second and third laws, the vis-viva equation, Lambert’s problem, Gauss’s method of preliminary orbit determination, and the restricted three-body problem where zero-velocity curves are also conics.
