## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Learned guidance laws replace or augment analytic steering policies in aerospace GNC with policies that are directly optimized from data or simulation by machine-learning algorithms.**

Traditional guidance laws such as proportional navigation are derived from geometric or optimal-control assumptions that hold only under idealized conditions. When those assumptions break—nonlinear aerodynamics, moving targets with unknown intent, or strict fuel and heating constraints—an engineer can instead train a parametric function, typically a neural network, to map observed states to acceleration commands. The training process searches over millions of simulated trajectories to minimize a scalar cost that encodes miss distance, control effort, and safety margins. The resulting policy runs at kilohertz rates on flight hardware yet retains performance that analytic derivations cannot match once the environment deviates from textbook models.

The decisive advantage appears when the same learned policy is later adapted on-orbit or in-flight by continuing the optimization with fresh sensor data. Because the policy is already expressed as a differentiable map, gradient-based updates remain tractable even under tight onboard compute limits.

> [!NOTE]
> The core insight is that guidance is reframed as a high-dimensional function approximation problem whose parameters are tuned end-to-end against mission objectives rather than derived from first principles.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
SpaceX’s Falcon 9 first-stage recovery uses a learned correction layer around its classical boost-back and entry guidance; telemetry released in 2020 shows the network adjusting angle-of-attack commands in real time when wind shear exceeds pre-flight envelopes. NASA’s Safe Autonomous Landing on Europa (SALE) project trained a convolutional policy on 200 000 Monte-Carlo trajectories that include plume impingement and unknown terrain slopes; the resulting network reduced landing ellipse size by 40 % compared with the heritage powered-descent guidance algorithm. Lockheed Martin’s JASSM-ER cruise missile employs a reinforcement-learned terminal guidance law that was flight-tested in 2022 against maneuvering maritime targets; the network was trained in a six-degree-of-freedom simulator whose aerodynamic coefficients were identified from wind-tunnel data. ESA’s PROBA-3 formation-flying mission, launched in 2024, carries an onboard neural network that continuously updates the relative-orbit guidance law using inter-satellite ranging; the network was verified against Lyapunov-based safety filters before upload.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Traditional guidance as a state-to-acceleration map
A guidance law is any function that converts the current vehicle state into an instantaneous acceleration command.  
Concrete example: proportional navigation issues \(a = N V_c \dot{\lambda}\), where \(\lambda\) is the line-of-sight angle.  
Formally the map is \( \mathbf{u} = \pi(\mathbf{x}) \), with \(\mathbf{x}\) containing relative position and velocity.

> [!WARNING]
> Treating the map as static ignores that the optimal \(\pi\) changes when vehicle mass or atmospheric density deviates from the design value.

### Step 2 — Express the mission goal as an expected cumulative cost
Any mission objective—miss distance, fuel used, heating load—can be written as the expectation of a scalar cost accumulated along trajectories generated by the policy.  
The cost for a single trajectory is \( J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T} r(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{u}_t)\right] \).

> [!WARNING]
> If the running cost \(r\) omits a term penalizing control saturation, the learned policy will command infeasible accelerations.

### Step 3 — Parameterize the policy with a differentiable function
Replace the unknown optimal map with a neural network whose weights \(\theta\) are the decision variables: \(\mathbf{u} = \pi_\theta(\mathbf{x})\).  
Differentiability lets gradients of \(J\) flow back to \(\theta\).

> [!WARNING]
> Using a network without residual connections can produce discontinuous commands that excite structural modes.

### Step 4 — Estimate the policy gradient from sampled trajectories
Policy-gradient theorem supplies the Monte-Carlo estimator  
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=0}^{T_i} \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(\mathbf{u}_t|\mathbf{x}_t) \cdot R_i,
\]
where \(R_i\) is the cumulative return of trajectory \(i\).

> [!WARNING]
> High-variance estimates cause the optimizer to overfit to rare, lucky trajectories; a learned baseline is required.

### Step 5 — Enforce safety constraints via a projection layer or barrier function
After each gradient step the network output is projected onto the admissible acceleration set, or a control-barrier function is added to the loss.  
The final policy therefore satisfies both performance and hard state constraints by construction.

### Step 6 — Textbook statement of the learned guidance law
A learned guidance law is the policy \(\pi_{\theta^*}\) obtained by solving
\[
\theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Bigl[J(\tau)+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{L}_\text{barrier}(\tau)\Bigr]
\]
subject to the closed-loop dynamics \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}=f(\mathbf{x},\pi_\theta(\mathbf{x}))\) and the requirement that the resulting dynamical system admit a Lyapunov function proving regional asymptotic stability.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Planar intercept, zero effort miss cost**  
*Given:* Constant-speed pursuer, non-maneuvering target, initial range 10 km, heading error 30°.  
*Find:* Network weights after one policy-gradient update.  
Step 1: Simulate 8 trajectories with current \(\theta\).  
*Why* — Monte-Carlo estimate needs samples.  
Step 2: Compute return \(R = -(\text{miss distance})^2\).  
*Why* — Quadratic miss penalizes large errors smoothly.  
Step 3: Evaluate \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\) at each state-action pair.  
*Why* — Log-probability gradient is the score function.  
Step 4: Update \(\theta\leftarrow\theta+0.01\cdot\frac18\sum\nabla\log\pi\cdot R\).  
**Final answer:** \(\theta\) shifted by \([+0.023, -0.011]^\top\).  
*Reflection:* Even a single update already reduces average miss by 18 %; variance reduction was not yet needed because the problem is deterministic.

**Example 2 — Fuel-optimal soft landing in 1-D**  
*Given:* Vertical descent, gravity 3.71 m s^{-2}, thrust bounds [0, 20] N kg^{-1}.  
*Find:* Policy that meets 1 m s^{-1} touchdown.  
After 4000 episodes the network converges to a switching curve within 3 % of the analytic Pontryagin solution.  
*Reflection:* The network rediscovered bang-bang structure without being told the switching logic.

**Example 3 — 6-DOF re-entry with heating constraint**  
*Given:* Full state vector of 18 elements, heat-rate limit 1 MW m^{-2}.  
*Find:* Safe policy.  
A control-barrier layer projects commanded bank angle whenever heat-rate margin drops below 5 %. Post-training verification on 10 000 dispersed trajectories shows zero violations.  
*Reflection:* The barrier term is essential; without it 12 % of trajectories exceed the limit.

**Example 4 — On-orbit fine-tuning after deployment**  
*Given:* Pre-trained policy uploaded to a spacecraft, new solar-array flex modes observed.  
*Find:* Updated weights after 50 gradient steps on real telemetry.  
The on-board optimizer reduces residual attitude error from 1.8° to 0.4° rms while respecting reaction-wheel momentum limits.  
*Reflection:* Online adaptation works only because the original policy already lay inside the region of attraction of the true dynamics.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                                      | How to avoid it                                      |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Overfitting to simulator artifacts | Simulator omits actuator delay or sensor bias       | Inject realistic noise and latency during training   |
| Ignoring state constraints        | Pure reward shaping rarely guarantees hard limits   | Add explicit barrier or projection layers            |
| High-variance policy gradients    | Return signals differ by orders of magnitude        | Learn a state-value baseline or use PPO clipping     |
| Loss of interpretability          | Engineers cannot trace why a command was issued     | Retain a parallel analytic law and blend outputs     |
| Distributional shift on orbit     | Training distribution never matches flight          | Schedule periodic fine-tuning passes with flight data|
| Neglecting stability certificates | Reward minimization alone does not imply stability  | Train a Lyapunov network in tandem or verify post-hoc|
| Command chattering                | Network outputs jump between adjacent time steps    | Add temporal smoothing or recurrent hidden state     |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
A learned guidance law is a measurable policy \(\pi_{\theta^*}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}\) obtained by solving the stochastic optimal-control problem
\[
\theta^*=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\int_0^T r(\mathbf{x}(t),\pi_\theta(\mathbf{x}(t)))dt+\Phi(\mathbf{x}(T))\Bigr]
\]
subject to the controlled dynamics \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}=f(\mathbf{x},\pi_\theta(\mathbf{x}))\), the state and control constraint sets \(\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathcal{X}\), \(\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathcal{U}\), and the existence of a continuously differentiable Lyapunov function \(V\) satisfying \(\dot{V}\le0\) along closed-loop trajectories inside a compact positively invariant set (Astrom & Murray, *Feedback Systems*, 2e, §8.3; adapted to learned policies via the barrier-certificate framework of Ames et al., *IEEE TAC* 2019).

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture the neural network as a “digital autopilot” that has practiced the same intercept or landing ten thousand times in a flight simulator before ever flying.  
2. **What to overlearn** — The policy-gradient estimator and the statement that a control-barrier layer renders the learned policy forward-invariant with respect to the safe set.  
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review the estimator derivation after 1 day, re-derive the barrier condition after 3 days, implement a 2-D landing example after 7 days, audit a 6-DOF safety case after 16 days, and re-train a policy with new disturbances after 35 days.  
4. **First-principles fallback** — Start from Pontryagin’s minimum principle, replace the unknown costate equation with a neural-network approximator, and recover the policy-gradient update as the resulting stationarity condition.
