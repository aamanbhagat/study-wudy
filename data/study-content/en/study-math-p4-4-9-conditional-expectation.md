## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Conditional expectation** \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\) is the \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable random variable that reproduces the integrals of \(X\) over every set in the sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{G}\).

Think of an ordinary expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) as a single number that summarises the average behaviour of \(X\). When extra information arrives—encoded as a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{G}\)—that single number must be replaced by a random variable whose value can change with the new information yet still respects the original averaging law on every measurable set. The resulting object is no longer a constant; it is a function of the information.

In elementary language, \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y]\) answers the question “what is the average of \(X\) once we know that \(Y\) equals \(y\)?” The full random variable \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\) simply assembles all these conditional averages into one object that is constant on the level sets of \(Y\).

> [!NOTE]
> The defining property is not “replace \(X\) by its average given \(Y\)”; it is the integral-matching requirement \(\int_A \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\,dP = \int_A X\,dP\) for every \(A \in \mathcal{G}\). Everything else follows from this single equality.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
In aerospace navigation, the extended Kalman filter used on the Mars Perseverance rover computes the conditional expectation of the vehicle’s position and velocity given noisy sensor readings; each measurement update replaces the prior mean with the conditional mean under the linear-Gaussian model.

In quantitative finance, the pricing of a barrier option under the risk-neutral measure requires the conditional expectation of the discounted payoff given the filtration generated by the underlying Brownian motion up to each trading time; this quantity is computed daily by every major investment bank’s pricing library.

Modern recommender systems at Netflix maintain a posterior over user taste vectors; the recommendation score for an unseen title is precisely the conditional expectation of the user’s rating given the observed viewing history, obtained via variational inference that approximates the true conditional expectation.

In semiconductor yield analysis, TSMC uses conditional expectations of defect counts given process parameters measured at earlier fabrication steps; these expectations drive real-time adjustments to ion-implantation doses, reducing scrap by several percentage points.

Particle-physics experiments at CERN compute conditional expectations of missing transverse energy given observed jet momenta; the resulting quantity enters the trigger decision logic that decides whether an event is worth storing.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Conditioning on an event
Plain-English claim: knowing that an event \(A\) occurred changes the average value of \(X\) to the ordinary expectation computed only on \(A\).

Concrete example: roll a fair die; let \(X\) be the outcome and \(A=\{4,5,6\}\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid A]=5\).

Formal statement:
\[
\mathbb{E}[X\mid A]=\frac{1}{P(A)}\int_A X\,dP.
\]

> [!WARNING]
> Treating \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid A]\) as a number attached to every outcome in \(A\) rather than as a random variable constant on \(A\) breaks later measurability arguments.

### Step 2 — Conditioning on a finite partition
Plain-English claim: when information partitions \(\Omega\) into finitely many atoms \(A_i\), the conditional expectation is the piecewise-constant random variable that equals \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid A_i]\) on each atom.

Formal statement: if \(\mathcal{G}=\sigma(\{A_1,\dots,A_n\})\), then
\[
\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]=\sum_i\mathbb{E}[X\mid A_i]\mathbf{1}_{A_i}.
\]

> [!WARNING]
> Forgetting that the value on each atom must itself be an average (not the value of \(X\) at a representative point) produces a non-measurable or biased object.

### Step 3 — Conditioning on a discrete random variable
Plain-English claim: replace the partition by the level sets of a discrete random variable \(Y\); the conditional expectation becomes a function of \(Y\).

Formal statement:
\[
\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\sum_y\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]\mathbf{1}_{\{Y=y\}}.
\]

> [!WARNING]
> Writing \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]\) outside an indicator and then treating the whole expression as a number leads to dimension errors when \(Y\) is later integrated.

### Step 4 — The integral-matching property
Plain-English claim: no matter how the information is represented, the conditional expectation must preserve the total “mass” of \(X\) on every measurable set.

Formal statement: a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable integrable random variable \(Z\) satisfies
\[
\int_A Z\,dP=\int_A X\,dP\qquad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{G}
\]
if and only if \(Z=\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\) almost surely.

> [!WARNING]
> Verifying the equality only for generators of \(\mathcal{G}\) is insufficient unless the monotone-class or Dynkin theorem is invoked to extend to the whole \(\sigma\)-algebra.

### Step 5 — Existence and uniqueness
Plain-English claim: the Radon–Nikodym theorem applied to the signed measure \(\nu(A)=\int_A X\,dP\) on \(\mathcal{G}\) yields a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable density that is the conditional expectation; any two versions differ on a null set.

Formal statement: there exists a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable integrable \(Z\) satisfying the integral identity; if \(Z'\) is another such random variable then \(Z=Z'\) a.s.

> [!WARNING]
> Claiming uniqueness everywhere rather than almost surely creates contradictions on null sets that later appear in martingale convergence arguments.

### Step 6 — The textbook statement
The conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\) is the a.s.-unique \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable random variable satisfying the integral identity above.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Discrete uniform**
- *Given:* \(\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\), \(P\) uniform, \(X(\omega)=\omega\), \(A=\{2,4,6\}\).
- *Find:* \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid A]\).
- Step 1: compute \(P(A)=1/2\).
  *Why:* definition of uniform probability.
- Step 2: \(\int_A X\,dP=(2+4+6)/6=2\).
  *Why:* sum of values on \(A\) divided by total measure.
- Step 3: divide: \(2/(1/2)=4\).
  *Why:* scaling by reciprocal probability.
**4**

*Reflection:* the result is constant on \(A\), illustrating the measurability requirement.

**Example 2 — Function of discrete RV**
- *Given:* \(Y\) takes values \(\pm1\) with equal probability; \(X=Y^2\).
- *Find:* \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\).
- Step 1: note \(X=1\) always.
  *Why:* algebraic identity.
- Step 2: on \(\{Y=1\}\) and \(\{Y=-1\}\) the value is 1.
  *Why:* \(X\) is constant, hence measurable w.r.t. any \(\sigma\)-algebra.
**1**

*Reflection:* shows that conditioning a constant yields the constant.

**Example 3 — Continuous bivariate normal**
- *Given:* \((X,Y)\) jointly normal, zero mean, \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=1\), \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)=1\), \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\rho\).
- *Find:* \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\).
- Step 1: the conditional density is normal with mean \(\rho Y\).
  *Why:* standard formula for bivariate normal.
- Step 2: expectation of that normal is its mean \(\rho Y\).
  *Why:* symmetry of normal around its mean.
**\rho Y**

*Reflection:* linearity appears because the joint distribution is elliptically symmetric.

**Example 4 — Indicator and tail event**
- *Given:* \(X\) integrable, \(\mathcal{G}=\sigma(X)\).
- *Find:* \(\mathbb{E}[X\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{X>0\}}\mid\mathcal{G}]\).
- Step 1: the product is already \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable.
  *Why:* composition of measurable functions.
- Step 2: integral identity holds trivially on sets in \(\mathcal{G}\).
  *Why:* both sides equal the integral of the same function.
**X·1_{X>0}**

*Reflection:* when the integrand is already measurable, conditioning leaves it unchanged.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                                      | How to avoid it                                      |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Treating \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\) as a number | Confusion with unconditional expectation            | Always verify the output is a random variable        |
| Writing \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]\) without indicator | Notation abuse                                      | Restore the indicator when forming the RV            |
| Assuming \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]\) always | Forgetting dependence                               | Check covariance or joint support                    |
| Confusing almost-sure equality with everywhere equality | Ignoring null sets                                  | State “a.s.” explicitly in every uniqueness claim    |
| Applying linearity without integrability      | Hidden infinite expectations                        | Verify \(\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty\) before any identity  |
| Conditioning on events of probability zero   | Division by zero in elementary formulas             | Use the integral definition exclusively              |
| Expecting monotonicity in the conditioning variable | Counter-examples exist (e.g., non-monotone regression) | Construct explicit counter-examples early            |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\) be a probability space, \(X\) an integrable random variable, and \(\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{F}\) a sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra. A random variable \(Z\) is a version of the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\) if (i) \(Z\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, (ii) \(\mathbb{E}|Z|<\infty\), and (iii)
\[
\int_A Z\,dP=\int_A X\,dP\qquad\text{for every }A\in\mathcal{G}.
\]
Any two versions coincide \(P\)-almost surely. (Billingsley, *Probability and Measure*, 3rd ed., §34.)

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — picture a lighthouse whose beam reveals only the average height of waves inside each illuminated sector; the height reported inside each sector is exactly \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\).
2. **What to overlearn** — the integral identity, the fact that \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, and the tower property \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]]=\mathbb{E}[X]\).
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.
4. **First-principles fallback** — reconstruct via Radon–Nikodym on the restricted measure space \((\Omega,\mathcal{G},P)\).

## 10. What this unlocks
Conditional expectation is the gateway to martingale theory, Doob’s optional sampling theorem, and the modern theory of stochastic processes. It also supplies the projection operator that underlies \(L^2\) theory of Hilbert spaces of random variables, Kalman filtering, and all modern formulations of Bayesian updating.

- Martingales and convergence theorems
- Markov chains and transition operators
- Stochastic calculus (Ito integral as limit of conditional expectations)
- Empirical risk minimisation viewed as conditional-expectation estimation
