## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Self-inductance \(L\) of a circuit is the constant of proportionality between the magnetic flux linkage through that circuit and the current that produces it; mutual inductance \(M\) is the corresponding constant between the flux linkage through one circuit and the current in a second, nearby circuit.**

Magnetic flux through any surface bounded by a wire loop equals the surface integral of \(\mathbf{B}\). When the loop carries current \(I\), the resulting \(\mathbf{B}\) is itself proportional to \(I\) by the Biot–Savart law. Consequently the total flux linkage \(\Phi\) scales linearly with \(I\), and the factor of proportionality is defined as the inductance. For a single circuit the factor is called self-inductance \(L\); for two distinct circuits it is mutual inductance \(M\).

Because Faraday’s law states that induced emf equals the negative rate of change of flux linkage, both \(L\) and \(M\) convert a changing current directly into an opposing voltage: \(\mathcal{E} = -L\,dI/dt\) or \(\mathcal{E}_2 = -M\,dI_1/dt\). The constants \(L\) and \(M\) therefore encode geometry and material properties but never depend on the value of the current itself.

> [!NOTE]
> The “opposition” is not resistance; it is inertia of the magnetic field. A current cannot jump discontinuously because that would require infinite voltage to change the stored magnetic energy in zero time.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
In the superconducting magnets of CERN’s LHC, 1232 dipole magnets store 11 GJ at 8.3 T using Nb-Ti coils whose self-inductance exceeds 100 H; any rapid quench must be managed through precisely known \(L\) to avoid destructive voltages.  

SpaceX’s Starlink satellites employ Hall-effect thrusters whose solenoidal electromagnets rely on calibrated self-inductance to maintain stable plasma confinement while the spacecraft draws pulsed power from solar arrays.  

Wireless charging pads standardized under the Qi protocol transfer energy between two planar coils whose mutual inductance \(M\) varies with alignment; circuit controllers continuously estimate \(M\) to keep efficiency above 80 % at 15 W.  

The ignition system of the Ariane 5 solid-rocket boosters uses mutual-inductance transformers to step up capacitor discharge voltages to 20 kV while remaining immune to the intense vibration and magnetic fields inside the motor casing.

## 3. Mental prerequisites

| Concept                  | Why you need it here                                      |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Magnetic flux \(\Phi = \int\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A}\) | Inductance is defined through flux linkage.               |
| Faraday’s law \(\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\) | Links changing flux directly to observable voltage.       |
| Biot–Savart or Ampère’s law for \(\mathbf{B}(I)\) | Shows that \(\mathbf{B}\) (hence \(\Phi\)) scales with current. |
| Linear superposition of fields | Guarantees \(\Phi \propto I\), so \(L\) and \(M\) are constants. |

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Flux is produced by current
A steady current in a wire creates a magnetic field whose strength at every point is proportional to the current. For any fixed geometry the surface integral of that field over a surface bounded by the wire therefore yields a flux \(\Phi\) that is strictly linear in \(I\).

Concrete example: a single circular loop of radius \(R\) carrying current \(I\) produces \(B = \mu_0 I/(2R)\) at its center; the flux through the loop is then \(\Phi = B\cdot\pi R^2 \propto I\).

Formal statement:
\[
\Phi = \int_S \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r};I)\,dA \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \Phi = (\text{geometry factor})\times I.
\]

> [!WARNING]
> Treating \(\mathbf{B}\) as independent of \(I\) will make the later derivative \(d\Phi/dt\) vanish, erasing induction entirely.

### Step 2 — Flux linkage for a multi-turn coil
When the same current threads \(N\) turns, each turn contributes its own flux. The total flux linkage \(\Lambda = N\Phi\) therefore multiplies the single-turn result by \(N\).

### Step 3 — Definition of self-inductance \(L\)
The constant that converts current into flux linkage for a single circuit is called self-inductance:
\[
\Lambda = L I \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad L \equiv \frac{\Lambda}{I}.
\]
\(L\) has units of henry (Wb A\(^{-1}\)) and depends only on geometry and permeability.

### Step 4 — Mutual flux between two circuits
Place a second circuit nearby. Current \(I_1\) in circuit 1 produces field \(\mathbf{B}_1\). The flux through circuit 2 is
\[
\Phi_{21} = \int_{S_2} \mathbf{B}_1\,dA.
\]
Again linearity gives \(\Phi_{21} = M I_1\), where the mutual inductance \(M\) encodes the shared geometry.

### Step 5 — Reciprocity theorem
The same calculation with roles reversed yields \(\Phi_{12} = M I_2\). For linear media \(M_{12} = M_{21} \equiv M\).

### Step 6 — Induced emf from Faraday’s law
Differentiating the defining relations with respect to time produces the voltages that appear in circuit equations:
\[
\mathcal{E}_1 = -L\frac{dI_1}{dt}, \qquad \mathcal{E}_2 = -M\frac{dI_1}{dt}.
\]
These are the textbook circuit-element definitions of inductors and coupled inductors.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Single-layer solenoid**  
*Given:* Length \(\ell\), cross-section \(A\), \(N\) turns, vacuum interior, current \(I\).  
*Find:* Self-inductance \(L\).  

Inside the solenoid \(B = \mu_0 N I/\ell\).  
*Why:* Ampère’s law applied to a rectangular loop.  

Flux through one turn: \(\Phi = B A = \mu_0 N I A/\ell\).  
*Why:* Uniform field assumed.  

Total flux linkage: \(\Lambda = N\Phi = \mu_0 N^2 A I/\ell\).  
*Why:* Each of the \(N\) turns links the same flux.  

Thus \(L = \Lambda/I = \mu_0 N^2 A/\ell\).  
**\(L = \mu_0 N^2 A/\ell\)**  

*Reflection:* The \(N^2\) factor arises because both the field and the number of turns that experience it grow with \(N\); this quadratic scaling appears in every tightly wound coil.

**Example 2 — Two coaxial solenoids**  
*Given:* Outer solenoid \(N_1,\ell,A_1\); inner solenoid \(N_2,\ell,A_2 < A_1\).  
*Find:* Mutual inductance \(M\).  

Field inside outer solenoid: \(B_1 = \mu_0 N_1 I_1/\ell\).  
*Why:* Same Ampère-law result.  

Flux through each inner turn: \(\Phi_{21} = B_1 A_2\).  
*Why:* Inner area limits the integral.  

\(\Lambda_2 = N_2\Phi_{21} = \mu_0 N_1 N_2 A_2 I_1/\ell\).  
Thus \(M = \mu_0 N_1 N_2 A_2/\ell\).  
**\(M = \mu_0 N_1 N_2 A_2/\ell\)**  

*Reflection:* \(M\) is smaller than either self-inductance because only the overlapping area contributes.

**Example 3 — Two loops, distance \(d \gg R\)**  
*Given:* Two circular loops, radius \(R\), centers separated by \(d\) along axis.  
*Find:* Approximate \(M\).  

On-axis field of loop 1 at distance \(d\): \(B_z = \mu_0 I_1 R^2/(2(R^2+d^2)^{3/2})\).  
*Why:* Standard Biot–Savart result.  

Flux \(\Phi_{21} \approx B_z\cdot\pi R^2\) (uniform-field approximation).  
\(M \approx \frac{\mu_0\pi R^4}{2d^3}\) for \(d\gg R\).  
**\(M \approx \frac{\mu_0\pi R^4}{2d^3}\)**  

*Reflection:* The rapid \(1/d^3\) decay shows why loosely coupled coils have small \(M\).

**Example 4 — Energy stored**  
*Given:* Current \(I\) established in inductor \(L\).  
*Find:* Stored magnetic energy.  

Power delivered: \(P = \mathcal{E}I = LI\,dI/dt\).  
*Why:* Instantaneous electrical power.  

Integrate: \(U = \int_0^I L I'\,dI' = \frac12 L I^2\).  
**\(U = \frac12 L I^2\)**  

*Reflection:* The factor \(1/2\) mirrors the electrostatic formula \(\frac12 C V^2\), underscoring duality between \(L\) and \(C\).

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap | Why it happens | How to avoid it |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| Using \(\Phi = B A\) when \(B\) is nonuniform | Students forget the integral definition. | Always verify field uniformity or integrate explicitly. |
| Sign error in mutual inductance | Direction of windings omitted. | Draw winding sense and apply right-hand rule before assigning \(M > 0\). |
| Treating \(L\) as constant when core saturates | Nonlinear \(B(H)\) curve ignored. | Check \(H < H_{\text{sat}}\) or use incremental inductance. |
| Confusing flux linkage \(\Lambda\) with single-turn flux \(\Phi\) | Notation \(N\Phi\) omitted. | Write \(\Lambda = N\Phi\) explicitly in every calculation. |
| Assuming \(M = \sqrt{L_1 L_2}\) always | Perfect coupling taken for granted. | Compute coupling coefficient \(k = M/\sqrt{L_1 L_2} \le 1\) from geometry. |
| Forgetting return flux in air-core coils | Only internal field counted. | Include external flux paths when calculating total linkage. |
| Units mismatch (Wb vs. Wb-turn) | Turns treated as dimensionless. | Keep track of \(N\) until the final division by \(I\). |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
For a single circuit carrying current \(I\), the self-inductance is defined by
\[
L = \frac{N\Phi_B}{I},
\]
where \(\Phi_B\) is the magnetic flux through one turn and the circuit is assumed linear and stationary. For two circuits the mutual inductance is
\[
M = \frac{N_2\Phi_{21}}{I_1} = \frac{N_1\Phi_{12}}{I_2}.
\]
These definitions appear in Griffiths, *Introduction to Electrodynamics*, 4e, §7.2.3 and §7.3. The induced emfs then follow directly from Faraday’s law.
