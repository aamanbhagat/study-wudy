## 1. The one-sentence answer
**The time-dependent Schrödinger equation is the fundamental dynamical law of non-relativistic quantum mechanics that evolves the wave function \(\psi(\mathbf{r},t)\) in time, while the time-independent Schrödinger equation is its stationary-state reduction that yields energy eigenfunctions and eigenvalues.**

In classical mechanics a particle follows a definite trajectory under Newton’s second law. In quantum mechanics the state is a complex-valued wave function whose squared modulus gives probability density. The time-dependent equation replaces \(F=ma\) by requiring that the time derivative of \(\psi\) is proportional to the action of the Hamiltonian operator on \(\psi\).

When the potential does not depend explicitly on time, the wave function can be factored into a spatial part and a pure phase that oscillates at frequency \(E/\hbar\). Substituting this product form immediately produces the time-independent eigenvalue problem whose solutions are the allowed energies and the corresponding stationary probability densities.

> [!NOTE]
> The time-independent equation is not an approximation; it is an exact consequence of the time-dependent equation for time-independent Hamiltonians, and every solution of the former can be reassembled into a solution of the latter by multiplying by the phase factor \(e^{-iEt/\hbar}\).

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
Semiconductor device engineers at Intel and TSMC solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation self-consistently with Poisson’s equation to predict electron tunneling through 1-nm gate oxides in FinFETs; without these solutions, leakage currents would be miscalculated by orders of magnitude.

NASA’s Cold Atom Laboratory on the ISS uses the time-dependent Schrödinger equation to model the expansion and interference of Bose–Einstein condensates in microgravity, enabling precision tests of the equivalence principle that feed into future satellite navigation corrections.

Quantum-computing teams at IBM and Google apply the time-dependent equation to simulate Trotterized evolution of superconducting qubits under time-varying control pulses, achieving gate fidelities above 99.9 % only after calibrating against the exact unitary generated by the Schrödinger dynamics.

In astrophysics, models of neutron-star crusts solve the time-independent equation for neutrons in a periodic nuclear lattice to predict superfluid gaps that govern glitch rise times observed by NICER and radio telescopes.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Probability density and continuity
A particle is described by a complex field \(\psi(\mathbf{r},t)\) such that \(|\psi|^2\) is the probability density. Probability must be conserved, so the continuity equation \(\partial_t|\psi|^2+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}=0\) must hold for some probability current \(\mathbf{j}\).

For the free particle the current that satisfies continuity is \(\mathbf{j}=(\hbar/2mi)(\psi^*\nabla\psi-\psi\nabla\psi^*)\).

> [!WARNING]
> Treating \(\psi\) itself as a probability density (instead of \(|\psi|^2\)) violates conservation and produces negative probabilities.

### Step 2 — Operator correspondence
Replace the classical energy relation \(E=T+V\) by operators: momentum \(\mathbf{p}\to-i\hbar\nabla\), position \(\mathbf{r}\to\mathbf{r}\), and therefore the Hamiltonian \(H\to\hat{H}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V(\mathbf{r})\).

The time derivative of \(\psi\) must be proportional to \(\hat{H}\psi\) because energy generates time translations in quantum mechanics.

### Step 3 — The time-dependent equation
Require that \(i\hbar\partial_t\psi=\hat{H}\psi\). This first-order-in-time equation automatically preserves normalization when \(\hat{H}\) is Hermitian.

The explicit form is
$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi+V(\mathbf{r})\psi.
$$

### Step 4 — Separation of variables
When \(V=V(\mathbf{r})\) only, assume a product solution \(\psi(\mathbf{r},t)=\phi(\mathbf{r})f(t)\). Substitute, divide by \(\phi f\), and obtain two ordinary equations linked by a separation constant \(E\):
$$
\frac{i\hbar}{f}\frac{df}{dt}=E,\qquad\hat{H}\phi=E\phi.
$$

### Step 5 — Time-independent equation
The spatial equation is the eigenvalue problem
$$
\hat{H}\phi_n(\mathbf{r})=E_n\phi_n(\mathbf{r}).
$$
Its solutions \(\phi_n\) are stationary states: \(|\psi|^2=|\phi_n|^2\) is constant in time.

### Step 6 — General solution
Any solution of the time-dependent equation is a linear superposition
$$
\psi(\mathbf{r},t)=\sum_nc_n\phi_n(\mathbf{r})e^{-iE_nt/\hbar},
$$
where the coefficients \(c_n\) are fixed by the initial condition.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Free particle plane wave**
*Given:* \(V=0\), initial wave function \(\psi(x,0)=e^{ikx}\).
*Find:* \(\psi(x,t)\).

The time-dependent equation reduces to
$$
i\hbar\partial_t\psi=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2\psi.
$$
*Why:* \(V=0\) removes the potential term.

Assume \(\psi(x,t)=e^{ikx}f(t)\).  
*Why:* The spatial part already satisfies the free-particle eigenfunction condition.

Substitution yields
$$
i\hbar f'=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}f\implies f(t)=e^{-iE t/\hbar},\quad E=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}.
$$
*Why:* The resulting ODE is first-order linear.

Final answer:
$$
\psi(x,t)=e^{i(kx-\omega t)},\quad\omega=E/\hbar.
$$

*Reflection:* The phase velocity \(\omega/k\) differs from the group velocity \(d\omega/dk\), illustrating that probability moves at the classical particle velocity.

**Example 2 — Infinite square well ground state**
*Given:* \(V=0\) for \(0<x<a\), infinite walls.
*Find:* Lowest-energy stationary state.

The time-independent equation inside the well is
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\phi}{dx^2}=E\phi.
$$
*Why:* \(V=0\) inside.

Boundary conditions \(\phi(0)=\phi(a)=0\) force
$$
\phi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right),\quad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}.
$$
*Why:* Sine functions vanish at both ends and are eigenfunctions of \(d^2/dx^2\).

**Example 3 — Time-dependent superposition**
*Given:* Well of width \(a\), initial state \(\psi(x,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\phi_1+\phi_2)\).
*Find:* \(\psi(x,t)\).

Expand in energy eigenfunctions and attach the phase factors:
$$
\psi(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\phi_1(x)e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}+\phi_2(x)e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\right).
$$
*Why:* Each stationary state evolves independently with its own energy.

Probability density oscillates at frequency \((E_2-E_1)/\hbar\).

**Example 4 — Harmonic oscillator coherent state**
*Given:* \(V=\frac12m\omega^2x^2\), initial coherent state \(|\alpha\rangle\).
*Find:* Time-evolved wave function.

The coherent state is a Poisson superposition of number states \(\phi_n\). Each \(\phi_n\) acquires phase \(e^{-i\omega(n+1/2)t}\). The net result is a Gaussian wave packet whose center follows the classical trajectory \(x(t)=x_0\cos(\omega t)\).

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap | Why it happens | How to avoid it |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| Forgetting \(\hbar\) in the phase factor | Classical intuition uses frequency \(\nu=E/h\) without \(\hbar= h/2\pi\) | Always write \(E_n/\hbar\) explicitly when attaching time phases. |
| Treating the time-independent equation as an approximation | Textbooks often solve only stationary cases first | Remember it is exact for time-independent \(V\); the full solution is the superposition above. |
| Normalizing only the spatial part | Students forget that time phases have modulus 1 | Normalization of \(\psi(\mathbf{r},t)\) is automatic once \(\{\phi_n\}\) are normalized. |
| Using \(\partial_t\psi=E\psi\) instead of \(i\hbar\partial_t\psi=\hat{H}\psi\) | Sign error from classical \(E=i\hbar\partial_t\) | Keep the \(i\) on the left; Hermitian \(\hat{H}\) then guarantees real \(E\). |
| Applying separation of variables when \(V(\mathbf{r},t)\) | Assumption that \(V\) is independent of \(t\) is violated | Check time dependence of the potential before separating. |
| Confusing group velocity with phase velocity | Both appear in the same plane-wave solution | Compute \(d\omega/dk\) for probability transport. |
| Ignoring degeneracy | Multiple \(\phi_n\) can share the same \(E\) | When constructing the general solution, sum over the entire degenerate subspace. |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
For a single spinless particle in a potential \(V(\mathbf{r})\) the time-dependent Schrödinger equation reads
$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\hat{H}\psi,\qquad\hat{H}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V(\mathbf{r}),
\]
where \(\psi\in L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)\) and \(\int|\psi|^2d^3r=1\). When \(V\) is time-independent the stationary states satisfy the eigenvalue problem
$$
\hat{H}\phi_n=E_n\phi_n,
\]
with \(\{\phi_n\}\) forming a complete orthonormal basis (Griffiths, *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics*, 2e, §2.1 and §2.4).

## 9. The memory technique

**The hook**  
Picture a clock whose hands are the real and imaginary parts of \(\psi\); the Hamiltonian is the torque that makes the hands rotate at a speed set by energy.

**What to overlearn**  
1. \(i\hbar\partial_t\psi=\hat{H}\psi\)  
2. Stationary states: \(\psi_n(\mathbf{r},t)=\phi_n(\mathbf{r})e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\)  
3. Superposition principle for the general solution.

**Spaced-repetition schedule**  
Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.

**First-principles fallback**  
Start from probability conservation, insert the operator form of energy, derive the time-dependent equation, then separate variables when \(V\) is static.

## 10. What this unlocks
Mastery of both Schrödinger equations is the gateway to perturbation theory, scattering, identical particles, and relativistic corrections.

- Time-dependent perturbation theory (Fermi’s golden rule)
