## 1. The one-sentence answer
**The extended Euclidean algorithm** finds integers \(x\) and \(y\) such that \(ax + by = d\), where \(d = \gcd(a,b)\).

It begins with the ordinary Euclidean algorithm that repeatedly replaces the larger number by the remainder of division. Each remainder is a linear combination of the two original numbers, though the coefficients remain hidden during the forward pass. By storing the quotients and reversing the steps, those hidden coefficients become explicit.

The process therefore yields both the greatest common divisor and a certificate that the divisor can be expressed exactly as an integer linear combination.

> [!NOTE]
> The single deepest insight is that every remainder generated by the Euclidean algorithm is already an integer linear combination of \(a\) and \(b\); the extended version merely keeps the coefficients visible.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
RSA laboratories and modern cryptographic libraries (OpenSSL, libsodium) rely on the extended Euclidean algorithm to compute modular inverses during key generation; without it, the Chinese-remainder step that reconstructs the private exponent from its value modulo \(p-1\) and \(q-1\) would be impossible.

In aerospace guidance systems, the algorithm supplies the Bézout coefficients used to solve simultaneous Diophantine equations that arise when reconciling clock drifts between GPS satellites whose periods are incommensurate.

Semiconductor place-and-route tools employ the extended Euclidean algorithm inside the computation of the Frobenius coin problem for two coprime feature sizes, guaranteeing that a target wire pitch can be realized exactly with the available metal layers.

Machine-learning frameworks such as PyTorch invoke the algorithm when reducing rational learning-rate schedules to lowest terms before casting them into fixed-point arithmetic on edge TPUs.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Every remainder is a linear combination
Any remainder produced by division is already an integer linear combination of the dividend and divisor.  
Example: \(17 = 5 \cdot 3 + 2\), so \(2 = 17 - 5 \cdot 3\).  
Formally, if \(a = qb + r\) then \(r = a - qb\).

> [!WARNING]
> Treating the remainder as an isolated number rather than as \(a - qb\) erases the only information needed for back-substitution.

### Step 2 — The Euclidean algorithm generates a chain of such relations
Repeated division yields
\[
\begin{align*}
a &= q_1 b + r_1, \\
b &= q_2 r_1 + r_2, \\
r_1 &= q_3 r_2 + r_3, \\
&\vdots \\
r_{k-2} &= q_k r_{k-1} + 0.
\end{align*}
\]
The last non-zero remainder is \(\gcd(a,b)\).

### Step 3 — Each relation can be solved for its remainder
Rearrangement gives
\[
r_1 = a - q_1 b, \quad r_2 = b - q_2 r_1, \quad r_3 = r_1 - q_3 r_2,
\]
and so on. Substituting earlier expressions into later ones propagates the linear-combination property.

### Step 4 — Back-substitution reaches the gcd
After the final non-zero remainder \(r_{k-1}\) appears, substitute upward until \(r_{k-1}\) is written solely in terms of \(a\) and \(b\). The coefficients that emerge are the required \(x\) and \(y\).

### Step 5 — The recursive formulation
If \(d = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)\) and \(d = b x' + (a \bmod b) y'\), then
\[
d = b x' + (a - q b) y' = a y' + b (x' - q y').
\]
Thus the pair \((x,y) = (y', x' - q y')\) solves the original equation.

### Step 6 — Textbook statement
The extended Euclidean algorithm returns integers \(d,x,y\) satisfying \(d = \gcd(a,b)\) and \(ax + by = d\).

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Small coprime pair**  
*Given:* \(a=17\), \(b=13\).  
*Find:* \(x,y\) with \(17x + 13y = 1\).

\[
17 = 1\cdot13 + 4 \qquad 4 = 17 - 1\cdot13
\]
*Why:* First remainder expressed as linear combination.

\[
13 = 3\cdot4 + 1 \qquad 1 = 13 - 3\cdot4
\]
*Why:* Next remainder.

Substitute:
\[
1 = 13 - 3(17-1\cdot13) = 4\cdot13 - 3\cdot17
\]
*Why:* Back-substitution eliminates the intermediate remainder.

**\(17(-3) + 13(4) = 1\)**

*Reflection:* The numbers are small, yet every algebraic cancellation is shown; the pattern of alternating signs already appears.

**Example 2 — Non-coprime pair**  
*Given:* \(a=48\), \(b=18\).  
*Find:* \(x,y\) with \(48x + 18y = 6\).

Euclidean steps:
\[
48=2\cdot18+12,\quad18=1\cdot12+6,\quad12=2\cdot6+0.
\]
Back-substitution:
\[
6=18-1\cdot12=18-1(48-2\cdot18)=3\cdot18-1\cdot48.
\]
**\(48(-1)+18(3)=6\)**

*Reflection:* The gcd is greater than 1; the algorithm still succeeds without extra handling.

**Example 3 — Larger numbers requiring three substitutions**  
*Given:* \(a=240\), \(b=46\).  
*Find:* coefficients for gcd 2.

\[
240=5\cdot46+10,\quad46=4\cdot10+6,\quad10=1\cdot6+4,\quad6=1\cdot4+2,\quad4=2\cdot2+0.
\]
Back-substitution yields
\[
2=240(-5)+46(26).
\]
**\(240(-5)+46(26)=2\)**

*Reflection:* Four divisions produce five substitutions; systematic bookkeeping prevents sign errors.

**Example 4 — One coefficient negative and large in magnitude**  
*Given:* \(a=123\), \(b=54\).  
*Find:* \(x,y\) for gcd 3.

After full back-substitution one obtains
\[
3=123(5)+54(-11).
\]
**\(123(5)+54(-11)=3\)**

*Reflection:* Negative coefficients are routine; the absolute values grow roughly like the quotients, foreshadowing worst-case Fibonacci behaviour.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                                      | How to avoid it                                      |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Forgetting to update both coefficients simultaneously | Treating only one variable at each substitution     | Maintain two running coefficient pairs at every step |
| Sign error during back-substitution | Alternating signs are easy to miscount              | Write the relation \(r_i = r_{i-2}-q_ir_{i-1}\) explicitly each time |
| Stopping at the zero remainder    | Confusing the last non-zero remainder with zero     | Halt when remainder becomes zero; record the previous remainder |
| Assuming \(x\) or \(y\) must be positive | The theorem permits any integers                    | Accept negative values; they are mathematically valid |
| Overflow in mental arithmetic     | Coefficients grow exponentially with input size     | Use a table or matrix formulation on paper           |
| Applying the algorithm to zero    | gcd(a,0)=a, yet the trivial case hides the method   | Handle gcd(a,0) as the base case before recursion    |
| Confusing quotient with remainder | Mixing \(q\) and \(r\) in the same expression       | Label columns clearly: dividend, divisor, quotient, remainder |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let \(a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\) with \(b\neq0\). There exist integers \(x,y\) such that
\[
ax+by=\gcd(a,b).
\]
Moreover, the extended Euclidean algorithm produces one such pair \((x,y)\) in \(O(\log\min(|a|,|b|))\) arithmetic operations. (Rosen, *Elementary Number Theory and Its Applications*, 6e, Theorem 3.7.)

## 8. Visual — diagram or schematic
```text
a ──q1──► b ──q2──► r1 ──q3──► r2 ──q4──► … ──qk──► rk-1 = d
          │         │         │
          ▼         ▼         ▼
       (1,0)     (-q1,1)   (… , …)   ← coefficient pairs (x,y)
```
Each downward arrow records the linear-combination coefficients of the current remainder. The final pair attached to \(d\) solves the Bézout equation.

## 9. The memory technique

1. **The hook** — Picture two rivers merging; every tributary carries a “label” of how much of each original source it contains. The extended algorithm simply keeps the labels attached until the final pool (the gcd) is reached.

2. **What to overlearn** — The update rule \((x,y)\leftarrow(y,x-qy)\) and the fact that the algorithm terminates with the same complexity as the ordinary Euclidean algorithm.

3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review the coefficient-update rule after 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, and 35 days.

4. **First-principles fallback** — Re-derive the substitution identity \(r=a-qb\) from the division algorithm; everything else follows by induction on the number of steps.

## 10. What this unlocks
Mastery of the extended Euclidean algorithm immediately permits the construction of modular inverses, the solution of linear Diophantine equations in two variables, and the proof that \(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\) is a field precisely when \(n\) is prime.

- Chinese Remainder Theorem via explicit inverses  
- RSA key generation and elliptic-curve scalar multiplication  
- Lattice-basis reduction (LLL) that repeatedly calls EEA on coefficient vectors  
- Continued-fraction expansion and best rational approximations
