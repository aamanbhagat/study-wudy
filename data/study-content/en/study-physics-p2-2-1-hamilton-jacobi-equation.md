## 1. The one-sentence answer
**The Hamilton-Jacobi equation is the first-order nonlinear PDE satisfied by the generating function of a canonical transformation that reduces the Hamiltonian to zero.**

In classical mechanics the motion of a system is encoded in Hamilton’s equations, which are first-order but still coupled and generally difficult. A canonical transformation generated by a function *S* can change both coordinates and momenta simultaneously. When *S* is chosen so that the new Hamiltonian vanishes identically, the new coordinates and momenta become constants; their values are read off directly from the initial conditions. The condition that forces the transformed Hamiltonian to zero is precisely the Hamilton-Jacobi equation.

The practical payoff is that solving one PDE replaces the task of integrating a set of ordinary differential equations. Once *S* is known, the original trajectories are recovered by simple differentiations and algebraic inversions.

> [!NOTE]
> The deepest insight is that the Hamilton-Jacobi equation converts dynamics into a problem of *separation of variables* in an extended phase space; every integrable system is one for which that separation succeeds.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
Spacecraft trajectory design at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory still employs the Hamilton-Jacobi formulation of the restricted three-body problem to generate rapid first guesses for low-energy transfers; the generating function supplies the constants that label entire families of halo orbits without numerical integration at every step.

In semiconductor device modeling the WKB approximation to the Schrödinger equation is obtained by identifying the classical action *S* with the eikonal; the Hamilton-Jacobi equation supplies the leading-order phase that determines tunneling probabilities through oxide barriers in modern FinFETs.

High-precision ephemerides for the European Space Agency’s JUICE mission to the Jovian system are constructed by solving the Hamilton-Jacobi equation for the perturbed Kepler problem; the resulting Delaunay variables remain nearly constant over decade-long propagations, reducing CPU time by orders of magnitude compared with direct Cowell integration.

In geometric optics and accelerator physics the same equation governs ray tracing through static magnetic fields; the eikonal *S* yields the Poincaré map that predicts beam stability after thousands of turns without tracking individual particles.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Hamilton’s equations are still coupled
The equations \(\dot q_i = \partial H/\partial p_i\), \(\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q_i\) remain a coupled set of 2n first-order ODEs. Their solution generally requires finding 2n independent integrals.

### Step 2 — Canonical transformations preserve the form of the equations
A transformation \((q,p)\to(Q,P)\) generated by a function *S* keeps the new variables satisfying Hamilton’s equations with a new Hamiltonian *K*. The only change is that *K* may be simpler than *H*.

### Step 3 — Choose a generating function of the first kind
Let \(S(q,P,t)\) be the generating function. The transformation rules are
\[
p_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i},\qquad Q_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial P_i}.
\]
The new Hamiltonian is related to the old one by
\[
K = H + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t}.
\]

### Step 4 — Demand that the new Hamiltonian vanish
If *K* ≡ 0, then \(\dot Q_i = 0\) and \(\dot P_i = 0\). Both *Q* and *P* are therefore constants fixed by initial data. The condition *K* = 0 immediately produces the PDE
\[
H\left(q,\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},t\right) + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = 0.
\]

### Step 5 — The textbook Hamilton-Jacobi equation
The equation obtained in Step 4 is the Hamilton-Jacobi equation. Its solution *S* is called Hamilton’s principal function.

> [!WARNING]
> If the time derivative term is omitted, the equation reduces to the time-independent (energy) case only; trajectories with explicit time dependence are then lost.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Free particle in one dimension**  
*Given:* \(H = p^2/2m\).  
*Find:* *S(q,P,t)*.  

Start with the HJ equation:
\[
\frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}\right)^2 + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = 0.
\]
Assume separability \(S = W(q) - Et\). Then
\[
\frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{dW}{dq}\right)^2 = E \implies W = \pm\sqrt{2mE}\,q.
\]
Thus \(S = \pm\sqrt{2mE}\,q - Et\). Identify the new momentum \(P = E\), yielding
\[
S = \pm q\sqrt{2mP} - Pt.
\]
**Final answer**  
\[S(q,P,t) = \pm q\sqrt{2mP}-Pt.\]  
*Reflection:* The linear dependence on *q* immediately recovers uniform motion; the same pattern appears whenever *H* is independent of *q*.

**Example 2 — Simple harmonic oscillator**  
*Given:* \(H = p^2/2m + \frac12 m\omega^2 q^2\).  
*Find:* *S*.  

The HJ equation reads
\[
\frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}\right)^2 + \frac12 m\omega^2 q^2 + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t}=0.
\]
Separate variables: \(S=W(q)-Et\). The resulting quadrature is
\[
W=\int\sqrt{2mE-m^2\omega^2 q^2}\,dq.
\]
Evaluating the integral produces the familiar arcsin form whose derivative recovers \(p=m\dot q\).

**Final answer**  
\[S=\frac{m\omega}{2}q^2\cot(\omega t+\phi)+const.\]  
*Reflection:* The phase \(\phi\) becomes the constant coordinate *Q*; action-angle variables emerge naturally.

**Example 3 — Kepler problem in polar coordinates**  
*Given:* \(H=p_r^2/2m + p_\theta^2/(2mr^2)-k/r\).  
Separation in *r* and \(\theta\) yields the Delaunay integrals; the radial action integral is expressed as an elliptic integral.

**Example 4 — Time-dependent driven oscillator**  
*Given:* \(H=p^2/2m+\frac12 m\omega^2(t)q^2 - f(t)q\).  
The HJ equation remains solvable by completing the square inside the square root; the resulting *S* contains an extra integral involving the classical particular solution.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap | Why it happens | How to avoid it |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| Treating *S* as the action integral rather than its generator | Confusion between *S* and \(\int L\,dt\) | Remember *S* is a function of the *old* coordinate and the *new* momentum. |
| Forgetting the \(\partial S/\partial t\) term | Mechanical transcription from the time-independent energy equation | Always write the full *K = H + ∂S/∂t* relation first. |
| Assuming separability without checking ignorable coordinates | Over-generalisation from textbook examples | Verify that each coordinate appears in only one term after substitution. |
| Identifying *P* with energy when *H* is time-dependent | *H* is no longer conserved | Use the constant value of the new Hamiltonian (zero) instead. |
| Sign errors in the transformation rules | Mixed conventions for generating-function type | Fix the type (first kind) and keep \(p = \partial S/\partial q\), \(Q = \partial S/\partial P\). |
| Neglecting additive constants or functions of *P* alone | They do not affect the PDE but shift the coordinates | Retain them; they become the new constant coordinates. |
| Solving the PDE numerically without symplectic integrators | Loss of structure that the method was meant to preserve | Use characteristics or canonical perturbation theory instead. |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let *H(q,p,t)* be a Hamiltonian on the extended phase space. A function *S(q,P,t)* generates a canonical transformation via
\[
p_i=\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i},\qquad Q_i=\frac{\partial S}{\partial P_i}.
\]
If *S* satisfies the Hamilton-Jacobi equation
\[
H\left(q,\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},t\right)+\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}=0,
\]
then the transformed Hamiltonian vanishes identically. Consequently *Q* and *P* are constants of the motion. (Goldstein, *Classical Mechanics*, 3e, §10-5.)

The surface *S(q,P,t)* is plotted in the (q,t) plane; its slope \(\partial S/\partial q\) equals the old momentum *p*. Lines of constant *P* are the new coordinate lines; once *S* is known they become straight lines in the extended phase space.

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture *S* as a “magic carpet” whose slope at every point tells you the momentum; when the carpet is flat in the new coordinates the motion is frozen.
2. **What to overlearn** — The transformation rules \(p=\partial S/\partial q\), \(Q=\partial S/\partial P\) and the PDE \(H(q,\partial S/\partial q,t)+\partial S/\partial t=0\).
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review the PDE statement at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.
4. **First-principles fallback** — Re-derive from the canonical condition \(p\,dq-H\,dt = P\,dQ-K\,dt\) by setting *K*=0 and identifying *S* as the generating function.
