## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Two random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent precisely when their joint cumulative distribution function equals the product of the marginal cumulative distribution functions everywhere.**

Independence begins with events: two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent when the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other. Random variables are functions on a probability space, so the same idea lifts by considering the events they generate. The pre-images \(\{X \in A\}\) and \(\{Y \in B\}\) must satisfy the product rule for every pair of Borel sets \(A\) and \(B\).

This requirement is equivalent to the joint law factoring completely. In practice it means that every probabilistic statement about \(X\) alone combines with every probabilistic statement about \(Y\) alone by ordinary multiplication, with no residual dependence term.

> [!NOTE]
> The single most important insight is that independence is a statement about the entire joint distribution, not merely about means or covariances; zero correlation is necessary but far from sufficient.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
In semiconductor yield analysis, TSMC models the threshold voltages of adjacent transistors on the same die as independent only after confirming that spatial correlation falls below a measured threshold; the resulting product distribution determines the probability that an entire SRAM block meets timing specs.

In modern large-language-model training, the AdamW optimiser treats successive gradient estimates as independent across mini-batches once the learning-rate schedule and data shuffling have removed detectable autocorrelation; the independence assumption justifies the separate exponential moving-average recursions for first and second moments.

NASA’s Deep Space Network uses independent fading models for Ka-band and X-band signals when a probe is occulted by a planet’s atmosphere; the product of the two marginal reliability functions determines the joint probability that at least one band maintains lock during critical manoeuvres.

Particle physicists at CERN treat the decay times of two distinct B-mesons produced in the same LHCb event as independent once the production vertex is fixed; the factorisation simplifies the unbinned likelihood used to extract the CP-violation parameter \(\sin 2\beta\).

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Independence of events
Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent when learning that one occurred supplies no new information about the other.  
Concrete example: the event “first coin lands heads” and the event “second coin lands heads” on two separate fair coins.  
Formally,
\[
P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B).
\]
> [!WARNING]
> Replacing the intersection by the union produces an immediate numerical error; the union formula contains an extra subtraction term.

### Step 2 — \(\sigma\)-algebras generated by random variables
Each random variable \(X\) induces the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\sigma(X) = \{X^{-1}(B) : B \text{ Borel}\}\). Independence of the random variables is defined by requiring every pair of sets, one from each \(\sigma\)-algebra, to be independent events.

### Step 3 — Joint versus product measure
The joint probability measure \(\mu_{X,Y}\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) must coincide with the product measure \(\mu_X \otimes \mu_Y\) on the product \(\sigma\)-algebra.

### Step 4 — Cumulative distribution functions
Evaluating the measures on rectangles \((-\infty,x] \times (-\infty,y]\) yields the classical functional statement
\[
F_{X,Y}(x,y) = F_X(x)F_Y(y) \qquad \forall x,y \in \mathbb{R}.
\]

### Step 5 — Densities when they exist
If a joint density \(f_{X,Y}\) exists, the product rule on the CDF implies
\[
f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x)f_Y(y)
\]
almost everywhere with respect to Lebesgue measure.

### Step 6 — Textbook definition via CDFs
The random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if and only if the displayed equality in Step 4 holds for every real pair \((x,y)\).

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Two independent fair coins**  
*Given:* Let \(X = 1\) if coin 1 is heads, \(0\) otherwise; \(Y\) defined analogously for coin 2. Both coins fair and physically separate.  
*Find:* Verify independence via the CDF definition.  
\(P(X \le 0, Y \le 0) = P(X=0,Y=0) = \frac14\).  
*Why:* Only the outcome TT contributes.  
\(P(X \le 0) = \frac12\), \(P(Y \le 0) = \frac12\).  
*Why:* Marginal probabilities each ignore the other coin.  
\(\frac12 \cdot \frac12 = \frac14\), which matches the joint.  
**Final answer:** The equality holds for all four corners of the unit square; hence \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent.  
*Reflection:* The discrete uniform joint table is exactly the outer product of the marginal vectors.

**Example 2 — Continuous uniform on the unit square**  
*Given:* Joint density \(f(x,y) = 1\) for \((x,y) \in [0,1]^2\).  
*Find:* Check independence.  
Marginal \(f_X(x) = \int_0^1 1\, dy = 1\) on \([0,1]\).  
*Why:* Integrating out \(y\) leaves a constant.  
The product \(f_X(x)f_Y(y) = 1\) recovers the joint density.  
**Final answer:** \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent.  
*Reflection:* The rectangular support is essential; any triangular support immediately destroys the product structure.

**Example 3 — Sum and difference of normals**  
*Given:* \(X,Y\) i.i.d. standard normal. Let \(U = X+Y\), \(V = X-Y\).  
*Find:* Are \(U\) and \(V\) independent?  
Joint density of \((U,V)\) is obtained by the linear transformation with Jacobian \(1/2\).  
The resulting exponent is \(-\frac{u^2+v^2}{4}\), which factors as a product of a function of \(u\) and a function of \(v\).  
**Final answer:** Yes, \(U\) and \(V\) are independent (both \(\mathcal{N}(0,2)\)).  
*Reflection:* Orthogonality of the transformation matrix plus normality together produce independence.

**Example 4 — Non-independent case**  
*Given:* Joint density \(f(x,y) = 2\) on the triangle \(0 < x < y < 1\).  
*Find:* Check the product of marginals.  
\(f_X(x) = 2(1-x)\) on \([0,1]\).  
\(f_Y(y) = 2y\) on \([0,1]\).  
Product \(4(1-x)y\) differs from the constant 2 on the triangle.  
**Final answer:** Not independent.  
*Reflection:* The triangular support forces \(X < Y\) almost surely, violating factorisation.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap | Why it happens | How to avoid it |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| Confusing “uncorrelated” with “independent” | Covariance only probes the mixed second moment | Always test the full CDF or density factorisation |
| Assuming independence of functions of independent variables | True only for measurable functions of each separately | Verify the \(\sigma\)-algebras remain independent |
| Forgetting that independence must hold for all Borel sets | Checking only intervals or quadrants feels sufficient | Use the monotone-class theorem or generator arguments |
| Treating discrete and continuous cases with different intuitions | The same product measure definition applies to both | Write the CDF statement first in every problem |
| Believing that zero mutual information implies independence | Mutual information zero is equivalent, but harder to check | Stay with the CDF definition for proofs |
| Extending pairwise independence to mutual independence | Pairwise does not control triple intersections | Check the full joint against the product of all marginals |
| Ignoring atoms when using densities | Continuous formulas miss point masses | Always begin with the measure-theoretic statement |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)\) be a probability space and let \(X,Y:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\) be random variables. Then \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if and only if
\[
P(X\in B_1,Y\in B_2)=P(X\in B_1)P(Y\in B_2)
\]
for every pair of Borel sets \(B_1,B_2\subset\mathbb{R}\). Equivalently,
\[
F_{X,Y}(x,y)=F_X(x)F_Y(y)\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}.
\]
(Durrett, *Probability: Theory and Examples*, 5th ed., §1.4, Definition 1.4.3.)

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture two perfectly isolated rooms: whatever happens inside one room has zero effect on the probability of any event inside the other; the walls are the \(\sigma\)-algebras.  
2. **What to overlearn** — The single equality \(F_{X,Y}(x,y)=F_X(x)F_Y(y)\) for all real \(x,y\).  
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.  
4. **First-principles fallback** — Start from the definition of independent events, form the generated \(\sigma\)-algebras, then restrict to rectangles to recover the CDF statement.

## 10. What this unlocks
Independence is the gateway to almost every limit theorem and to the clean separation of conditional expectations.  
- Weak and strong laws of large numbers for i.i.d. sequences  
- Central limit theorem  
- Kolmogorov’s zero-one law  
- Factorisation of joint moment-generating functions  
- Simplification of conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]\)  
- Product measures on infinite product spaces
