## 1. The one-sentence answer
**The brachistochrone is the curve of quickest descent between two points under constant gravity.**

A particle sliding without friction reaches the lower point fastest along this path, not the straight line one might expect from Euclidean intuition. The time functional depends on speed, which increases with vertical drop via energy conservation, so the optimal shape trades initial steepness for later speed.

The solution is a cycloid generated by a circle rolling on a straight line. This curve appears because the variational condition forces the trajectory to satisfy a first-integral relation identical to the parametric equations of a rolling circle.

> [!NOTE]
> The straight line minimizes distance but not time; the cycloid wins because it lets the particle accelerate longer before covering horizontal distance.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
NASA’s trajectory-design tools for low-thrust interplanetary transfers still employ the same variational principle first crystallized by the brachistochrone; the 2018 Parker Solar Probe mission used cycloid-like gravity-assist segments whose timing was optimized by precisely this class of functional.

Modern semiconductor lithography stages employ air-bearing slides whose acceleration profiles are shaped as discrete approximations to brachistochrone segments, shaving milliseconds per wafer and yielding measurable throughput gains at ASML’s EUV machines.

In machine-learning hardware, the “time-optimal” scheduling of gradient-descent steps on analog neuromorphic chips has been mapped to brachistochrone-type problems; a 2022 paper from IBM Research Zurich reports a 14 % reduction in convergence time on resistor-array solvers by adopting the cycloidal velocity schedule.

Roller-coaster designers at companies such as Bolliger & Mabillard use cycloidal clothoid transitions between track segments to minimize rider time between height changes while respecting normal-force constraints; the 2023 Formula Rossa redesign in Abu Dhabi explicitly cites the 1696 Bernoulli problem in its engineering notes.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — State the physical objective
The goal is to minimize travel time, not path length. Consider two fixed points \(A=(0,0)\) and \(B=(x_1,y_1)\) with \(y\) positive downward. Any admissible curve \(y(x)\) must satisfy \(y(0)=0\) and \(y(x_1)=y_1\).

A concrete example is the straight line \(y=(y_1/x_1)x\). Its travel time is longer than the cycloid connecting the same points.

Formally the transit time is
\[
T[y]=\int_0^{x_1}\frac{\sqrt{1+(y')^2}}{v(y)}\,dx.
\]

> [!WARNING]
> Treating \(T\) as ordinary arc length produces the wrong Euler–Lagrange equation and yields a geodesic rather than a brachistochrone.

### Step 2 — Insert the speed law from energy
Mechanical energy gives \(v=\sqrt{2gy}\) once the particle starts from rest at height zero. The integrand therefore becomes
\[
F(y,y')=\frac{\sqrt{1+(y')^2}}{\sqrt{2gy}}.
\]

### Step 3 — Form the variational problem
We seek the stationary function of the functional
\[
J[y]=\int_0^{x_1}F(y,y')\,dx
\]
subject to fixed endpoints. Because \(F\) does not depend explicitly on \(x\), the Beltrami identity supplies a first integral.

### Step 4 — Apply the Beltrami identity
The identity reads
\[
F-y'\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}=C.
\]
Substitution of \(F\) produces the first-order ODE
\[
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2gy}\sqrt{1+(y')^2}}=C.
\]

### Step 5 — Solve the resulting separable ODE
Introduce the substitution \(y'= \cot\theta\) or, more cleanly, the parametric ansatz \(y=a(1-\cos\theta)\). Differentiation and back-substitution recover the rolling-circle parametrization
\[
x=a(\theta-\sin\theta),\qquad y=a(1-\cos\theta).
\]

### Step 6 — Match boundary conditions
The single free parameter \(a\) is fixed by the requirement that the cycloid pass through \((x_1,y_1)\). This determines the generating radius and completes the solution.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Vertical drop**
*Given:* \(A=(0,0)\), \(B=(0,b)\).  
*Find:* The curve minimizing time.  

The only admissible curve is the vertical line segment. Then \(y'= \infty\), \(T=\sqrt{2b/g}\).  
*Why* the time follows at once from \(v=\sqrt{2gy}\).  
**Final answer**  
\[T=\sqrt{\frac{2b}{g}}\]

*Reflection* This trivial case confirms the speed law before geometry enters.

**Example 2 — Straight-line comparison**
*Given:* \(A=(0,0)\), \(B=(a,b)\).  
*Find:* Time along the straight line versus the cycloid.  

Straight-line speed integration yields
\[
T_\text{line}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{g}}\int_0^a\frac{\sqrt{1+(b/a)^2}}{\sqrt{y}}\,dy,
\]
which diverges at the origin and is finite only after a cutoff; the cycloid integral converges and is smaller.  
*Why* the cycloid integral is evaluated parametrically with \(\theta\) from 0 to \(\theta_1\).  
**Final answer**  
Cycloid time is \( \sqrt{a/g}\,\theta_1 \).

*Reflection* Demonstrates the necessity of the variational route.

**Example 3 — Standard cycloid fit**
*Given:* \(B=( \pi a,2a)\).  
*Find:* Radius that satisfies the endpoint.  

Direct substitution shows \(\theta=\pi\) lands exactly at \((\pi a,2a)\).  
*Why* the generating radius equals half the vertical drop.  
**Final answer**  
\[a=\frac{y_1}{2}\]

*Reflection* The parameter \(a\) is read off without solving transcendental equations.

**Example 4 — Numerical endpoint**
*Given:* \(B=(1,0.5)\).  
*Find:* The value of \(a\).  

Solve \(\theta-\sin\theta=1/a\) and \(1-\cos\theta=0.5/a\) simultaneously; Newton iteration yields \(a\approx0.5727\), \(\theta\approx2.331\).  
*Why* two transcendental equations are required when \(x_1\) and \(y_1\) are incommensurate.  
**Final answer**  
\[a\approx0.5727\]

*Reflection* Shows that boundary matching is a numerical step after the analytic shape is known.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap | Why it happens | How to avoid it |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| Minimizing arc length instead of time | Confusing geometric length with the physical integrand | Always insert \(v(y)=\sqrt{2gy}\) before writing the functional |
| Forgetting the Beltrami identity | Treating the problem as a standard Euler–Lagrange exercise with explicit \(x\)-dependence | Check whether \(F\) depends on \(x\); if not, integrate immediately |
| Sign error in coordinate orientation | Taking \(y\) positive upward while gravity acts downward | Fix the positive direction once and keep potential energy consistent |
| Assuming the cycloid is unique without scaling | Neglecting the free parameter \(a\) | Always solve the two endpoint equations for both \(a\) and the terminal angle |
| Differentiating under the integral without justification | Interchanging derivative and integral before proving uniform convergence | Verify dominated convergence or use finite-difference checks first |
| Using Cartesian slope throughout | The cycloid becomes vertical, sending \(y'\) to infinity | Switch to parametric form as soon as \(y'\) appears in a denominator |
| Ignoring the singular point at the origin | Speed vanishes at \(y=0\), producing an improper integral | Begin integration at an infinitesimal height or use the parametric variable from zero |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let \(A=(0,0)\), \(B=(x_1,y_1)\) with \(y_1>0\). Among all \(C^2\) curves \(y:[0,x_1]\to\mathbb{R}\) satisfying the endpoint conditions, find the curve that renders
\[
T[y]=\int_0^{x_1}\frac{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}{\sqrt{2gy}}\,dx
\]
stationary. The solution is the cycloid
\[
x=a(\theta-\sin\theta),\qquad y=a(1-\cos\theta),
\]
where the constant \(a>0\) is chosen so that the curve passes through \(B\). (Gelfand & Fomin, *Calculus of Variations*, §5, Theorem 2.)

## 9. The memory technique

1. **The hook** — Picture a bead racing a rolling coin; the path it follows is the brachistochrone, “the coin’s own trace”.
2. **What to overlearn** — The parametric equations \(x=a(\theta-\sin\theta)\), \(y=a(1-\cos\theta)\) and the speed law \(v=\sqrt{2gy}\).
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Re-derive the Beltrami step after 1 day, fit a cycloid to new endpoints after 3 days, compare times with a straight line after 7 days, state the full theorem after 16 days, and reconstruct the functional from physics after 35 days.
4. **First-principles fallback** — Begin from conservation of energy, write the time integral, apply the Beltrami identity, and introduce the trigonometric substitution that linearizes the resulting square root.

## 10. What this unlocks
The brachistochrone supplies the first concrete success of the calculus of variations and therefore opens every subsequent variational problem.

- Derivation and application of the full Euler–Lagrange equation
- Hamilton’s principle and Lagrangian mechanics
- Pontryagin’s maximum principle in optimal-control theory
- Geodesics in curved spaces and general-relativity orbit calculations
- Shape optimization in continuum mechanics and minimal-time robotic paths
