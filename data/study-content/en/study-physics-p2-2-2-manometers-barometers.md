## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Manometers and barometers measure pressure by balancing it against the hydrostatic weight of a static liquid column.**

Pressure at any depth in a connected fluid body is set solely by the vertical height of fluid above that point. A barometer seals one end of a liquid column against vacuum and reads atmospheric pressure directly from the supported height. A manometer leaves both ends open or connects them to two different pressures and converts the observed height difference into a pressure difference. The same underlying relation therefore governs both instruments.

The liquid must be incompressible and at rest; any motion or dissolved gas invalidates the simple height-to-pressure conversion. Because the free surface is exposed to a known pressure (usually atmosphere or vacuum), the pressure change with depth follows immediately from vertical equilibrium.

> [!NOTE]
> The single decisive insight is that only the vertical height difference matters; the shape, length, or inclination of the connecting tube never enters the pressure calculation.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
SpaceX uses differential manometers on the propellant feed lines of Falcon 9 to verify that tank ullage pressures remain within 0.2 % of the commanded values before engine ignition; a misread column height of only 3 mm of RP-1 would produce an unacceptable mixture-ratio shift.

Commercial weather stations still rely on mercury barometers as primary standards; the WMO requires periodic recalibration against a Fortin barometer whose column height is measured to 0.05 mm, corresponding to a pressure uncertainty of 0.07 hPa.

Aircraft cabin-pressure controllers on the Boeing 787 employ silicon-membrane manometers whose output is cross-checked against a redundant aneroid capsule; the cross-check algorithm treats the capsule height as an independent barometric measurement to detect sensor drift above 40 000 ft.

Semiconductor etch tools at TSMC maintain chamber pressures between 5 and 200 mTorr using capacitance manometers; these instruments are themselves calibrated against a spinning-rotor gauge whose reference pressure is generated by a mercury U-tube manometer at the national standards laboratory.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Pressure increases with depth
In any stationary liquid the weight of the fluid above a horizontal surface must be supported by the pressure difference across that surface.  
Consider a 1 m cube of water: its weight is \(\rho g \times 1\,\text{m}^3\). This weight is distributed over the 1 m² base, producing an extra pressure of \(\rho g\) at the bottom.  
$$P_2 - P_1 = \rho g h$$  
> [!WARNING]
> Replacing the vertical height \(h\) with the slant length of an inclined tube produces an immediate error whose magnitude grows with the sine of the tilt angle.

### Step 2 — The free surface sets the reference pressure
At the liquid–gas interface the pressure is fixed by the gas above it. In a barometer the gas above the column is essentially vacuum, so the reference pressure is zero.  
The pressure at depth \(h\) is therefore exactly the weight per unit area of the column:  
$$P = \rho g h$$  
> [!WARNING]
> Treating the meniscus surface tension as a pressure offset of a few pascals is harmless for water but becomes a 0.3 % error for mercury columns under 100 mm.

### Step 3 — Manometer reads a pressure difference
When both ends of a U-tube are exposed to different pressures \(P_A\) and \(P_B\), the liquid displaces until the hydrostatic pressure difference exactly cancels the applied difference.  
Equating pressures at the lower horizontal level yields  
$$P_A - P_B = \rho g \Delta h$$  
> [!WARNING]
> Forgetting that the higher liquid surface lies on the side of the lower pressure reverses the sign of \(\Delta h\) and produces a measurement that is wrong by twice the true value.

### Step 4 — Different liquids require density weighting
If the manometer fluid differs from the process fluid, the pressure balance must be written separately in each leg. The interface height cancels, leaving only the manometer-fluid column.  
$$P_A - P_B = \rho_m g \Delta h$$  
where \(\rho_m\) is the manometer density.

### Step 5 — Inclined manometer multiplies resolution
Tilting one leg lengthens the visible scale distance for a given vertical height change. The vertical component remains \(\Delta h = L \sin\theta\), so the pressure sensitivity improves by \(1/\sin\theta\) while the governing equation is unchanged.

### Step 6 — Textbook statement of the hydrostatic result
For an incompressible fluid at rest the pressure gradient is  
$$\nabla P = \rho \mathbf{g}$$  
Integration along any vertical path recovers the manometer and barometer formulae above.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Simple mercury barometer**  
*Given:* A mercury column stands 752.3 mm above the reservoir surface; \(\rho_{\text{Hg}} = 13595.1\,\text{kg m}^{-3}\), \(g = 9.80665\,\text{m s}^{-2}\).  
*Find:* Atmospheric pressure in pascals.  

Convert height to metres: \(h = 0.7523\,\text{m}\).  
Multiply by density and \(g\):  
$$P = 13595.1 \times 9.80665 \times 0.7523 = 100\,560\,\text{Pa}.$$  
*Why:* Each factor converts the geometric height into force per unit area.  
**100560 Pa**

*Reflection:* The only arithmetic risk is unit conversion; once height is in metres the rest is direct multiplication.

**Example 2 — U-tube manometer, same fluid**  
*Given:* Water manometer shows 340 mm difference; both legs open to air except one connected to a tank.  
*Find:* Gauge pressure in the tank.  

Apply the manometer equation directly:  
$$P_{\text{gauge}} = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 0.340 = 3335.4\,\text{Pa}.$$  
*Why:* The open leg supplies atmospheric reference, leaving only the gauge contribution.  
**3335 Pa (gauge)**

*Reflection:* Sign error is avoided by always placing the higher liquid surface on the low-pressure side.

**Example 3 — Two-fluid manometer**  
*Given:* An oil–water manometer has a 120 mm water column and a 180 mm oil column (\(\rho_{\text{oil}} = 850\,\text{kg m}^{-3}\)).  
*Find:* Pressure difference.  

Pressure balance at interface level:  
$$P_A - P_B = \rho_w g (0.120) - \rho_o g (0.180).$$  
Substitute values:  
$$P_A - P_B = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 0.120 - 850 \times 9.81 \times 0.180 = 328\,\text{Pa}.$$  
*Why:* Each leg contributes its own \(\rho g h\) term; the interface height cancels.  
**328 Pa**

*Reflection:* The subtraction appears only because the oil column opposes the water column; reversing the geometry would add the terms.

**Example 4 — Inclined manometer**  
*Given:* An inclined manometer at 30° shows a reading \(L = 450\,\text{mm}\) along the tube; fluid is red oil, \(\rho = 810\,\text{kg m}^{-3}\).  
*Find:* Pressure difference.  

Vertical height: \(\Delta h = L \sin 30^\circ = 0.450 \times 0.5 = 0.225\,\text{m}\).  
Pressure:  
$$P_A - P_B = 810 \times 9.81 \times 0.225 = 1789\,\text{Pa}.$$  
*Why:* Only the vertical component produces hydrostatic pressure.  
**1789 Pa**

*Reflection:* The sine factor improves readability but must be restored before the final multiplication.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                              | How to avoid it                              |
|-----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Using slant length instead of vertical height | Tube geometry is visually dominant          | Always compute \(h = L\sin\theta\) first     |
| Ignoring temperature effect on density | Density tables are given at 20 °C           | Apply \(\rho(T)\) correction or thermostat the instrument |
| Reading the wrong meniscus surface | Parallax or capillary rise misidentified    | Sight along the flat mercury surface or add 0.3 mm correction for water |
| Sign reversal of \(\Delta h\)     | Left–right confusion in U-tube sketch       | Sketch the higher liquid level on the low-pressure side before writing the equation |
| Treating gauge pressure as absolute | Forgetting the open leg is atmospheric      | Explicitly label every pressure as absolute or gauge |
| Neglecting surface tension in small tubes | Capillary rise becomes comparable to \(\Delta h\) | Use tubes > 8 mm diameter or apply Young–Laplace correction |
| Unit mismatch (mmHg vs Pa)        | Mixed legacy units in lab notebooks         | Convert to pascals immediately after reading |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
In an incompressible fluid of constant density \(\rho\) that is motionless in a uniform gravitational field \(\mathbf{g}\), the pressure satisfies  
$$\frac{dP}{dz} = -\rho g$$  
where \(z\) increases upward. Integration between two points connected by a continuous liquid column yields  
$$P_2 - P_1 = \rho g (z_1 - z_2).$$  
When point 1 lies at a free surface exposed to pressure \(P_{\text{ref}}\) and point 2 lies a vertical distance \(h\) below it, the manometer or barometer equation follows at once. (White, *Fluid Mechanics*, 8e, §2.3.)

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture a tall mercury “thermometer” whose “temperature” is actually atmospheric pressure; the vacuum at the top is the ultimate low-pressure reference.  
2. **What to overlearn** — \(P = \rho g h\) for barometers and \(\Delta P = \rho g \Delta h\) for manometers; density of mercury at 0 °C = 13595 kg m^{-3}.  
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review the two equations at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days after first mastery.  
4. **First-principles fallback** — Start from vertical force balance on a fluid element: weight equals net pressure force; integrate \(dP = -\rho g\, dz\).

## 10. What this unlocks
Mastery of hydrostatic columns supplies the reference pressures needed for every subsequent instrument and for the derivation of buoyancy, Archimedes’ principle, and the Bernoulli equation along a streamline.  
- Pitot-static tubes  
- Hydraulic jack force multiplication  
- Stability of floating bodies  
- Derivation of the speed of sound in an isothermal atmosphere
