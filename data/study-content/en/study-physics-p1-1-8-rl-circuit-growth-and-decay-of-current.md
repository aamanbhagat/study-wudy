## 1. The one-sentence answer
**An RL circuit is a series combination of resistance R and inductance L driven by a DC source; the inductor forces the current to rise or fall exponentially with time constant τ = L/R rather than instantly.**

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field and therefore resists any instantaneous change in current. When a voltage is first applied, the back emf generated by the inductor starts at the full supply voltage and falls to zero, allowing the current to climb smoothly from zero toward its steady value E/R. When the source is removed and the circuit is closed through the resistor alone, the stored magnetic energy dissipates resistively and the current decays exponentially to zero.

The governing equation is obtained from Kirchhoff’s voltage law. The sum of the resistive drop and the inductive term must equal the applied voltage, producing a first-order linear differential equation whose solution is the familiar exponential form.

> [!NOTE]
> The current never jumps; only the voltage across the inductor can change discontinuously. This single fact explains every transient waveform in an RL circuit.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
In reusable rocket engines, fast-acting solenoid valves that meter cryogenic propellants are driven by RL circuits; the L/R time constant sets the minimum valve opening time and therefore the smallest impulse bit that can be delivered for precise attitude control on vehicles such as SpaceX’s SuperDraco thrusters.

Power-electronics modules inside satellite power-conditioning units use RL snubbers to limit di/dt during MOSFET turn-off; incorrect sizing of L or R produces voltage spikes that have destroyed flight hardware on multiple GEO communications satellites.

Particle-accelerator beam-dump kickers at CERN employ high-current RL pulse-forming networks; the exponential rise of current through the kicker magnets must be known to sub-microsecond accuracy to avoid beam loss that could damage downstream superconducting magnets.

Electric-motor controllers in electric vertical-take-off aircraft (eVTOL) prototypes rely on RL motor windings whose electrical time constants interact with the mechanical inertia; miscalculation of τ produces torque ripple that excites airframe resonances and has caused flight-test aborts.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Inductors oppose changes in current
An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field; any attempt to alter the current instantly would require infinite power.  
Consider a 10 mH inductor carrying 2 A. If the current were forced to zero in 1 µs the required voltage would exceed 20 kV.  
The defining relation is  
$$V_L = L\frac{di}{dt}.$$  
> [!WARNING] Treating the inductor voltage as zero during transients produces the false conclusion that current can jump discontinuously.

### Step 2 — Write the loop equation for growth
Close a switch connecting a battery E, resistor R, and inductor L in series at t = 0.  
Kirchhoff’s voltage law around the single loop yields  
$$E = Ri + L\frac{di}{dt}.$$  
> [!WARNING] Reversing the sign of the inductive term inverts the direction of the transient and yields an unphysical growing exponential.

### Step 3 — Rearrange into standard form
Collect terms:  
$$L\frac{di}{dt} + Ri = E.$$  
Divide by L:  
$$\frac{di}{dt} + \frac{R}{L}i = \frac{E}{L}.$$  
This is now a first-order linear differential equation with constant coefficients.

### Step 4 — Solve the homogeneous equation
The homogeneous equation is  
$$\frac{di}{dt} + \frac{R}{L}i = 0.$$  
Its solution is the decaying exponential  
$$i_h(t) = Ae^{-(R/L)t}.$$

### Step 5 — Find a particular solution and apply initial conditions
A constant particular solution i_p = E/R satisfies the non-homogeneous equation.  
The general solution is therefore  
$$i(t) = \frac{E}{R} + Ae^{-(R/L)t}.$$  
At t = 0 the current is zero, fixing A = −E/R and producing  
$$i(t) = \frac{E}{R}(1 - e^{-(R/L)t}).$$

### Step 6 — Treat the decay case
Open the battery and short the RL series combination at t = 0 with initial current I_0.  
The equation becomes  
$$L\frac{di}{dt} + Ri = 0,$$  
with solution  
$$i(t) = I_0e^{-(R/L)t}.$$  
The same time constant governs both growth and decay.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Basic growth**  
*Given:* E = 12 V, R = 4 Ω, L = 2 H, switch closed at t = 0.  
*Find:* i(0.5 s).  
The governing expression is  
$$i(t) = \frac{E}{R}(1 - e^{-(R/L)t}).$$  
Substitute values:  
$$i(0.5) = \frac{12}{4}(1 - e^{-(4/2)(0.5)}) = 3(1 - e^{-1}).$$  
Numerically, e^{-1} ≈ 0.3679, so  
$$i(0.5) = 3(0.6321) = 1.896\,\text{A}.$$  
**1.896 A**  
*Reflection:* The only algebraic step that can be missed is confirming the initial condition i(0) = 0 before applying the formula.

**Example 2 — Time to reach a fraction of final current**  
*Given:* Same circuit; find t when i = 2 A (2/3 of steady value).  
Set  
$$\frac{2}{3} = 1 - e^{-(R/L)t}.$$  
Solve:  
$$e^{-2t} = \frac{1}{3} \implies -2t = \ln(1/3) \implies t = \frac{\ln 3}{2} = 0.5493\,\text{s}.$$  
**0.549 s**  
*Reflection:* Taking the natural log is the step students most often forget to justify.

**Example 3 — Decay from initial current**  
*Given:* After steady state the battery is removed; initial current is 3 A. Find i(0.3 s).  
$$i(t) = 3e^{-2t}.$$  
At t = 0.3 s:  
$$i(0.3) = 3e^{-0.6} \approx 3 \times 0.5488 = 1.646\,\text{A}.$$  
**1.646 A**  
*Reflection:* The decay formula re-uses the identical time constant; sign errors arise only when the circuit topology is misread.

**Example 4 — Energy dissipated during decay**  
*Given:* Same decay; compute total energy dissipated in R from t = 0 to ∞.  
Instantaneous power is i²R; integrate:  
$$\int_0^\infty I_0^2e^{-2(R/L)t}R\,dt = \frac{1}{2}LI_0^2.$$  
Substitute I_0 = 3 A, L = 2 H yields 9 J.  
**9 J**  
*Reflection:* The integral recovers exactly the magnetic energy stored at t = 0, confirming energy conservation.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                              | How to avoid it                                      |
|-----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Using i = E/R at t = 0+           | Confusing inductor with resistor            | Always enforce i(0) = 0 for growth problems          |
| Sign error in L di/dt             | Ambiguous loop direction                    | Fix the direction of positive current once and check voltage polarity |
| Treating τ as RC instead of L/R   | Mixing capacitor and inductor formulas      | Write the differential equation before quoting τ     |
| Forgetting steady-state value     | Jumping straight to transient term          | Solve the particular integral before adding homogeneous solution |
| Applying growth formula to decay  | Not redrawing circuit after switch opens    | Redraw the loop and write a new KVL equation         |
| Ignoring internal resistance of L | Real inductors have series R                | Measure or include winding resistance in total R     |
| Differentiating the exponential incorrectly | Chain-rule slip                        | Verify d/dt [e^{-αt}] = −α e^{-αt} each time         |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
For a series RL circuit with constant voltage E applied at t = 0 and zero initial current, the current is  
$$i(t) = \frac{E}{R}(1 - e^{-(R/L)t}),\quad t\geq 0.$$  
When the source is removed at t = 0 with initial current I_0 the current becomes  
$$i(t) = I_0e^{-(R/L)t}.$$  
Both expressions assume ideal linear elements and neglect radiation and core losses. (Halliday, Resnick & Walker, *Fundamentals of Physics*, 12e, §30-7.)

## 9. The memory technique

**The hook**  
Picture an inductor as a heavy flywheel bolted to the current; resistance is the friction that eventually stops it. The flywheel cannot start or stop spinning instantly.

**What to overlearn**  
- τ = L/R (seconds)  
- Growth: i(t) = (E/R)(1 − e^{−t/τ})  
- Decay: i(t) = I_0 e^{−t/τ}

**Spaced-repetition schedule**  
Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.

**First-principles fallback**  
Start from V_L = L di/dt, write KVL, rearrange to di/dt + (R/L)i = constant, solve the linear first-order DE.
