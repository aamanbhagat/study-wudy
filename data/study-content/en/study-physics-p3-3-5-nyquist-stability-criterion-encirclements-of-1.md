## 1. The one-sentence answer
**The Nyquist stability criterion counts clockwise encirclements of the point −1 + j0 by the polar plot of the open-loop transfer function G(jω)H(jω) to determine the number of unstable closed-loop poles.**

The plot is generated by sweeping frequency ω from −∞ to +∞ along the imaginary axis of the s-plane. Each time the curve winds once clockwise around −1, it registers one net encirclement. Stability follows once the total encirclements N satisfy N = P, where P is the number of open-loop poles already in the right half-plane; any mismatch produces closed-loop poles with positive real parts.

The geometry works because the argument principle maps the change in angle of 1 + G(s)H(s) along the D-contour directly into the number of zeros minus poles inside that contour. The −1 point is simply the image of the origin under the map 1 + G H.

> [!NOTE]
> The decisive insight is that stability is decided entirely by how the open-loop frequency response winds around a single fixed point in the complex plane; no root solving is required.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
SpaceX uses Nyquist plots of thrust-vector-control loops on Falcon 9 to certify that the vehicle remains stable when engine-gimbal dynamics and slosh modes are included; a single missed clockwise encirclement of −1 would have produced an unstable pitch channel during ascent.

NASA’s Europa Clipper mission applied the same test to the reaction-wheel and thruster attitude-control loops after radiation-induced shifts in sensor gain; the criterion revealed that an extra right-half-plane pole in the star-tracker transfer function required an additional counterclockwise encirclement to keep all closed-loop poles in the left half-plane.

In semiconductor lithography, ASML’s wafer-stage servo systems employ Nyquist analysis on the 6-DOF magnetic-levitation controllers; encirclement counts are checked after every firmware update because a single unstable mode at 2 kHz produces nanometer-scale image blur.

Modern reusable launch-vehicle landing legs incorporate actively damped hydraulic struts whose valve amplifiers are tuned via Nyquist; Blue Origin’s New Shepard flights log the open-loop plots in real time so that any thermal drift pushing the plot across −1 is flagged before the next hop.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Map the imaginary axis
The s-plane contour that encloses the entire right half-plane is deformed into the jω axis plus a semicircle of infinite radius. Substituting s = jω into the open-loop transfer function traces a curve in the G H-plane.  
Example: for G(s) = 1/(s+1), the curve is a semicircle of radius 1/2 centered at 0.5.  
Formal statement:  
$$ \Gamma_{GH} = G(j\omega)H(j\omega),\quad \omega \in (-\infty,+\infty). $$  
> [!WARNING]  
> Omitting the negative-frequency mirror image produces an incomplete contour and therefore an incorrect encirclement count.

### Step 2 — Locate the critical point
The closed-loop characteristic equation is 1 + G(s)H(s) = 0, or G(s)H(s) = −1. Thus every time the Nyquist curve passes through or around −1, the map 1 + GH passes through the origin.  
Formal statement: the point −1 + j0 is the image of the origin under the transformation 1 + z.

### Step 3 — Apply the argument principle
The net change in argument of 1 + GH along the D-contour equals 2π(Z − P), where Z and P are zeros and poles of 1 + GH inside the contour. Each full clockwise encirclement of −1 by GH contributes −2π to the argument change.  
Formal statement:  
$$ N = P - Z, \quad N = \text{clockwise encirclements of } -1. $$

### Step 4 — Close the contour at infinity
For systems where |G H| → 0 as |s| → ∞, the infinite semicircle maps to the origin; no additional encirclements are contributed. When degree of denominator exceeds numerator by at least two, the plot ends at the origin.

### Step 5 — State the stability test
For closed-loop stability, Z must be zero. Therefore the Nyquist plot must encircle −1 exactly P times clockwise (or P times counterclockwise if the convention is reversed). When P = 0 the plot must not encircle −1 at all.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — First-order stable plant**  
*Given:* \( G(s) = \frac{1}{s+1} \), unity feedback, P = 0.  
*Find:* N required for stability.  
Substitute s = jω:  
$$ G(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1+j\omega}. $$  
Magnitude = 1/√(1+ω²), phase = −arctan(ω).  
The locus is a circle of radius 0.5 centered at 0.5. It lies entirely to the right of Re = 0 and never reaches −1.  
*Why:* direct substitution yields the parametric equations.  
The plot does not encircle −1, so N = 0 = P, hence Z = 0.  
**Final answer: stable.**  
*Reflection:* the curve never crosses the negative real axis beyond −1, a pattern that generalises to all minimum-phase systems with adequate gain margin.

**Example 2 — System with one open-loop unstable pole**  
*Given:* \( G(s) = \frac{1}{s-1} \), P = 1.  
*Find:* required encirclements.  
The Nyquist plot is a circle of radius 0.5 centered at −0.5. It passes directly through −1.  
*Why:* pole at +1 shifts the center to the left.  
A single clockwise encirclement occurs when the mirror-image negative-frequency arc is added.  
**Final answer: N = 1 satisfies N = P, Z = 0.**  
*Reflection:* the unstable pole forces the plot to start on the left half of the GH-plane, automatically creating the needed encirclement.

**Example 3 — Conditional stability**  
*Given:* \( G(s) = \frac{K(s+2)}{s(s-1)(s+3)} \), P = 1.  
*Find:* range of K for which N = 1.  
The plot crosses the negative real axis at two points whose locations depend on K. Solving |G(jω)| = 1 at the two phase-crossover frequencies yields the gain window 6 < K < 18.  
*Why:* each crossing contributes an encirclement change of ±1.  
**Final answer: stable only inside that interval.**  
*Reflection:* multiple crossings illustrate that encirclement count can change with gain, a fact invisible to simple Bode margins.

**Example 4 — Two unstable poles**  
*Given:* double integrator with positive feedback around an unstable mode, P = 2.  
*Find:* N.  
The plot forms a figure-eight that encircles −1 twice clockwise.  
*Why:* each right-half-plane pole contributes 180° of starting phase.  
**Final answer: N = 2 ⇒ Z = 0.**  
*Reflection:* higher-order unstable plants require correspondingly more encirclements; counting must be performed on the completed contour, not on magnitude alone.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                                      | How to avoid it                                      |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Forgetting negative-frequency arc | Plot drawn only for ω > 0                           | Always mirror the curve across the real axis         |
| Miscounting direction             | Clockwise vs counterclockwise convention mixed      | Fix clockwise as positive N and verify on a known example |
| Ignoring poles on imaginary axis  | D-contour indentation omitted                       | Indent with small semicircles and add 180° phase jump |
| Assuming P = 0 always             | Plant drawn without checking open-loop poles        | Factor denominator first and count right-half roots  |
| Infinite-radius arc contribution  | Type-0 systems map to nonzero point at ∞            | Verify relative degree ≥ 2 before collapsing to origin |
| Gain-scheduled systems            | Single Nyquist plot used for all operating points   | Generate family of plots, one per scheduling variable|
| Non-minimum-phase zeros           | RHP zero adds extra phase lag not visible in magnitude | Mark RHP zeros on the plot and adjust expected N     |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let G(s)H(s) be rational, proper, and free of poles or zeros on the standard D-contour except possibly simple poles on the imaginary axis. Let P be the number of poles of G H with positive real part. Let N be the net number of clockwise encirclements of the point −1 + j0 by the image of the D-contour under G H. Then the number of closed-loop poles with positive real part is Z = P − N. The closed-loop system is asymptotically stable if and only if Z = 0. (Dorf & Bishop, *Modern Control Systems*, 14e, §6.5.)

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture the Nyquist curve as a rubber band that must lasso the point −1 exactly P times clockwise; each missed lasso leaves an unstable pole loose in the right half-plane.  
2. **What to overlearn** — N = P − Z with Z = 0 for stability; clockwise positive; P counted from open-loop poles only.  
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.  
4. **First-principles fallback** — Redraw the D-contour, apply the argument principle to 1 + GH, and read N directly from the resulting winding number.
