## 1. The one-sentence answer
**When the characteristic equation of a second-order linear homogeneous ODE with constant coefficients possesses a repeated real root \(r\), a second linearly independent solution is obtained by multiplying the obvious exponential solution by the independent variable \(x\).**

The characteristic equation arises by substituting the trial form \(y = e^{rx}\) into the ODE \(y'' + p y' + q y = 0\). A repeated root signals that the two exponential solutions have collapsed into one, so the solution space must be enlarged by a new function that is still annihilated by the same differential operator. Reduction of order supplies that function systematically: assume the second solution has the product form \(y_2 = v(x) y_1(x)\) where \(y_1 = e^{rx}\), substitute into the ODE, and solve the resulting first-order equation for \(v'(x)\). The calculation always yields \(v(x) = x\) (up to an irrelevant constant), producing the second solution \(x e^{rx}\).

The general solution is therefore the two-parameter family \((c_1 + c_2 x) e^{rx}\). This form is forced by the algebraic multiplicity of the root; any other candidate function either reproduces the first solution or fails to satisfy the ODE.

> [!NOTE]
> The factor of \(x\) is not an arbitrary guess; it is the exact antiderivative that appears when the Wronskian identity is integrated after the repeated root has cancelled the leading term.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
Critical damping in automotive suspension systems occurs precisely when the damping ratio equals one, producing a repeated root in the characteristic equation; engineers at Bosch and Continental tune shock absorbers so that the displacement returns to equilibrium in minimal time without oscillation, and the \(x e^{rx}\) term governs the fastest non-oscillatory return.

In power-system stability analysis, the swing equation for synchronous generators under small disturbances is a second-order linear ODE; when the damping coefficient places the eigenvalues on the repeated-root boundary, utilities such as PJM Interconnection must verify that rotor-angle deviations decay as \((c_1 + c_2 t) e^{-\alpha t}\) to prevent loss of synchronism after a fault.

Semiconductor laser rate equations, after linearization about a steady state, yield a characteristic polynomial whose repeated real root corresponds to critically damped relaxation oscillations; device physicists at Intel and TSMC use the exact solution containing the linear-\(t\) prefactor to set bias currents that minimize turn-on jitter in optical interconnects.

The radial Schrödinger equation for the hydrogen atom in the degenerate \(n=2\) manifold reduces, after separation of variables, to an ODE whose indicial equation has a repeated root; the second solution generated by reduction of order supplies the \(r e^{-r}\) factor that distinguishes the 2s orbital from the 2p orbital and determines the degeneracy lifting under external fields.

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — The obvious solution collapses
A repeated real root \(r\) means that \(e^{rx}\) satisfies the ODE, yet a second, independent exponential solution does not exist.  
Example: \(y'' - 2y' + y = 0\) has characteristic equation \((s-1)^2 = 0\).  
Formal statement: if \(r\) satisfies both \(r^2 + p r + q = 0\) and \(2r + p = 0\), then \(e^{rx}\) is a solution but any second exponential is linearly dependent on it.  
> [!WARNING]
> Treating the repeated root as two distinct roots produces only one function and leaves the general solution under-determined.

### Step 2 — Reduction-of-order ansatz
Assume a second solution of the product form \(y_2 = v(x) \cdot e^{rx}\).  
Example: substitute into the ODE above to obtain an equation for \(v\).  
Formal statement: \(y_2 = v(x) y_1(x)\) with \(y_1 = e^{rx}\).  
> [!WARNING]
> Using a constant multiple or an exponential factor instead of a general \(v(x)\) merely reproduces \(y_1\) and yields no new solution.

### Step 3 — Substitution and cancellation
After inserting \(y_2\) and its derivatives, the coefficient of \(v\) vanishes identically because \(y_1\) already solves the ODE; only terms containing \(v'\) and \(v''\) survive.  
Example: the ODE reduces to \(v'' e^{x} = 0\).  
Formal statement: the resulting equation is \(y_1 v'' + (2 y_1' + p y_1) v' = 0\).  
> [!WARNING]
> Forgetting to differentiate the product \(v y_1\) twice produces an incorrect first-order equation missing the \(v''\) term.

### Step 4 — First-order equation for \(w = v'\)
Set \(w = v'\); the equation collapses to a separable first-order ODE in \(w\).  
Example: \(w' + 2w = 0\) integrates to \(w = C e^{-2x}\).  
Formal statement: \(w' + (2 r + p) w = 0\).  
> [!WARNING]
> Treating the equation as homogeneous in \(v\) rather than first-order in \(w\) leads to an unnecessary second integration constant that is later discarded.

### Step 5 — Integration yields the factor \(x\)
Because the root is repeated, \(2r + p = 0\), so \(w' = 0\) and therefore \(w\) is constant; one integration gives \(v(x) = x\).  
Example: \(v(x) = x\) produces \(y_2 = x e^{x}\).  
Formal statement: \(v(x) = \int e^{-\int (2r+p)\,dx}\,dx = x\) (constant omitted).  
> [!WARNING]
> Omitting the integration constant is harmless only because any additive constant simply reproduces a multiple of \(y_1\).

### Step 6 — Textbook general solution
The two linearly independent solutions \(e^{rx}\) and \(x e^{rx}\) span the solution space, so every solution is their linear combination.  
Formal statement: \(y = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{rx}\).

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Standard repeated root**  
*Given:* \(y'' - 6y' + 9y = 0\).  
*Find:* general solution.  
Assume \(y = e^{rx}\). Characteristic equation: \(r^2 - 6r + 9 = 0 \implies (r-3)^2 = 0\).  
One solution: \(y_1 = e^{3x}\).  
Set \(y_2 = v(x) e^{3x}\).  
\(y_2' = v' e^{3x} + 3v e^{3x}\),  
\(y_2'' = v'' e^{3x} + 6v' e^{3x} + 9v e^{3x}\).  
Substitute: \(v'' e^{3x} = 0 \implies v'' = 0 \implies v' = C \implies v = Cx + D\).  
Take \(C=1\), \(D=0\): \(y_2 = x e^{3x}\).  
General solution: \(y = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{3x}\).  
**\( y = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{3x} \)**  
*Reflection:* The second derivative vanished immediately because the repeated root cancelled every lower-order term; the same cancellation occurs for any constant-coefficient operator with multiplicity two.

**Example 2 — Non-monic leading coefficient**  
*Given:* \(4y'' + 4y' + y = 0\).  
*Find:* general solution.  
Characteristic equation: \(4r^2 + 4r + 1 = 0 \implies (2r+1)^2 = 0\), repeated root \(r = -1/2\).  
\(y_1 = e^{-x/2}\).  
Reduction yields \(v'' = 0\), hence \(y_2 = x e^{-x/2}\).  
**\( y = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{-x/2} \)**  
*Reflection:* Scaling the leading coefficient only rescales the characteristic polynomial; the multiplicity still forces the linear factor \(x\).

**Example 3 — Initial-value problem**  
*Given:* \(y'' + 4y' + 4y = 0\), \(y(0)=3\), \(y'(0)=-2\).  
*Find:* unique solution.  
Repeated root \(r=-2\).  
General solution \(y = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{-2x}\).  
\(y(0) = c_1 = 3\).  
\(y' = -2(c_1 + c_2 x)e^{-2x} + c_2 e^{-2x}\).  
\(y'(0) = -2c_1 + c_2 = -2 \implies c_2 = 4\).  
**\( y = (3 + 4x) e^{-2x} \)**  
*Reflection:* Initial conditions fix both constants exactly as they do for distinct roots; only the functional form changes.

**Example 4 — Verification by direct substitution**  
*Given:* \(y = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{rx}\) with repeated root.  
*Find:* confirm it satisfies \(y'' - 2ry' + r^2 y = 0\).  
Differentiate twice, collect coefficients of \(c_1\) and \(c_2\); every term cancels because both \(e^{rx}\) and \(x e^{rx}\) are solutions.  
**Identity holds identically.**  
*Reflection:* Direct substitution verifies linear independence via a nonzero Wronskian \(e^{2rx}\).

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                                      | How to avoid it                                      |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Writing two identical exponentials | Treating multiplicity as two distinct roots         | Check the discriminant; if zero, force the \(x\) factor |
| Using \(v(x) = e^{kx}\)           | Confusing reduction of order with undetermined coefficients | Always integrate the first-order equation for \(w = v'\) |
| Forgetting the product rule twice | Under-differentiating \(v y_1\)                     | Write all three derivatives explicitly before substitution |
| Discarding the linear term        | Thinking \(x e^{rx}\) is “extra”                    | Verify Wronskian is nonzero                          |
| Applying the formula when roots differ | Over-generalizing the repeated-root case            | First compute the discriminant or factor the characteristic polynomial |
| Sign error in the integrating factor | Losing track of the coefficient \(2r + p\)          | Use the fact that \(2r + p = 0\) for repeated roots   |
| Initial-condition algebra slip    | Differentiating the product incorrectly             | Always recompute \(y'\) from the final expression    |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let \(L[y] = y'' + p y' + q y\) with constant coefficients. Suppose the characteristic polynomial \(r^2 + p r + q\) has a repeated real root \(r\). Then the functions \(y_1(x) = e^{rx}\) and \(y_2(x) = x e^{rx}\) are linearly independent solutions of \(L[y] = 0\) on \(\mathbb{R}\), and the general solution is
\[
y(x) = (c_1 + c_2 x) e^{rx}, \qquad c_1,c_2 \in \mathbb{R}.
\]
(Boyce & DiPrima, *Elementary Differential Equations*, 11e, §3.4, Theorem 3.4.2.)

## 8. Visual — diagram or schematic
```text
Solution space (2-dimensional vector space)
          y2 = x e^{r x}
             /
            /   slope at x=0 equals 1
           /
y1 = e^{r x}  ---->  (both functions satisfy L[y]=0)
```
The two curves are tangent at \(x=0\) (value 1, derivative \(r\)) yet become linearly independent for \(x\neq 0\) because their Wronskian \(e^{2rx}\) never vanishes.

## 9. The memory technique
1. **The hook** — Picture a double root as two identical runners crossing the finish line together; the second solution must “stretch” the first by the distance \(x\), like tying an extra rubber band that grows linearly with time.
2. **What to overlearn** — The pair \(\{e^{rx}, x e^{rx}\}\) and the fact that the Wronskian is exactly \(e^{2rx}\).
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule** — Review the derivation at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.
4. **First-principles fallback** — Start from the definition \(L[y]=0\), insert \(y=v(x)e^{rx}\), cancel the known zero term, integrate the resulting first-order equation for \(v'\) using \(2r+p=0\).

## 10. What this unlocks
Mastery of the repeated-root case completes the constant-coefficient classification and opens the route to reduction of order for variable-coefficient equations, to the method of undetermined coefficients with resonance, and to the construction of fundamental matrices for systems.

- Variation of parameters for non-homogeneous problems
- Frobenius series solutions about regular singular points
- Jordan canonical form for defective matrices in linear systems
- Laplace-transform inversion tables for repeated poles
