## 1. The one-sentence answer
**Roots of unity are the solutions to the equation \(z^n=1\) in the complex plane; they lie at the vertices of a regular n-gon inscribed in the unit circle.**

Any complex number satisfying \(z^n=1\) must have modulus 1, because the modulus of a product is the product of the moduli and \(|z|^n=1\) forces \(|z|=1\). Once the modulus is fixed at 1, the argument \(\theta\) must satisfy \(n\theta=2\pi k\) for integer \(k\), so the possible angles are exactly the multiples \(2\pi k/n\). These n distinct angles place the roots at equal angular intervals around the circle.

The geometric picture is immediate: multiplication by any root simply rotates the plane by a fixed multiple of \(2\pi/n\). Raising that root to the nth power therefore rotates by a full multiple of \(2\pi\) and returns every point to itself, which is why the power equals 1.

> [!NOTE]
> The single deepest insight is that the roots are generated by repeated rotation; once you see them as the orbit of 1 under rotation by \(2\pi/n\), every algebraic property follows from geometry rather than from solving polynomials by hand.

## 2. Why this matters — concrete and current
In modern digital signal processing the Cooley–Tukey FFT decomposes a length-n transform into smaller blocks whose twiddle factors are precisely the nth roots of unity; NVIDIA’s cuFFT library and Apple’s Accelerate framework both rely on this factorisation to achieve real-time audio and image filtering on consumer hardware.

In quantum computing the discrete Fourier transform that extracts frequencies from qubit phase information is again multiplication by roots of unity; IBM’s Qiskit and Google’s Cirq both implement the quantum Fourier transform using exactly these roots as controlled-phase gates.

In aerospace guidance systems the characteristic equation of a sampled-data controller yields roots of unity when the sampling rate produces periodic modes; SpaceX telemetry pipelines use these roots to separate vibration harmonics from orbital data without aliasing.

In semiconductor crystallography the band-structure calculations for silicon and gallium-nitride lattices reduce to evaluating polynomials whose roots lie on the unit circle; the symmetry operations of the lattice are generated by cube roots and sixth roots of unity.

## 3. Mental prerequisites

| Concept                  | Why you need it here                                      |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Polar form \(re^{i\theta}\) | Converts multiplication into addition of angles, the only practical way to solve \(z^n=1\). |
| De Moivre’s theorem      | Supplies the rule \((re^{i\theta})^n=r^ne^{in\theta}\) that turns the equation into two independent real equations. |
| Argument modulo \(2\pi\) | Guarantees that angles differing by full rotations represent the same complex number, producing exactly n distinct roots. |

## 4. Building the idea — from intuition to formalism

### Step 1 — Start with the defining equation
A complex number z satisfies the equation when raised to the nth power it returns exactly 1. For n=3 the equation is \(z^3-1=0\). The number 1 itself is always a solution; the task is to locate the remaining solutions inside \(\mathbb{C}\).

### Step 2 — Switch to polar representation
Write \(z=re^{i\theta}\). Then \(z^n=r^ne^{in\theta}\). Setting this equal to 1, whose polar form is \(1\cdot e^{i0}\), forces the two real conditions \(r^n=1\) and \(n\theta=2\pi k\) for some integer k.

### Step 3 — Solve for the modulus
The only positive real solution of \(r^n=1\) is \(r=1\). Thus every root lies on the unit circle; no root can lie inside or outside.

> [!WARNING]
> Treating r as possibly negative or complex at this stage produces extraneous solutions that fail to satisfy the original equation.

### Step 4 — Solve for the argument
The angle equation becomes \(\theta=2\pi k/n\). Because angles are identified modulo \(2\pi\), the distinct values arise from k=0,1,…,n−1. Any larger k simply repeats one of these angles.

### Step 5 — Write the explicit roots
The n roots are therefore
\[
\omega_k=e^{2\pi i k/n},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n-1.
\]
They are conventionally labelled so that \(\omega_0=1\) and \(\omega_1\) is the primitive root that generates all others by successive powers.

### Step 6 — Geometric placement
Plotting the points \(\omega_k\) on the unit circle shows them separated by the constant angle \(2\pi/n\). Multiplication by \(\omega_1\) rotates the entire set onto itself; after n such rotations the set returns to its original position.

### Step 7 — Textbook statement reached
The set of all solutions is exactly the cyclic group generated by any primitive nth root of unity, and these solutions are the vertices of a regular n-gon centred at the origin.

## 5. Worked examples — every step shown

**Example 1 — Cube roots of unity**  
*Given:* Solve \(z^3=1\).  
*Find:* All three roots in rectangular form.  
Write \(z=re^{i\theta}\). Then \(r^3=1\) forces \(r=1\). The angle condition is \(3\theta=2\pi k\), so \(\theta=2\pi k/3\). The values k=0,1,2 give angles 0, \(2\pi/3\), \(4\pi/3\).  
Thus the roots are
\[
1,\quad -\frac12+i\frac{\sqrt3}2,\quad -\frac12-i\frac{\sqrt3}2.
\]
**Final answer**  
\[1,\quad\omega,\quad\omega^2\quad\text{where}\quad\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}.\]  
*Reflection:* The only algebraic work was converting the angle list into sine and cosine; the geometry supplied the angles directly.

**Example 2 — Fifth roots of unity**  
*Given:* Find the primitive fifth root.  
*Find:* Its rectangular coordinates to three decimals.  
The primitive root is \(e^{2\pi i/5}\). Its real part is \(\cos(2\pi/5)\approx0.309\), imaginary part \(\sin(2\pi/5)\approx0.951\).  
**Final answer**  
\[0.309+0.951i.\]  
*Reflection:* The numerical values are secondary; the exact algebraic form \(\frac{-1+\sqrt5}{4}+\frac{\sqrt{10+2\sqrt5}}{4}i\) follows from solving a quadratic once the angle is known.

**Example 3 — Distinguishing primitive roots**  
*Given:* Among the sixth roots of unity, identify the primitive ones.  
*Find:* Which exponents k give roots of exact order 6.  
A sixth root \(\omega^k\) is primitive when k is coprime to 6, i.e., k=1 or 5.  
**Final answer**  
\[e^{2\pi i/6},\quad e^{10\pi i/6}.\]  
*Reflection:* Checking gcd(k,n)=1 prevents mistakenly listing repeated roots.

**Example 4 — Geometric verification**  
*Given:* The four fourth roots of unity.  
*Find:* Their vector sum.  
The roots are 1, i, −1, −i. Their sum is the centroid of a square centred at the origin, hence zero.  
**Final answer**  
\[0.\]  
*Reflection:* The cancellation is forced by rotational symmetry; the same argument shows the sum of all nth roots is zero for any n>1.

## 6. Common traps and how to avoid them

| Trap                              | Why it happens                                | How to avoid it                              |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Forgetting that angles wrap at \(2\pi\) | Students treat k as running to infinity       | Always reduce k modulo n before listing roots |
| Writing \(r=-1\) as a possible modulus | Confusing real and complex modulus equations  | Solve \(r^n=1\) over positive reals only     |
| Listing n+1 “distinct” roots      | Including k=n which repeats k=0               | Stop the list at k=n−1                       |
| Confusing primitive roots with all roots | Not checking gcd(k,n)=1                       | Test coprimality for each candidate          |
| Using degrees instead of radians in the exponential | Mixing calculator modes                       | Keep every angle in radians inside \(e^{i\theta}\) |
| Assuming all roots are real       | Visualising only on the real line             | Draw the unit circle before solving          |
| Forgetting \(\omega^n=1\) implies \((\omega^k)^n=1\) | Overlooking the cyclic property               | Verify that every power of a root is again a root |

## 7. The textbook-precise statement
Let n be a positive integer. The equation \(z^n=1\) has exactly n distinct solutions in \(\mathbb{C}\), given by
\[
z_k=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i k}{n}\right),\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n-1.
\]
These solutions form a cyclic multiplicative group of order n. A root \(z_k\) is primitive precisely when \(\gcd(k,n)=1\). (Ahlfors, *Complex Analysis*, 3rd ed., §2.3.)

## 9. The memory technique
**The hook** — picture a bicycle wheel with n spokes; each spoke is a root of unity and spinning the wheel by one spoke multiplies by the primitive root.

**What to overlearn**  
- \(\exp(2\pi i k/n)\) for k=0 to n−1  
- The sum of all roots equals zero for n>1  
- A root is primitive iff its exponent is coprime to n

**Spaced-repetition schedule** — review at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.

**First-principles fallback** — start from \(z=re^{i\theta}\), equate modulus and argument separately, reduce angles modulo \(2\pi\).

## 10. What this unlocks
Mastery of roots of unity supplies the algebraic engine behind cyclotomic polynomials, the discrete Fourier transform, and the factorisation of \(x^n-1\). These in turn open the study of cyclotomic fields in algebraic number theory, efficient convolution algorithms in computer science, and the representation theory of cyclic groups.

- Cyclotomic polynomials and irreducibility criteria  
- Fast Fourier transform and its inverse  
- Minimal polynomials of regular polygons  
- Character tables of cyclic groups
