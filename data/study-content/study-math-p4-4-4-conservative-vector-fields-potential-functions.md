## 1. What it is — in plain English

Imagine you're pushing a toy car around a room. If the floor is perfectly flat and frictionless, the "work" you do (how much energy you expend) depends entirely on how far you push it and in what direction, but *not* on the wiggly path you take. Whether you push it in a straight line or a looping curve, as long as it starts at point A and ends at point B, the net work done by you is the same.

A "conservative vector field" is like that perfectly flat, frictionless floor, but for forces or influences that exist everywhere in space. It's a field where, if you move an object from one point to another, the total "effort" or "work" expended by the field depends *only* on the starting and ending points, and *not* on the specific path taken between them.

Think of gravity. If you lift a book from the floor to a shelf, the energy you expend against gravity is the same whether you lift it straight up or in a big arc. The gravitational field is conservative. The "potential function" is like a secret map that tells you the "energy level" at every point in space. If you know this map, you can figure out the work done just by looking at the energy levels of the start and end points, without tracing the whole path.

So, in simple terms: a conservative vector field is one where the "journey doesn't matter, only the destination." And a potential function is the magic key that lets you calculate the "cost" of the journey just by knowing the start and end points.

## 2. Why it matters — real-world applications

The concept of conservative vector fields and potential functions is fundamental across many scientific and engineering disciplines because it often implies the conservation of energy, simplifies calculations, and provides deep insights into the nature of forces and fields.

1.  **Physics — Gravitational and Electrostatic Fields:**
    *   **Application:** Understanding planetary orbits, designing satellites, and analyzing the behavior of charged particles.
    *   **Specificity:** The gravitational field $\mathbf{F}_g$ is conservative, meaning the work done by gravity on an object moving from point A to point B depends only on the change in its potential energy (gravitational potential). Similarly, the electrostatic field $\mathbf{E}$ generated by stationary charges is conservative. This allows engineers at companies like **SpaceX** or **NASA** to calculate the energy required to move a rocket or satellite between different altitudes without needing to integrate along the complex trajectory. For example, the escape velocity of a rocket can be derived from the gravitational potential energy, which is the potential function for the gravitational field. In electronics, the voltage (electrical potential) across components is a direct application of the potential function for the electrostatic field, crucial for designing circuits in devices made by **Intel** or **Apple**.

2.  **Fluid Dynamics — Irrotational Flow:**
    *   **Application:** Designing efficient aircraft wings, optimizing pipeline flow, and predicting weather patterns.
    *   **Specificity:** A fluid flow is "irrotational" if its velocity field is conservative. This means the fluid doesn't "spin" locally (its curl is zero). For an incompressible, irrotational flow, a velocity potential function $\phi$ exists such that the velocity field $\mathbf{v} = \nabla \phi$. This greatly simplifies the analysis of fluid motion. Aerospace engineers at **Boeing** or **Airbus** use this concept to model airflow over wings, reducing drag and improving lift. By finding a potential function, they can easily calculate the velocity and pressure at any point in the flow field, which is critical for aerodynamic design and performance prediction.

3.  **Machine Learning and Optimization — Gradient Descent:**
    *   **Application:** Training neural networks, finding optimal parameters for models, and solving complex optimization problems.
    *   **Specificity:** In machine learning, many optimization algorithms, particularly gradient descent, operate on a "loss function" or "cost function." This loss function can be thought of as a scalar potential function in a high-dimensional parameter space. The negative gradient of this loss function, $-\nabla L$, defines a vector field that points in the direction of steepest decrease. When this "gradient field" is conservative, it means that the "work" done in moving through the parameter space to reach an optimum depends only on the starting and ending configurations, not the specific path taken by the optimization algorithm. This property is implicitly leveraged by algorithms used by companies like **Google** or **Meta** to efficiently find the minimum of complex loss landscapes, ensuring that the training process converges reliably.

## 3. Prerequisites — what you must know first

Before diving into conservative vector fields and potential functions, ensure you have a solid grasp of the following concepts:

*   **Vectors and Vector Operations:** Understanding vector addition, subtraction, scalar multiplication, dot product, and cross product.
*   **Partial Derivatives:** How to differentiate a multivariable function with respect to one variable while treating others as constants.
*   **Gradient of a Scalar Function:** The vector field formed by taking the partial derivatives of a scalar function, denoted $\nabla f$. It points in the direction of the steepest increase of the function.
*   **Vector Fields:** What a vector field is (a function that assigns a vector to each point in space) and how to visualize simple ones.
*   **Line Integrals (Scalar and Vector):** How to compute integrals of functions and vector fields along a curve. Specifically, $\int_C f(x,y,z) ds$ and $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$.
*   **Curl of a Vector Field:** A vector operator that measures the "rotation" or "circulation" of a vector field at a point, denoted $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$. For a 2D field $\mathbf{F}(P,Q) = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j}$, the "2D curl" or scalar curl is $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}$. For a 3D field $\mathbf{F}(P,Q,R) = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j} + R\mathbf{k}$, the curl is a 3D vector.
*   **Simply Connected Regions:** A region in space where every closed loop within it can be continuously shrunk to a point without leaving the region (e.g., a solid ball is simply connected, a donut is not). This is crucial for the curl test.
*   **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Single Variable):** The idea that the definite integral of a derivative is just the difference of the original function evaluated at the endpoints: $\int_a^b f'(x) dx = f(b) - f(a)$. This concept is generalized to line integrals of conservative fields.

## 4. The core idea — step by step

Let's build up the concept of conservative vector fields and potential functions piece by piece, starting with the fundamental idea of "work."

### Step 1: Work Done by a Force Field

**Plain English:** When a force acts on an object and moves it, we say that the force does "work" on the object. If the force changes as the object moves, or if the path is curved, calculating the total work requires summing up tiny bits of work along the path.

**Small Concrete Example:** Imagine a tiny bug crawling along a curved wire. All along the wire, there's a gentle breeze (our "force field") pushing the bug. The breeze might be stronger in some spots, or pushing in different directions. To find the total work done by the breeze on the bug, we'd have to consider the force at each tiny segment of the wire and how much it helps (or hinders) the bug's movement along that segment.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** The work $W$ done by a force field $\mathbf{F}$ in moving an object along a curve $C$ is given by the line integral:
$$ W = \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} $$
where $\mathbf{r}(t)$ is the parametrization of the curve $C$, and $d\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}'(t) dt$.

**What Could Go Wrong:** Not all forces are constant, and not all paths are straight. If you simply multiply force by distance, you're assuming a constant force and a straight path, which is rarely the case in multivariable calculus problems. The line integral correctly accounts for varying forces and curved paths.

### Step 2: Path Independence

**Plain English:** For some special force fields, the total work done in moving an object from a starting point A to an ending point B is *always the same*, regardless of the specific path taken between A and B. It doesn't matter if you take a direct route, a winding path, or even loop around a few times; as long as you start at A and end at B, the work done is identical.

**Small Concrete Example:** Lifting a heavy box from the floor to a shelf. The work you do against gravity depends only on the change in height, not on whether you lift it straight up, or slide it across the floor first, then lift it. Gravity is a force field where the work done is path-independent.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** A vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is said to be **path-independent** if for any two points $A$ and $B$ in its domain, the line integral $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ is the same for all piecewise-smooth curves $C$ that start at $A$ and end at $B$.
This implies that if $C_1$ and $C_2$ are any two paths from $A$ to $B$, then:
$$ \int_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} $$
An equivalent condition is that the line integral around *any* closed loop $C$ (where $A=B$) is zero:
$$ \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0 $$

**What Could Go Wrong:** Assuming *every* field is path-independent. A field with friction, for instance, is *not* path-independent; sliding a box across a rough floor will do more work if you take a longer path.

### Step 3: Conservative Vector Fields

**Plain English:** A vector field that exhibits path independence (as described in Step 2) is called a **conservative vector field**. This term comes from physics, where such fields are associated with the conservation of mechanical energy.

**Small Concrete Example:** The gravitational field is a classic example. The electrostatic field (the force between electric charges) is another. Magnetic fields, however, are generally *not* conservative.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** A vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is called **conservative** if it is path-independent. Formally, $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative if for any two points $A$ and $B$, the line integral $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ is independent of the path $C$ connecting $A$ to $B$. This is often the *definition* of a conservative field.

**What Could Go Wrong:** Confusing "conservative" with "constant." A conservative field can vary greatly throughout space; it just has this special property regarding work done over paths.

### Step 4: Potential Functions

**Plain English:** If a vector field is conservative, there's a special scalar function (a function that just gives a number, not a vector, at each point) called a **potential function** (or scalar potential). The amazing thing is that the conservative vector field is precisely the *gradient* of this potential function. Think of the potential function as a "height map" or "energy landscape," and the vector field as the "slope" or "steepness" at every point, indicating the direction of greatest increase.

**Small Concrete Example:** For the gravitational field, the potential function is essentially the gravitational potential energy. If you know the potential energy at every point in space, you can figure out the gravitational force at any point just by taking its gradient. The force always points "downhill" on the potential energy landscape.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** A vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative if and only if there exists a scalar function $f(x,y,z)$ such that $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$. The function $f$ is called a **potential function** for $\mathbf{F}$.
Recall that the gradient $\nabla f$ is defined as:
$$ \nabla f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \mathbf{j} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \mathbf{k} $$
So, if $\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j} + R\mathbf{k}$, then for $\mathbf{F}$ to be conservative, we must have:
$$ P = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \quad Q = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, \quad R = \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} $$

**What Could Go Wrong:** Forgetting that $\mathbf{F}$ is a vector field and $f$ is a scalar function. They are fundamentally different types of mathematical objects, linked by the gradient operator. In physics, potential energy $U$ is often defined such that $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$, so be mindful of the sign convention. In pure mathematics, $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$ is standard.

### Step 5: Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals

**Plain English:** This is the payoff for having a conservative field and its potential function. If you want to calculate the work done by a conservative field along *any* path, you don't need to do a complicated line integral. You just need to find the potential function, evaluate it at the endpoint, evaluate it at the starting point, and subtract! It's exactly like the single-variable Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

**Small Concrete Example:** If you know the gravitational potential energy function $U(x,y,z)$, and you move a mass from point $(x_1,y_1,z_1)$ to $(x_2,y_2,z_2)$, the work done by gravity is $U(x_1,y_1,z_1) - U(x_2,y_2,z_2)$. Or, if using our mathematical convention $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$, then the work done *by the field* is $f(x_2,y_2,z_2) - f(x_1,y_1,z_1)$.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** Let $\mathbf{F}$ be a conservative vector field with potential function $f$ (i.e., $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$) in an open region $D$. If $C$ is a piecewise-smooth curve in $D$ parametrized by $\mathbf{r}(t)$ for $a \le t \le b$, then:
$$ \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_C \nabla f \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{r}(b)) - f(\mathbf{r}(a)) $$
This theorem is incredibly powerful because it turns a potentially complex line integral calculation into a simple subtraction of scalar function values.

**What Could Go Wrong:** This theorem *only* applies if the vector field is conservative. If you try to use it on a non-conservative field, you'll get the wrong answer. Always check for conservatism first!

### Step 6: Testing for Conservatism (Curl Test)

**Plain English:** How do we know if a field is conservative without trying every possible path? There's a quick mathematical test. In 3D, if the vector field has "no curl" (meaning it doesn't tend to spin or rotate anything placed in it), then it's conservative. In 2D, this translates to a condition on its partial derivatives. This test is valid provided the domain of the field is "simply connected" (no holes, like a donut).

**Small Concrete Example:** Imagine a river. If the water flows smoothly and parallel (no eddies or whirlpools), its velocity field has zero curl and is conservative. If there are whirlpools, it has non-zero curl and is not conservative.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:**
Let $\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j} + R\mathbf{k}$ be a vector field whose component functions $P, Q, R$ have continuous first-order partial derivatives on an open, simply connected region $D$.
1.  **In 3D:** $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative if and only if its **curl is zero**:
    $$ \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} $$
    The curl is calculated as:
    $$ \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left( \frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z} \right)\mathbf{i} + \left( \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x} \right)\mathbf{j} + \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right)\mathbf{k} $$
    So, for $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$, all three components must be zero.
2.  **In 2D:** If $\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j}$ (we can think of this as $R=0$ in 3D), then the curl condition simplifies. The $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$ components of the 3D curl become $\frac{\partial 0}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z} = -\frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}$ and $\frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial 0}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}$. Since $P$ and $Q$ only depend on $x$ and $y$, these partials with respect to $z$ are zero. Thus, for 2D, the condition for conservatism is:
    $$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 0 \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} $$
    This is often called the "mixed partials test" because it stems from the fact that if $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$, then $P = \partial f / \partial x$ and $Q = \partial f / \partial y$, so $\partial P / \partial y = \partial^2 f / (\partial y \partial x)$ and $\partial Q / \partial x = \partial^2 f / (\partial x \partial y)$. For continuous second partials, mixed partials are equal.

**What Could Go Wrong:**
*   Forgetting the "simply connected region" condition. If the domain has holes (e.g., $\mathbf{F} = \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\mathbf{i} + \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\mathbf{j}$ has zero curl everywhere *except* the origin, but its domain is not simply connected if it excludes the origin, and it's not conservative on such a domain).
*   Incorrectly calculating partial derivatives.
*   Applying the 2D condition when the field is truly 3D, or vice versa.

### Step 7: Finding the Potential Function

**Plain English:** Once we've confirmed a field is conservative, we can find its potential function $f$. We do this by "reversing" the gradient process. Since we know $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$, we can integrate each component of $\mathbf{F}$ with respect to its corresponding variable, and then combine the results carefully, ensuring all parts match up.

**Small Concrete Example:** If $\mathbf{F} = (2x)\mathbf{i} + (3y^2)\mathbf{j}$, we know $\partial f / \partial x = 2x$ and $\partial f / \partial y = 3y^2$. Integrating $2x$ with respect to $x$ gives $x^2 + C_1(y)$ (where $C_1(y)$ is a "constant" that can depend on $y$). Integrating $3y^2$ with respect to $y$ gives $y^3 + C_2(x)$ (where $C_2(x)$ can depend on $x$). By comparing these, we can deduce $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^3 + K$ (where $K$ is a true constant).

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** Given $\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j} + R\mathbf{k}$ (or $P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j}$ for 2D), and knowing $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$:
1.  Start with $P = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$. Integrate $P$ with respect to $x$ to get $f(x,y,z) = \int P \, dx + C_1(y,z)$. The "constant of integration" $C_1$ can be any function of $y$ and $z$.
2.  Next, differentiate this preliminary $f$ with respect to $y$ and set it equal to $Q$:
    $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \int P \, dx + C_1(y,z) \right) = Q$. This allows you to solve for $\frac{\partial C_1}{\partial y}$.
3.  Integrate $\frac{\partial C_1}{\partial y}$ with respect to $y$ to find $C_1(y,z) = \int \left( Q - \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \int P \, dx \right) dy + C_2(z)$. Now $C_2$ is a function of $z$ only.
4.  Finally, differentiate the current $f$ with respect to $z$ and set it equal to $R$:
    $\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \dots + C_2(z) \right) = R$. This allows you to solve for $\frac{d C_2}{d z}$.
5.  Integrate $\frac{d C_2}{d z}$ with respect to $z$ to find $C_2(z)$, which will be $K$ (a true constant).
6.  Combine all parts to get $f(x,y,z)$.

**Alternatively (and often easier):**
1.  Integrate $P$ with respect to $x$: $f_1(x,y,z) = \int P \, dx = F_P(x,y,z) + g(y,z)$.
2.  Integrate $Q$ with respect to $y$: $f_2(x,y,z) = \int Q \, dy = F_Q(x,y,z) + h(x,z)$.
3.  Integrate $R$ with respect to $z$: $f_3(x,y,z) = \int R \, dz = F_R(x,y,z) + k(x,y)$.
4.  The potential function $f(x,y,z)$ is formed by taking the union of all unique terms from $F_P, F_Q, F_R$ and adding a constant $K$. For example, if $f_1 = x^2y + \sin(z) + g(y,z)$, $f_2 = x^2y + yz^2 + h(x,z)$, and $f_3 = \sin(z) + yz^2 + k(x,y)$, then $f(x,y,z) = x^2y + \sin(z) + yz^2 + K$.

**What Could Go Wrong:**
*   Forgetting the "constants of integration" that are functions of other variables.
*   Not correctly matching up terms when combining the results of multiple integrations.
*   Trying to find a potential function for a field that is *not* conservative (the steps will lead to a contradiction).

## 5. Worked examples — multiple, with every step shown

### Example 1: Easy 2D Field

**Problem:** Given the vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (2xy + y^2)\mathbf{i} + (x^2 + 2xy)\mathbf{j}$.
a) Determine if $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.
b) If it is, find a potential function $f(x,y)$.
c) Use the potential function to evaluate the line integral $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ along any curve $C$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$.

**Given:** Vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (2xy + y^2)\mathbf{i} + (x^2 + 2xy)\mathbf{j}$.
**Want:** a) Conservatism check. b) Potential function $f(x,y)$. c) Line integral value.

---

**a) Determine if $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.**

1.  **Identify $P$ and $Q$ components:**
    $P(x,y) = 2xy + y^2$
    $Q(x,y) = x^2 + 2xy$
    *These are the components of the vector field $\mathbf{F}$.*

2.  **Calculate the partial derivative of $P$ with respect to $y$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2xy + y^2) = 2x + 2y $$
    *We are checking the condition for a 2D conservative field, which involves comparing mixed partials.*

3.  **Calculate the partial derivative of $Q$ with respect to $x$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2 + 2xy) = 2x + 2y $$
    *This is the other mixed partial we need to compare.*

4.  **Compare the partial derivatives:**
    Since $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 2x + 2y$ and $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 2x + 2y$, we have $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}$.
    *The condition for a 2D field to be conservative is met.*

5.  **Conclusion for (a):**
    Since the partial derivatives are equal and the domain of $\mathbf{F}$ (all of $\mathbb{R}^2$) is open and simply connected, the vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is **conservative**.
    *This confirms that a potential function exists.*

---

**b) If it is, find a potential function $f(x,y)$.**

1.  **Start with the definition of a potential function:**
    We know that if $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$, then $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = P$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = Q$.
    So, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy + y^2$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + 2xy$.
    *We are trying to reverse the gradient operation.*

2.  **Integrate $P$ with respect to $x$ to find a preliminary $f(x,y)$:**
    $$ f(x,y) = \int (2xy + y^2) \, dx = x^2y + xy^2 + g(y) $$
    *When integrating with respect to $x$, any terms that depend only on $y$ are treated as constants. So, the constant of integration can be a function of $y$, denoted $g(y)$.*

3.  **Differentiate this preliminary $f(x,y)$ with respect to $y$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2y + xy^2 + g(y)) = x^2 + 2xy + g'(y) $$
    *We now have an expression for $\partial f / \partial y$ that includes $g'(y)$.*

4.  **Compare this to the given $Q(x,y)$:**
    We know that $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ must be equal to $Q(x,y) = x^2 + 2xy$.
    So, $x^2 + 2xy + g'(y) = x^2 + 2xy$.
    *This step allows us to determine what $g'(y)$ must be.*

5.  **Solve for $g'(y)$:**
    Subtracting $x^2 + 2xy$ from both sides gives $g'(y) = 0$.
    *This means $g(y)$ is a constant.*

6.  **Integrate $g'(y)$ with respect to $y$ to find $g(y)$:**
    $$ g(y) = \int 0 \, dy = C $$
    *This confirms $g(y)$ is an arbitrary constant.*

7.  **Substitute $g(y)$ back into the expression for $f(x,y)$:**
    $$ f(x,y) = x^2y + xy^2 + C $$
    *This is our potential function.*

8.  **Conclusion for (b):**
    A potential function for $\mathbf{F}$ is $\boxed{f(x,y) = x^2y + xy^2 + C}$.
    *We can choose $C=0$ for simplicity, so $f(x,y) = x^2y + xy^2$.*

---

**c) Use the potential function to evaluate the line integral $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ along any curve $C$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$.**

1.  **Identify the starting and ending points:**
    Starting point $A = (0,0)$.
    Ending point $B = (1,2)$.
    *The Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals only requires the endpoints.*

2.  **Evaluate the potential function at the endpoints:**
    Using $f(x,y) = x^2y + xy^2$ (setting $C=0$ for convenience):
    At point $A=(0,0)$: $f(0,0) = (0)^2(0) + (0)(0)^2 = 0$.
    At point $B=(1,2)$: $f(1,2) = (1)^2(2) + (1)(2)^2 = 1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 4 = 2 + 4 = 6$.
    *We are finding the "potential energy" at the start and end.*

3.  **Apply the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals:**
    $$ \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(B) - f(A) $$
    $$ \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(1,2) - f(0,0) = 6 - 0 = 6 $$
    *The line integral is simply the difference in the potential function values.*

4.  **Conclusion for (c):**
    The value of the line integral is $\boxed{6}$.
    *This demonstrates the power of potential functions for conservative fields: no need to parameterize the curve or perform complex integration.*

**Reflection:** This example was straightforward because the mixed partials matched perfectly, leading to a simple $g'(y)=0$. The integration steps were also basic polynomials. The final step clearly showed the utility of the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals.

### Example 2: Medium 3D Field

**Problem:** Given the vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = yz\mathbf{i} + (xz + 2y)\mathbf{j} + (xy + 2z)\mathbf{k}$.
a) Determine if $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.
b) If it is, find a potential function $f(x,y,z)$.

**Given:** Vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = yz\mathbf{i} + (xz + 2y)\mathbf{j} + (xy + 2z)\mathbf{k}$.
**Want:** a) Conservatism check. b) Potential function $f(x,y,z)$.

---

**a) Determine if $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.**

1.  **Identify $P$, $Q$, and $R$ components:**
    $P(x,y,z) = yz$
    $Q(x,y,z) = xz + 2y$
    $R(x,y,z) = xy + 2z$
    *These are the components of the 3D vector field.*

2.  **Calculate the components of the curl $\nabla \times \mathbf{F}$:**
    We need to check if $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$. This means checking three conditions:
    *   $\frac{\partial R}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}$
    *   $\frac{\partial P}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial R}{\partial x}$
    *   $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}$
    *This is the 3D curl test for conservatism.*

3.  **Calculate $\frac{\partial R}{\partial y}$ and $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial R}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(xy + 2z) = x $$
    $$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(xz + 2y) = x $$
    *The first pair matches.*

4.  **Calculate $\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}$ and $\frac{\partial R}{\partial x}$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(yz) = y $$
    $$ \frac{\partial R}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xy + 2z) = y $$
    *The second pair matches.*

5.  **Calculate $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xz + 2y) = z $$
    $$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(yz) = z $$
    *The third pair matches.*

6.  **Conclusion for (a):**
    Since all three curl components are zero ($\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$), and the domain of $\mathbf{F}$ (all of $\mathbb{R}^3$) is open and simply connected, the vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is **conservative**.
    *All conditions are met, so a potential function exists.*

---

**b) If it is, find a potential function $f(x,y,z)$.**

1.  **Start with the definition of a potential function:**
    We know $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$, so:
    $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = P = yz$
    $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = Q = xz + 2y$
    $\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = R = xy + 2z$
    *We need to integrate these partial derivatives and combine them.*

2.  **Integrate $P$ with respect to $x$:**
    $$ f(x,y,z) = \int yz \, dx = xyz + g(y,z) $$
    *The constant of integration $g(y,z)$ can be a function of $y$ and $z$ because it would disappear if we differentiated with respect to $x$.*

3.  **Differentiate this $f(x,y,z)$ with respect to $y$ and compare to $Q$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(xyz + g(y,z)) = xz + \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} $$
    We know $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = Q = xz + 2y$.
    So, $xz + \frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = xz + 2y$.
    This implies $\frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 2y$.
    *This step helps us find the $y$-dependent part of $g(y,z)$.*

4.  **Integrate $\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}$ with respect to $y$ to find $g(y,z)$:**
    $$ g(y,z) = \int 2y \, dy = y^2 + h(z) $$
    *The constant of integration $h(z)$ can be a function of $z$ because it would disappear if we differentiated with respect to $y$.*

5.  **Substitute $g(y,z)$ back into the expression for $f(x,y,z)$:**
    $$ f(x,y,z) = xyz + y^2 + h(z) $$
    *Now we have an expression for $f$ with only one unknown function $h(z)$.*

6.  **Differentiate this $f(x,y,z)$ with respect to $z$ and compare to $R$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(xyz + y^2 + h(z)) = xy + h'(z) $$
    We know $\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = R = xy + 2z$.
    So, $xy + h'(z) = xy + 2z$.
    This implies $h'(z) = 2z$.
    *This step helps us find the $z$-dependent part of $h(z)$.*

7.  **Integrate $h'(z)$ with respect to $z$ to find $h(z)$:**
    $$ h(z) = \int 2z \, dz = z^2 + C $$
    *The final constant of integration $C$ is a true constant.*

8.  **Substitute $h(z)$ back into the expression for $f(x,y,z)$:**
    $$ f(x,y,z) = xyz + y^2 + z^2 + C $$
    *This is our complete potential function.*

9.  **Conclusion for (b):**
    A potential function for $\mathbf{F}$ is $\boxed{f(x,y,z) = xyz + y^2 + z^2 + C}$.
    *We can choose $C=0$ for simplicity, so $f(x,y,z) = xyz + y^2 + z^2$.*

**Reflection:** This example involved more variables and steps for finding the potential function, but the systematic approach of integrating one component, differentiating, comparing, and integrating again worked smoothly. The curl test was essential to confirm the field was indeed conservative before attempting to find $f$.

### Example 3: Harder 2D Field with Tricky Integration

**Problem:** Given the vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \left(e^x \sin y + 3x^2\right)\mathbf{i} + \left(e^x \cos y - 2y\right)\mathbf{j}$.
a) Determine if $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.
b) If it is, find a potential function $f(x,y)$.
c) Evaluate $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ where $C$ is the curve from $(\ln 2, 0)$ to $(\ln 3, \pi/2)$.

**Given:** $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \left(e^x \sin y + 3x^2\right)\mathbf{i} + \left(e^x \cos y - 2y\right)\mathbf{j}$.
**Want:** a) Conservatism check. b) Potential function $f(x,y)$. c) Line integral value.

---

**a) Determine if $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.**

1.  **Identify $P$ and $Q$ components:**
    $P(x,y) = e^x \sin y + 3x^2$
    $Q(x,y) = e^x \cos y - 2y$
    *These are the components of the vector field.*

2.  **Calculate $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(e^x \sin y + 3x^2) = e^x \cos y + 0 = e^x \cos y $$
    *Differentiate $P$ with respect to $y$, treating $x$ as a constant.*

3.  **Calculate $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(e^x \cos y - 2y) = e^x \cos y - 0 = e^x \cos y $$
    *Differentiate $Q$ with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a constant.*

4.  **Compare the partial derivatives:**
    Since $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = e^x \cos y$ and $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = e^x \cos y$, we have $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}$.
    *The mixed partials are equal.*

5.  **Conclusion for (a):**
    Since the partial derivatives are equal and the domain of $\mathbf{F}$ (all of $\mathbb{R}^2$) is open and simply connected, the vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is **conservative**.
    *This confirms a potential function exists.*

---

**b) If it is, find a potential function $f(x,y)$.**

1.  **Start with the definition of a potential function:**
    $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = P = e^x \sin y + 3x^2$
    $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = Q = e^x \cos y - 2y$
    *We need to integrate these to find $f(x,y)$.*

2.  **Integrate $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ with respect to $x$:**
    $$ f(x,y) = \int (e^x \sin y + 3x^2) \, dx $$
    $$ f(x,y) = e^x \sin y + x^3 + g(y) $$
    *Integrating $e^x \sin y$ with respect to $x$ gives $e^x \sin y$. Integrating $3x^2$ with respect to $x$ gives $x^3$. The "constant" of integration is a function of $y$, $g(y)$.*

3.  **Differentiate this preliminary $f(x,y)$ with respect to $y$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(e^x \sin y + x^3 + g(y)) = e^x \cos y + 0 + g'(y) = e^x \cos y + g'(y) $$
    *We are now using this expression to find $g(y)$.*

4.  **Compare this to the given $Q(x,y)$:**
    We know $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = Q = e^x \cos y - 2y$.
    So, $e^x \cos y + g'(y) = e^x \cos y - 2y$.
    *Equating the two expressions for $\partial f / \partial y$.*

5.  **Solve for $g'(y)$:**
    Subtracting $e^x \cos y$ from both sides gives $g'(y) = -2y$.
    *This isolates $g'(y)$.*

6.  **Integrate $g'(y)$ with respect to $y$ to find $g(y)$:**
    $$ g(y) = \int (-2y) \, dy = -y^2 + C $$
    *Integrating $-2y$ with respect to $y$ gives $-y^2$. The final constant of integration is $C$.*

7.  **Substitute $g(y)$ back into the expression for $f(x,y)$:**
    $$ f(x,y) = e^x \sin y + x^3 - y^2 + C $$
    *This is our complete potential function.*

8.  **Conclusion for (b):**
    A potential function for $\mathbf{F}$ is $\boxed{f(x,y) = e^x \sin y + x^3 - y^2 + C}$.
    *We can choose $C=0$ for simplicity, so $f(x,y) = e^x \sin y + x^3 - y^2$.*

---

**c) Evaluate $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ where $C$ is the curve from $(\ln 2, 0)$ to $(\ln 3, \pi/2)$.**

1.  **Identify the starting and ending points:**
    Starting point $A = (\ln 2, 0)$.
    Ending point $B = (\ln 3, \pi/2)$.
    *The Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals makes this calculation easy.*

2.  **Evaluate the potential function at the endpoints:**
    Using $f(x,y) = e^x \sin y + x^3 - y^2$:
    At point $A=(\ln 2, 0)$:
    $f(\ln 2, 0) = e^{\ln 2} \sin(0) + (\ln 2)^3 - (0)^2$
    $f(\ln 2, 0) = 2 \cdot 0 + (\ln 2)^3 - 0 = (\ln 2)^3$.
    *Recall that $e^{\ln a} = a$ and $\sin(0) = 0$.*

    At point $B=(\ln 3, \pi/2)$:
    $f(\ln 3, \pi/2) = e^{\ln 3} \sin(\pi/2) + (\ln 3)^3 - (\pi/2)^2$
    $f(\ln 3, \pi/2) = 3 \cdot 1 + (\ln 3)^3 - \frac{\pi^2}{4} = 3 + (\ln 3)^3 - \frac{\pi^2}{4}$.
    *Recall that $e^{\ln a} = a$ and $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$.*

3.  **Apply the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals:**
    $$ \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(B) - f(A) $$
    $$ \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \left(3 + (\ln 3)^3 - \frac{\pi^2}{4}\right) - (\ln 2)^3 $$
    $$ \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 3 + (\ln 3)^3 - (\ln 2)^3 - \frac{\pi^2}{4} $$
    *The line integral is the difference in potential values.*

4.  **Conclusion for (c):**
    The value of the line integral is $\boxed{3 + (\ln 3)^3 - (\ln 2)^3 - \frac{\pi^2}{4}}$.
    *The result can look complex, but the process was simplified by using the potential function.*

**Reflection:** This example involved trigonometric and exponential functions, making the partial derivatives and integrations slightly more complex. The evaluation of the potential function at the endpoints also required careful handling of logarithms and trigonometric values. It highlights that even with complicated functions, the method remains the same and effective.

### Example 4: Non-Conservative Field (2D)

**Problem:** Given the vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (x^2y)\mathbf{i} + (xy^2)\mathbf{j}$.
a) Determine if $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.
b) If it is not, explain why a potential function does not exist.

**Given:** Vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (x^2y)\mathbf{i} + (xy^2)\mathbf{j}$.
**Want:** a) Conservatism check. b) Explanation if not conservative.

---

**a) Determine if $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative.**

1.  **Identify $P$ and $Q$ components:**
    $P(x,y) = x^2y$
    $Q(x,y) = xy^2$
    *These are the components of the vector field.*

2.  **Calculate the partial derivative of $P$ with respect to $y$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2y) = x^2 $$
    *We are checking the 2D conservatism condition.*

3.  **Calculate the partial derivative of $Q$ with respect to $x$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xy^2) = y^2 $$
    *This is the other mixed partial.*

4.  **Compare the partial derivatives:**
    We have $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = x^2$ and $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = y^2$.
    Since $x^2 \neq y^2$ (in general, they are only equal when $x=\pm y$), the condition $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}$ is **not met**.
    *The mixed partials are not equal.*

5.  **Conclusion for (a):**
    Since $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \neq \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}$, the vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is **not conservative**.
    *The field fails the conservatism test.*

---

**b) If it is not, explain why a potential function does not exist.**

1.  **Recall the definition of a potential function:**
    A vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative if and only if there exists a scalar function $f(x,y)$ such that $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$.
    This means $P = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ and $Q = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$.
    *This is the fundamental link between a conservative field and its potential function.*

2.  **Consider the implications for mixed partials:**
    If such a function $f$ existed, then its second-order mixed partial derivatives would be:
    $$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x} $$
    $$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y} $$
    *These are the partial derivatives we calculated in part (a).*

3.  **Apply Clairaut's Theorem (or Schwarz's Theorem):**
    For a function $f$ with continuous second-order partial derivatives (which $P$ and $Q$ imply for $f$), Clairaut's Theorem states that the mixed partials are equal: $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y}$.
    *This theorem is the mathematical basis for the curl test.*

4.  **Connect to the current problem:**
    In part (a), we found that $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = x^2$ and $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = y^2$. Since $x^2 \neq y^2$ (they are not identically equal for all $x,y$), this means $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x} \neq \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y}$.
    *Our calculations contradict Clairaut's Theorem if a potential function were to exist.*

5.  **Conclusion for (b):**
    A potential function $f(x,y)$ for $\mathbf{F}$ **does not exist** because if it did, its mixed second partial derivatives $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y}$ would have to be equal (by Clairaut's Theorem, given that $P$ and $Q$ have continuous partials). However, we found that $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = x^2$ and $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = y^2$, which are not equal. This contradiction proves that no such potential function $f$ can exist for this vector field.

**Reflection:** This example is crucial for understanding *why* the curl test works and what it means when a field is *not* conservative. It reinforces the idea that the existence of a potential function is directly tied to the equality of mixed partials (or zero curl in 3D).

## 6. Common mistakes and traps

1.  **Forgetting the "Simply Connected Region" Condition:** The curl test ($\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ or $\partial Q / \partial x = \partial P / \partial y$) is a sufficient condition for conservatism *only* if the domain of the vector field is simply connected. If the domain has "holes" (e.g., $\mathbb{R}^2$ excluding the origin), a field can have zero curl everywhere it's defined but still not be conservative.
2.  **Incorrectly Calculating Partial Derivatives for Curl:** A single mistake in differentiating $P, Q,$ or $R$ with respect to the wrong variable will lead to an incorrect curl calculation and thus a wrong conclusion about conservatism. Double-check all partial derivatives.
3.  **Missing the Constant of Integration when Finding Potential:** When integrating to find $f(x,y,z)$, remember that the "constant" of integration is actually a function of the other variables (e.g., $g(y,z)$ when integrating with respect to $x$). Forgetting this or treating it as a simple constant will lead to an incorrect or incomplete potential function.
4.  **Applying the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals to Non-Conservative Fields:** This is a major error. The theorem $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{r}(b)) - f(\mathbf{r}(a))$ is *only* valid if $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative. Always perform the conservatism check first. If the field is not conservative, you *must* evaluate the line integral by parameterizing the curve.
5.  **Confusing Potential Function $f$ with Potential Energy $U$ (Physics Convention):** In physics, potential energy $U$ is often defined such that $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U$. In mathematics, the potential function $f$ is typically defined such that $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$. Be aware of the sign difference, especially if transitioning between math and physics contexts.
6.  **Assuming Path Independence Implies Zero Work on a Closed Loop (or vice-versa) Without Understanding the Equivalence:** While it's true that path independence implies zero work around any closed loop, and vice-versa, students sometimes treat these as separate facts. Understanding that they are equivalent conditions for a conservative field is key.

## 7. Textbook-precise explanation

Let $D$ be an open region in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$. Let $\mathbf{F}$ be a vector field defined on $D$, given by $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = P(x,y)\mathbf{i} + Q(x,y)\mathbf{j}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = P(x,y,z)\mathbf{i} + Q(x,y,z)\mathbf{j} + R(x,y,z)\mathbf{k}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

**Definition 1: Conservative Vector Field**
A vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is called **conservative** on an open region $D$ if there exists a scalar function $f$ such that $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$ for all points in $D$. The function $f$ is called a **potential function** for $\mathbf{F}$.

**Theorem 1: Equivalence of Path Independence and Conservatism**
Let $\mathbf{F}$ be a vector field whose components are continuous on an open, connected region $D$. The following statements are equivalent:
a) $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative on $D$.
b) The line integral $\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ is independent of the path $C$ in $D$ connecting any two points.
c) The line integral $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0$ for every closed path $C$ in $D$.

**Theorem 2: Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals**
Let $\mathbf{F}$ be a conservative vector field on an open region $D$, and let $f$ be a potential function for $\mathbf{F}$ (i.e., $\mathbf{F} = \nabla f$). If $C$ is a piecewise-smooth curve in $D$ parametrized by $\mathbf{r}(t)$ for $a \le t \le b$, then:
$$ \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{r}(b)) - f(\mathbf{r}(a)) $$
(See: Stewart, Calculus, 9e, §16.3; Marsden & Tromba, Vector Calculus, 6e, §6.3)

**Definition 2: Simply Connected Region**
An open region $D$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$ is called **simply connected** if every simple closed curve in $D$ encloses only points that are in $D$. Informally, a simply connected region has no "holes" in it. For example, $\mathbb{R}^2$ is simply connected, a disk is simply connected. $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the origin removed is *not* simply connected.

**Theorem 3: Test for Conservative Vector Fields (Curl Test)**
Let $\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j} + R\mathbf{k}$ be a vector field on an open, simply connected region $D$, and suppose $P, Q, R$ have continuous first-order partial derivatives.
a) **In 3D:** $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative if and only if $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ throughout $D$.
    This means:
    $$ \frac{\partial R}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}, \quad \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial R}{\partial x}, \quad \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} $$
b) **In 2D:** If $\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j}$, then $\mathbf{F}$ is conservative if and only if $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}$ throughout $D$.
(See: Stewart, Calculus, 9e, §16.5; Marsden & Tromba, Vector Calculus, 6e, §6.4)

**Procedure for Finding a Potential Function:**
If $\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j} + R\mathbf{k}$ is conservative, then to find $f$ such that $\nabla f = \mathbf{F}$:
1.  Integrate $P$ with respect to $x$: $f(x,y,z) = \int P \, dx + g(y,z)$.
2.  Differentiate this $f$ with respect to $y$ and set it equal to $Q$. This will give an expression for $\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}$.
3.  Integrate this expression for $\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}$ with respect to $y$ to find $g(y,z) = \int \left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}\right) \, dy + h(z)$.
4.  Substitute $g(y,z)$ back into $f(x,y,z)$.
5.  Differentiate the current $f$ with respect to $z$ and set it equal to $R$. This will give an expression for $h'(z)$.
6.  Integrate $h'(z)$ with respect to $z$ to find $h(z) = \int h'(z) \, dz + C$.
7.  Substitute $h(z)$ back into $f(x,y,z)$ to obtain the full potential function.
