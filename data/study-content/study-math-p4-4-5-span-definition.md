## 1. What it is — in plain English

Imagine you have a small set of special building blocks, say, just a few different types of LEGO bricks. You can combine these bricks in many ways: you can stack them, put them side-by-side, and use as many of each type as you want. The "span" of these building blocks is the collection of *all possible structures* you could ever build using only those specific types of bricks.

In the world of mathematics, our "building blocks" are called **vectors**. Instead of physically stacking them, we combine them using two basic operations: scaling (making them longer or shorter, or reversing their direction) and adding them together. This process is called forming a "linear combination."

So, the "span" of a set of vectors is simply **the entire collection of all possible vectors you can create by combining those original vectors through scaling and addition.** It's like having a limited palette of colors (our vectors) and being able to mix them in any proportion (scaling) and combine them (adding) to create every possible shade and hue.

If you have a vector pointing along the x-axis and another along the y-axis, their span would be every single point on the entire 2D plane. You can reach any point by moving some distance along x, and then some distance along y. The "span" is the entire flat surface that these two vectors "stretch" to cover.

Crucially, the "span" always includes the origin (the starting point, or the zero vector) because you can always choose to use zero of each building block, resulting in nothing, or zero.

## 2. Why it matters — real-world applications

Understanding "span" is fundamental because it helps us describe the "reach" or "coverage" of a set of components or influences. It's about defining the entire space of possibilities generated by a limited set of ingredients.

1.  **Computer Graphics and Animation (e.g., Pixar, Unity 3D):** In 3D modeling, complex shapes are often represented as a collection of vertices, edges, and faces. These are essentially vectors in a coordinate system. Animators might define a character's pose by a few key "control vectors" (e.g., vectors representing bone rotations or joint positions). The "span" of these control vectors describes all the possible poses or movements the character can achieve within a certain range. When you see a character move smoothly, it's often an interpolation (a specific linear combination) within the span of its key poses.

2.  **Machine Learning and Data Science (e.g., Google, Netflix):**
    *   **Feature Engineering:** Imagine a dataset where each data point is described by several features (e.g., age, income, education level). Each feature can be thought of as a vector. New, more informative features are often created as linear combinations of existing ones. The "span" of the original features represents the entire space of information that can be extracted from them.
    *   **Principal Component Analysis (PCA):** A core technique for dimensionality reduction. PCA finds a new set of orthogonal vectors (principal components) that "span" the data with the maximum variance. By selecting a subset of these principal components, we effectively find a lower-dimensional subspace (the span of selected components) that captures most of the original data's information, useful for visualization or reducing computational load.

3.  **Physics and Engineering (e.g., NASA, Boeing):**
    *   **Force Analysis:** In mechanics, any force acting on an object in 3D space can be expressed as a linear combination of three fundamental forces acting along the x, y, and z axes. The "span" of these three orthogonal basis vectors is the entire 3D physical space, meaning any force or displacement vector can be described.
    *   **Aerospace Control Systems:** When designing a rocket or an aircraft, engineers need to understand all possible states (position, velocity, orientation) the vehicle can reach given its thrusters and control surfaces. The "span" of the vectors representing the maximum effect of each thruster or control surface defines the "controllable space" of the vehicle. If a desired state is outside this span, it's impossible to reach.

4.  **Signal Processing (e.g., Telecommunications):** Digital signals are often represented as vectors. Techniques like Fourier analysis decompose a complex signal into a sum (linear combination) of simpler sine and cosine waves of different frequencies. The "span" of these fundamental sine and cosine waves is the entire space of signals that can be perfectly reconstructed or analyzed using this method.

## 3. Prerequisites — what you must know first

Before diving deep into "span," ensure you have a solid grasp of these foundational concepts. If any of these feel unfamiliar, pause and review them thoroughly.

*   **Vectors:** An ordered list of numbers (components) representing a magnitude and direction. Geometrically, an arrow starting from the origin.
*   **Scalar:** A single number (like 3, -0.5, $\pi$) that scales vectors.
*   **Scalar Multiplication:** Multiplying a vector by a scalar, which changes its length and potentially its direction (if the scalar is negative).
*   **Vector Addition:** Adding two vectors geometrically (head-to-tail or parallelogram rule) or component-wise.
*   **Linear Combination:** The fundamental operation of combining scalar multiplication and vector addition, in the form $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k$.
*   **Set Theory Notation:** Understanding symbols like $\in$ (is an element of), $\{\dots\}$ (a set), $\forall$ (for all), $\exists$ (there exists).
*   **Field (of Scalars):** The set from which the scalars ($c_i$) are drawn, usually the real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$) in introductory linear algebra.

## 4. The core idea — step by step

Let's build up the concept of "span" piece by piece, starting from the very basics.

### Step 1: What is a Vector?

**Plain English:** Think of a vector as an instruction to move from one point to another, like "go 3 units east and 2 units north." It has both a size (magnitude) and a direction.

**Small Concrete Example:** In a 2D coordinate system, the vector $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ means starting at the origin (0,0), move 3 units along the x-axis and 2 units along the y-axis.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** A vector $\mathbf{v}$ is an element of a vector space $V$. For now, we'll primarily work with vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, which are $n$-tuples of real numbers.
$$ \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n $$

**What could go wrong:** Confusing a vector with a scalar. A scalar is just a number (like 5); a vector is a list of numbers with a direction (like $\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$). They are distinct mathematical objects.

### Step 2: Scalar Multiplication and Vector Addition

**Plain English:** These are our basic "building operations." Scalar multiplication means stretching or shrinking a vector (or flipping its direction). Vector addition means combining two movement instructions to get a single net movement.

**Small Concrete Example:**
*   **Scalar Multiplication:** If $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$, then $2\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \times 3 \\ 2 \times 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$. It's twice as long, in the same direction. If $-1\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$, it's the same length but opposite direction.
*   **Vector Addition:** If $\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$, then $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 1+3 \\ 1+2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$. Geometrically, you place the tail of $\mathbf{v}$ at the head of $\mathbf{u}$, and the sum is the vector from the tail of $\mathbf{u}$ to the head of $\mathbf{v}$.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** For $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_n \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$:
*   **Scalar Multiplication:** $c\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} cv_1 \\ \vdots \\ cv_n \end{pmatrix}$
*   **Vector Addition:** $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} u_1+v_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_n+v_n \end{pmatrix}$

**What could go wrong:** Mixing up component-wise operations. You can only add vectors of the same dimension. You can't multiply two vectors in the same way you multiply two scalars (though dot products and cross products exist, they are different operations).

### Step 3: Linear Combination

**Plain English:** A linear combination is simply any result you get by taking a bunch of vectors, scaling each one by some number, and then adding all the scaled vectors together. It's like following a recipe where you can choose how much of each ingredient to use.

**Small Concrete Example:** Let $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
A linear combination could be:
$3\mathbf{v}_1 + 2\mathbf{v}_2 = 3\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
Another linear combination could be:
$-1\mathbf{v}_1 + 5\mathbf{v}_2 = -1\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 5\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** Given a set of vectors $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ in a vector space $V$, a vector $\mathbf{w}$ is a linear combination of the vectors in $S$ if there exist scalars $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:
$$ \mathbf{w} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k $$

**What could go wrong:** Forgetting that *all* vectors in the combination must be scaled. You can't just add them directly without scalars (though the scalar 1 is implied if not written). Also, the scalars can be any real number, including zero or negative numbers.

### Step 4: The Idea of "Generating" or "Reaching"

**Plain English:** If you have a set of vectors, and you can form *any* linear combination of them, what kind of "space" do all these combinations fill up? This "space" is what we're interested in. It's the set of all points you can "reach" starting from the origin, using only your given vectors and the operations of scaling and adding.

**Small Concrete Example:**
*   If you only have one non-zero vector, say $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Any linear combination is $c\mathbf{v}$. This means you can get $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, etc. All these points lie on a straight line passing through the origin and the point (1,1). So, this single vector "generates" a line.
*   If you have two non-zero vectors that don't point in the same or opposite directions (i.e., they are not scalar multiples of each other), say $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Any linear combination $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{pmatrix}$. You can pick any $c_1, c_2$ you want, so you can reach *any* point $(x,y)$ in the entire 2D plane. These two vectors "generate" the entire $\mathbb{R}^2$ plane.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** The "space" generated by a set of vectors $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ is precisely the set of all possible linear combinations of these vectors.

**What could go wrong:** Thinking that the generated space only includes points "between" the vectors. It includes points "beyond" them (by using large scalars) and in opposite directions (by using negative scalars).

### Step 5: Definition of Span

**Plain English:** The "span" of a set of vectors is the *collection* of all possible vectors that can be formed by taking linear combinations of those vectors. It's the smallest "flat" space (a subspace) that contains all the original vectors and the origin.

**Small Concrete Example:**
*   The span of $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the x-axis.
*   The span of $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the entire $\mathbb{R}^2$ plane.
*   The span of $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the xy-plane (a plane through the origin in 3D space).

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** Let $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ be a set of vectors in a vector space $V$. The **span of $S$**, denoted as $\text{span}(S)$ or $\text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$, is the set of all linear combinations of the vectors in $S$.
$$ \text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\} = \{c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k \mid c_1, \dots, c_k \in \mathbb{R}\} $$
This set forms a **subspace** of $V$. (A subspace is a subset of a vector space that is itself a vector space under the same operations. It must contain the zero vector, be closed under vector addition, and closed under scalar multiplication.)

**What could go wrong:** Confusing the *set of vectors* $S$ with its *span*. $S$ is a finite collection of specific vectors. $\text{span}(S)$ is an infinite collection of *all possible linear combinations* of those vectors, forming a continuous "space."

### Step 6: Geometric Interpretation

**Plain English:** The span always looks like a "flat" geometric object passing through the origin.
*   If you span with one non-zero vector, you get a line through the origin.
*   If you span with two non-zero vectors that don't lie on the same line, you get a plane through the origin.
*   If you span with three non-zero vectors that don't lie on the same plane, you get a 3D space (if you're in $\mathbb{R}^3$).

**Small Concrete Example:**
*   $\text{span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ is a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$ passing through $(0,0)$ and $(1,2)$.
*   $\text{span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ is the xy-plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
*   $\text{span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ is *still* the x-axis in $\mathbb{R}^3$, because $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is just $2 \times \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Adding it doesn't "stretch" the space any further than the first vector already did. This highlights the concept of linear dependence (covered in a later lesson).

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** The span of a set of vectors is the smallest subspace of $V$ that contains all the vectors in the set. It is always a vector subspace.

**What could go wrong:** Assuming that the span of $k$ vectors always results in a $k$-dimensional space. This is only true if the vectors are linearly independent. If they are linearly dependent (meaning one vector can be expressed as a linear combination of the others), then some vectors are redundant, and the dimension of the span will be less than $k$.

### Step 7: Spanning a Vector Space

**Plain English:** If the span of your set of vectors covers the *entire* vector space you're working in (e.g., if a set of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ can generate *any* vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$), then we say that the set of vectors "spans" that entire space. It means those vectors are sufficient to build anything in that space.

**Small Concrete Example:**
*   The set $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ spans $\mathbb{R}^2$, because any vector $\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ can be written as $x\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + y\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
*   The set $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ spans $\mathbb{R}^3$.
*   The set $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ does *not* span $\mathbb{R}^2$. It only spans a line (the line $y=x$), because $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = 2 \times \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so the second vector is redundant.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:** A set of vectors $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ **spans** a vector space $V$ if $\text{span}(S) = V$. This means that for every vector $\mathbf{w} \in V$, there exist scalars $c_1, \dots, c_k \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $\mathbf{w} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k$.

**What could go wrong:** Assuming that simply having enough vectors (e.g., three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$) guarantees they span the entire space. They must also be "different enough" (linearly independent) to cover all directions.

## 5. Worked examples — multiple, with every step shown

### Example 1: Span of a single non-zero vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$

**Problem:** Describe the span of the set $S = \left\{ \mathbf{v}_1 \right\}$ where $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Given:** A single vector $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
**We want:** To describe the set of all possible linear combinations of $\mathbf{v}_1$.

**Step-by-step solution:**

1.  **Recall the definition of span:** The span of a set of vectors is the set of all possible linear combinations of those vectors.
    $$ \text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1\} = \{c_1\mathbf{v}_1 \mid c_1 \in \mathbb{R}\} $$
    *Explanation:* We're applying the definition directly. Since there's only one vector, there's only one scalar $c_1$ to multiply it by.

2.  **Substitute the given vector:**
    $$ \text{span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\} = \left\{ c_1 \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \mid c_1 \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$
    *Explanation:* We've replaced $\mathbf{v}_1$ with its specific components.

3.  **Perform the scalar multiplication:**
    $$ \text{span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 2c_1 \\ c_1 \end{pmatrix} \mid c_1 \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$
    *Explanation:* We're carrying out the operation $c_1 \times \mathbf{v}_1$ component-wise.

4.  **Describe the resulting set geometrically:** The vectors in this set are of the form $(2c_1, c_1)$. Let $x = 2c_1$ and $y = c_1$. We can see that $x = 2y$. This is the equation of a straight line in the $xy$-plane. Since $c_1$ can be any real number, this line extends infinitely in both directions and passes through the origin (when $c_1=0$).
    *Explanation:* We're interpreting the algebraic form of the vectors in the span as a geometric object.

**Final Answer:**
The span of $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ is **the line in $\mathbb{R}^2$ passing through the origin and the point $(2,1)$ (or any scalar multiple of $(2,1)$), described by the equation $x = 2y$.**

**Reflection:** This example is straightforward because a single non-zero vector always spans a line through the origin. The key is to recognize that any point on that line can be reached by simply scaling the original vector.

### Example 2: Span of two linearly independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$

**Problem:** Describe the span of the set $S = \left\{ \mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2 \right\}$ where $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Given:** Two vectors $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
**We want:** To describe the set of all possible linear combinations of $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$.

**Step-by-step solution:**

1.  **Recall the definition of span:** The span of a set of vectors is the set of all possible linear combinations of those vectors.
    $$ \text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\} = \{c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 \mid c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}\} $$
    *Explanation:* We're applying the definition. With two vectors, we have two scalars $c_1$ and $c_2$.

2.  **Substitute the given vectors:**
    $$ \text{span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \right\} = \left\{ c_1 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + c_2 \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \mid c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$
    *Explanation:* Replace $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ with their specific components.

3.  **Perform scalar multiplication and vector addition:**
    $$ \text{span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \right\} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ c_2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \mid c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$
    $$ = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \mid c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$
    *Explanation:* First, we multiply each vector by its respective scalar. Then, we add the resulting scaled vectors component-wise.

4.  **Describe the resulting set geometrically:** The vectors in this set are of the form $(c_1, c_2, 0)$. This means that the first component can be any real number ($c_1$), the second component can be any real number ($c_2$), but the third component *must always be zero*. This describes all points $(x,y,z)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ where $z=0$. This is the equation of the $xy$-plane.
    *Explanation:* We're interpreting the algebraic form. The fixed zero in the third component is the key.

**Final Answer:**
The span of $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ is **the $xy$-plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$, which is the set of all vectors $\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ where $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$.**

**Reflection:** These two vectors are linearly independent and define a plane because they don't point in the same direction and are in a 3D space. The zero in the third component of both original vectors is what constrains the span to a 2D plane within $\mathbb{R}^3$.

### Example 3: Span of two linearly dependent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$

**Problem:** Describe the span of the set $S = \left\{ \mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2 \right\}$ where $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Given:** Two vectors $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
**We want:** To describe the set of all possible linear combinations of $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$.

**Step-by-step solution:**

1.  **Recall the definition of span:**
    $$ \text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\} = \{c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 \mid c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}\} $$
    *Explanation:* Standard application of the definition.

2.  **Substitute the given vectors:**
    $$ \text{span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \right\} = \left\{ c_1 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + c_2 \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \mid c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$
    *Explanation:* Replace $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ with their specific components.

3.  **Perform scalar multiplication and vector addition:**
    $$ \text{span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \right\} = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ 2c_1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 3c_2 \\ 6c_2 \end{pmatrix} \mid c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$
    $$ = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} c_1 + 3c_2 \\ 2c_1 + 6c_2 \end{pmatrix} \mid c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$
    *Explanation:* Execute the scalar multiplication and then the vector addition.

4.  **Observe the relationship between the original vectors:** Notice that $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = 3 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = 3\mathbf{v}_1$. This means $\mathbf{v}_2$ is a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{v}_1$.
    *Explanation:* This is a crucial observation. If one vector is a scalar multiple of another, they point in the same (or opposite) direction, meaning they are linearly dependent.

5.  **Simplify the linear combination using this relationship:**
    Since $\mathbf{v}_2 = 3\mathbf{v}_1$, any linear combination can be rewritten as:
    $$ c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2(3\mathbf{v}_1) $$
    $$ = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + 3c_2\mathbf{v}_1 $$
    $$ = (c_1 + 3c_2)\mathbf{v}_1 $$
    *Explanation:* Substitute the relationship $\mathbf{v}_2 = 3\mathbf{v}_1$ into the linear combination formula. Then, factor out $\mathbf{v}_1$.

6.  **Redefine the scalar:** Let $k = c_1 + 3c_2$. Since $c_1$ and $c_2$ can be any real numbers, their sum $c_1 + 3c_2$ can also be any real number. So, $k \in \mathbb{R}$.
    *Explanation:* We're simplifying the expression. The combination of two arbitrary scalars ($c_1, 3c_2$) is still just an arbitrary scalar ($k$).

7.  **Describe the resulting set:** The span simplifies to $\{k\mathbf{v}_1 \mid k \in \mathbb{R}\}$. This is exactly the definition of the span of a single vector, $\mathbf{v}_1$.
    $$ \text{span}\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \right\} = \left\{ k \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \mid k \in \mathbb{R} \right\} $$
    This represents all scalar multiples of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
    *Explanation:* The problem has reduced to Example 1's form.

**Final Answer:**
The span of $\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$ is **the line in $\mathbb{R}^2$ passing through the origin and the point $(1,2)$ (or any scalar multiple of $(1,2)$), described by the equation $y = 2x$.**

**Reflection:** This example highlights that adding a linearly dependent vector to a set does not increase its span. If one vector is a multiple of another, it's redundant in terms of generating new directions. The span remains the same as if only the independent vector were present.

### Example 4: Determine if a given vector is in the span of a set of vectors

**Problem:** Determine if the vector $\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$ is in the span of the set $S = \left\{ \mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2 \right\}$ where $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ -9 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$.

**Given:** Vectors $\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ -9 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$.
**We want:** To determine if $\mathbf{b}$ can be written as a linear combination of $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$. That is, do scalars $c_1, c_2$ exist such that $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{b}$?

**Step-by-step solution:**

1.  **Set up the linear combination equation:**
    We need to check if there exist scalars $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:
    $$ c_1\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + c_2\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ -9 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} $$
    *Explanation:* This is the direct translation of the question "Is $\mathbf{b}$ in the span of $S$?" into a mathematical equation.

2.  **Convert the vector equation into a system of linear equations:**
    Performing the scalar multiplication and vector addition on the left side:
    $$ \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ -2c_1 \\ c_1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 5c_2 \\ -9c_2 \\ 6c_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} $$
    $$ \begin{pmatrix} c_1 + 5c_2 \\ -2c_1 - 9c_2 \\ c_1 + 6c_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} $$
    Equating the components gives us a system of three linear equations with two unknowns:
    (1) $c_1 + 5c_2 = 7$
    (2) $-2c_1 - 9c_2 = 4$
    (3) $c_1 + 6c_2 = -3$
    *Explanation:* This is the standard procedure for solving problems involving linear combinations. We transform the vector equation into a more familiar system of equations.

3.  **Solve the system of linear equations:** We can use methods like substitution or elimination. Let's use elimination.

    *   **From (1) and (2):** Multiply equation (1) by 2 and add it to equation (2):
        $2(c_1 + 5c_2) = 2(7) \Rightarrow 2c_1 + 10c_2 = 14$
        $(-2c_1 - 9c_2) + (2c_1 + 10c_2) = 4 + 14$
        $c_2 = 18$
        *Explanation:* We eliminate $c_1$ to find a value for $c_2$.

    *   **Substitute $c_2 = 18$ into equation (1):**
        $c_1 + 5(18) = 7$
        $c_1 + 90 = 7$
        $c_1 = 7 - 90$
        $c_1 = -83$
        *Explanation:* Now that we have $c_2$, we can find $c_1$ using one of the original equations.

    *   **Check consistency with the third equation (3):** We must verify if these values of $c_1$ and $c_2$ satisfy all three equations. Substitute $c_1 = -83$ and $c_2 = 18$ into equation (3):
        $c_1 + 6c_2 = -3$
        $(-83) + 6(18) = -3$
        $-83 + 108 = -3$
        $25 = -3$
        *Explanation:* This is the critical step. If the system is consistent, the values of $c_1$ and $c_2$ must satisfy *all* equations.

4.  **Conclusion:** The statement $25 = -3$ is false. This means there are no scalars $c_1$ and $c_2$ that can satisfy all three equations simultaneously. Therefore, the vector $\mathbf{b}$ cannot be written as a linear combination of $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$.

**Final Answer:**
The vector $\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$ **is NOT in the span of** $S = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ -9 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \right\}$.

**Reflection:** This example demonstrates the practical application of the span definition. It shows that not every vector in the larger space ($\mathbb{R}^3$ in this case) is necessarily reachable by a smaller set of vectors. The trickiest part is ensuring consistency across *all* equations in the system. If even one equation is not satisfied, the vector is not in the span. Geometrically, this means $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ define a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and $\mathbf{b}$ lies off that plane.

## 6. Common mistakes and traps

1.  **Confusing the set of spanning vectors with the span itself:** Students often think of $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2\}$ as the span. But $S$ is just a finite set of specific vectors; the span is the *infinite collection* of all linear combinations those vectors can form.
2.  **Forgetting that the zero vector is always in the span:** Since you can always choose all scalars $c_i = 0$, the linear combination $0\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + 0\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}$ is always possible. The span is a subspace, and all subspaces must contain the zero vector.
3.  **Assuming that more vectors always lead to a "larger" span:** If new vectors are linearly dependent on the existing ones (i.e., they are already in the span of the existing vectors), adding them to the set does not increase the span. The "size" or dimension of the span is determined by the number of *linearly independent* vectors.
4.  **Not understanding the geometric interpretation:** Forgetting that the span always forms a "flat" object (a point, line, plane, or hyperplane) that passes through the origin. Forgetting this can lead to incorrect visualizations or conclusions.
5.  **Incorrectly applying scalar multiplication or vector addition:** Basic arithmetic errors or conceptual misunderstandings of these fundamental operations will lead to incorrect linear combinations and thus incorrect spans.
6.  **Confusing "span" with "basis":** While related, they are not the same. A basis is a *linearly independent* set of vectors that spans a space. A spanning set might contain redundant (linearly dependent) vectors, whereas a basis is the most efficient spanning set for a given space.

## 7. Textbook-precise explanation

Let $V$ be a vector space over a field of scalars $F$ (typically $F=\mathbb{R}$ for real vector spaces or $F=\mathbb{C}$ for complex vector spaces).

**Definition:** Let $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ be a non-empty finite set of vectors in $V$. The **span of $S$**, denoted as $\text{span}(S)$ or $\text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$, is defined as the set of all possible linear combinations of the vectors in $S$.
Formally,
$$ \text{span}(S) = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i \mathbf{v}_i \mid c_i \in F \text{ for } i=1, \dots, k \right\} $$
If $S$ is the empty set $\emptyset$, its span is defined as the zero vector space, i.e., $\text{span}(\emptyset) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$.

**Properties:**
1.  **Subspace Property:** The span of any non-empty set of vectors $S$ in a vector space $V$ is always a subspace of $V$. This means $\text{span}(S)$ satisfies the three axioms of a subspace:
    *   It contains the zero vector ($\mathbf{0} \in \text{span}(S)$).
    *   It is closed under vector addition (if $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{w} \in \text{span}(S)$, then $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w} \in \text{span}(S)$).
    *   It is closed under scalar multiplication (if $\mathbf{u} \in \text{span}(S)$ and $c \in F$, then $c\mathbf{u} \in \text{span}(S)$).
2.  **Smallest Subspace:** $\text{span}(S)$ is the smallest subspace of $V$ that contains all the vectors in $S$. Any other subspace of $V$ that contains $S$ must also contain $\text{span}(S)$.

**Spanning a Vector Space:**
A set of vectors $S = \{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ **spans** (or **generates**) a vector space $V$ if $\text{span}(S) = V$. This means that every vector in $V$ can be expressed as a linear combination of the vectors in $S$.

## 9. Memory technique — never forget this

1.  **Mnemonic/Visual Hook:**
    *   **Mnemonic:** "SPANS = Scalar Products AND Sums." This reminds you that the span is built using scalar multiplication and vector addition.
    *   **Visual Hook:** Imagine a **spiderweb**. The central point is the origin. The initial vectors are like the first few strong threads the spider lays out from the center. The "span" is the *entire web* – all the countless other threads and connections the spider can create, using the initial threads as guides, extending outwards in all directions they allow. You can make any point on the web by moving along the initial threads and then across them.

2.  **The 1-3 formulas/facts they MUST overlearn:**
    1.  **Definition:** $\text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\} = \{c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k \mid c_i \in \mathbb{R}\}$ (or $c_i \in F$ for a general field).
    2.  **Subspace Property:** The span of any set of vectors is always a vector subspace. This implies it always contains the zero vector and is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication.
    3.  **Redundancy:** If a vector $\mathbf{v}_j$ is a linear combination of other vectors in the set $S$, then $\text{span}(S)$ is the same as $\text{span}(S \setminus \{\mathbf{v}_j\})$. (Adding redundant vectors doesn't expand the span).

3.  **Spaced-repetition schedule:**
    *   Review the definition and properties: **1 day** after initial learning.
    *   Re-work a few examples and explain the concept in your own words: **3 days** after.
    *   Explain it to someone else (or an imaginary friend) without notes: **7 days** after.
    *   Solve problems requiring determining if a vector is in a span: **16 days** after.
    *   Connect "span" to "basis" and "dimension": **35 days** after.

4.  **The first-principles re-derivation pathway:**
    If you forget the exact definition of "span," go back to "linear combination."
    *   What is a linear combination? It's combining vectors using scalar multiplication and vector addition: $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k$.
    *   What if you want to find *all* possible vectors you can make from a given set $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$? You'd have to consider *every single possible choice* for the scalars $c_1, \dots, c_k$.
    *   The collection of *all* such possible linear combinations is precisely what "span" means. It's the set of everything you can "reach" with those vectors. This thought process leads directly back to the formal definition.

## 10. Connections — what this leads to

The concept of "span" is one of the foundational ideas in linear algebra. Mastering it unlocks understanding of many subsequent, more advanced topics:

*   **Subspaces:** The span of any set of vectors *always* forms a subspace. This is a crucial link, as understanding subspaces is central to vector space theory.
*   **Linear Independence and Dependence:** This concept defines when a set of vectors is "efficient" in its span. If vectors are linearly dependent, some are redundant and don't expand the span. If they are linearly independent, each vector contributes a new "direction" to the span.
*   **Basis:** A **basis** for a vector space (or subspace) is a set of vectors that is both **linearly independent** and **spans** the entire space. The idea of span is half of the definition of a basis.
*   **Dimension:** The **dimension** of a vector space is the number of vectors in any basis for that space. Understanding span is necessary to grasp how many "independent directions" are needed to cover a space.
*   **Column Space of a Matrix:** The column space of a matrix $A$ is defined as the span of its column vectors. This is a fundamental subspace associated with a matrix, directly related to the existence of solutions to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$.
*   **Row Space of a Matrix:** Similarly, the row space of a matrix $A$ is the span of its row vectors.
*   **Null Space (Kernel):** While not a direct span of input vectors, the null space is intimately related to the column space and provides another way to understand the "reach" of a linear transformation.
*   **Rank of a Matrix:** The rank of a matrix is the dimension of its column space (or row space), which is the dimension of the space spanned by its column (or row) vectors.
*   **Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues:** These concepts describe special vectors that are only scaled (not rotated) by a linear transformation. Understanding the span helps in visualizing the subspaces associated with eigenvectors.
*   **Change of Basis:** When you change the basis of a vector space, you're essentially choosing a different set of vectors that still span the *same* space, but might offer a more convenient representation.
