## 1. What it is — in plain English

Imagine you're comparing two things, like the height of two friends or the price of two items. Sometimes they are exactly the same, like two identical twins having the same height. In math, we use an "equals" sign ($=$) for that.

But most of the time, things aren't exactly equal. One friend might be taller than the other, or one item might be cheaper. When we want to show that one thing is *not* equal to another, but rather bigger or smaller, we use something called an "inequality." It's like saying, "This side is heavier than that side," or "This number is less than that number."

So, an inequality is a mathematical statement that shows a relationship between two expressions that are *not necessarily equal*. Instead of saying "$x$ *is* 5," we might say "$x$ *is greater than* 5," or "$x$ *is less than or equal to* 5." This means $x$ could be many different numbers, not just one specific value.

Think of it like a balance scale that isn't perfectly balanced. One side is heavier, meaning it has a greater "value" or "weight" than the other. Inequalities help us describe this imbalance using special symbols.

## 2. Why it matters — real-world applications

Inequalities are everywhere in the real world, helping us set limits, define boundaries, and make decisions.

1.  **Engineering and Design (Aerospace):** When designing an airplane wing, engineers must ensure that the stress on any part of the wing ($S$) remains *below* a certain maximum allowable stress ($S_{max}$) to prevent structural failure. This is expressed as $S < S_{max}$. Similarly, the lift generated by the wing ($L$) must be *greater than or equal to* the weight of the aircraft ($W$) for it to fly: $L \ge W$. These inequalities are critical for safety and performance in aerospace.

2.  **Finance and Budgeting:** If you have a budget of \$500 for groceries for the month, the total amount you spend ($C$) must be *less than or equal to* \$500: $C \le 500$. Companies use inequalities to ensure expenses stay within budget, or to calculate how many units of a product they need to sell to make a profit (revenue must be *greater than* costs).

3.  **Machine Learning and AI:** In classification tasks, machine learning models often use inequalities to make decisions. For example, a spam filter might classify an email as spam if its "spam score" ($S$) is *greater than* a certain threshold ($T$): $S > T$. If $S \le T$, it's considered not spam. This simple logic, based on inequalities, forms the backbone of many AI decision-making processes.

4.  **Physics and Safety Regulations:** Speed limits are classic examples of inequalities. If the speed limit on a highway is 65 mph, your speed ($v$) must be *less than or equal to* 65 mph: $v \le 65$. In physics, the range of a projectile might be limited by factors like initial velocity and air resistance, leading to inequalities describing its possible landing points.

## 3. Prerequisites — what you must know first

Before diving deep into inequalities, ensure you have a solid grasp of these fundamental algebraic concepts:

*   **Numbers and Operations:** Comfort with integers, rational numbers (fractions and decimals), and performing basic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) accurately.
*   **Order of Operations (PEMDAS/BODMAS):** Knowing the correct sequence to evaluate expressions to avoid calculation errors.
*   **Variables and Expressions:** Understanding what a variable is (a symbol representing an unknown value) and how to work with algebraic expressions (combinations of variables, numbers, and operations).
*   **Solving Linear Equations:** The ability to isolate a variable in an equation using inverse operations, like $2x + 3 = 7$. This is the direct precursor to solving linear inequalities.
*   **Number Line Representation:** Familiarity with how to plot individual numbers on a number line, which is crucial for visualizing the solution sets of inequalities.

## 4. The core idea — step by step

The core idea behind solving linear inequalities is remarkably similar to solving linear equations. We want to isolate the variable. However, there's one critical difference that we'll explore.

### Step 1: Understanding Inequality Symbols

**Plain English:** These are the special symbols that tell us how two things compare in terms of size or value. They replace the equals sign when things aren't perfectly balanced.

**Example:**
Imagine you have 5 apples and your friend has 3 apples.
*   You have *more than* your friend.
*   Your friend has *less than* you.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:**
We use four primary symbols:
*   $<$ : "less than"
*   $>$ : "greater than"
*   $\le$ : "less than or equal to"
*   $\ge$ : "greater than or equal to"

Sometimes you might also see $\ne$ for "not equal to", but it's less common in the context of linear inequalities as it represents two separate conditions ($<$ or $>$).

**What could go wrong:** Confusing the direction of the symbols. A simple trick is to remember that the "alligator mouth" always eats the bigger number. So, $5 > 3$ means 5 is greater than 3.

### Step 2: What a Solution Means for an Inequality

**Plain English:** For an equation like $x = 5$, there's only one number that makes it true. For an inequality, there's usually a whole bunch of numbers that make it true. This collection of numbers is called the "solution set."

**Example:**
Consider the inequality $x > 3$.
*   If $x=4$, then $4 > 3$, which is true. So, 4 is a solution.
*   If $x=100$, then $100 > 3$, which is true. So, 100 is a solution.
*   If $x=3.0001$, then $3.0001 > 3$, which is true. So, 3.0001 is a solution.
*   If $x=3$, then $3 > 3$, which is false. So, 3 is *not* a solution.
*   If $x=0$, then $0 > 3$, which is false. So, 0 is *not* a solution.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:**
The **solution set** of an inequality is the set of all real numbers that, when substituted for the variable, make the inequality a true statement. Unlike equations which often have a finite number of solutions, linear inequalities typically have an infinite number of solutions.

**What could go wrong:** Thinking there's only one answer, like in an equation. Remember, an inequality describes a *range* of values.

### Step 3: Properties of Inequality — The "Same Operation" Rule

**Plain English:** Just like with equations, we can do the same thing to both sides of an inequality (add, subtract, multiply, divide) to try and get the variable by itself. Most of the time, this doesn't change the direction of the inequality symbol.

**Example:**
If you know that $5 < 7$:
*   Add 2 to both sides: $5+2 < 7+2 \implies 7 < 9$ (Still true!)
*   Subtract 3 from both sides: $5-3 < 7-3 \implies 2 < 4$ (Still true!)
*   Multiply by 2 (a positive number): $5 \times 2 < 7 \times 2 \implies 10 < 14$ (Still true!)
*   Divide by 1 (a positive number): $5/1 < 7/1 \implies 5 < 7$ (Still true!)

**Formal/Mathematical Version:**
Let $a, b, c$ be real numbers.
1.  **Addition Property:** If $a < b$, then $a+c < b+c$. (Also true for $>, \le, \ge$)
2.  **Subtraction Property:** If $a < b$, then $a-c < b-c$. (Also true for $>, \le, \ge$)
3.  **Multiplication/Division Property (Positive Number):** If $a < b$ and $c > 0$, then $ac < bc$ and $\frac{a}{c} < \frac{b}{c}$. (Also true for $>, \le, \ge$)

**What could go wrong:** Forgetting that these properties only hold when you're adding/subtracting any number, or multiplying/dividing by a *positive* number. The next step is where things get tricky.

### Step 4: The Critical Difference — Multiplying or Dividing by a Negative Number

**Plain English:** This is the *most important rule* to remember for inequalities! If you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality by a *negative* number, you *must* flip the direction of the inequality symbol. If you don't, your answer will be completely wrong.

**Example:**
Let's start with a true statement: $2 < 5$.
*   Multiply by -1:
    *   Left side: $2 \times (-1) = -2$
    *   Right side: $5 \times (-1) = -5$
    *   Now compare -2 and -5. On a number line, -2 is to the right of -5, so $-2 > -5$.
    *   Notice the symbol flipped from $<$ to $>$.

If we didn't flip it, we'd get $-2 < -5$, which is false!

**Formal/Mathematical Version:**
Let $a, b, c$ be real numbers.
1.  **Multiplication Property (Negative Number):** If $a < b$ and $c < 0$, then $ac > bc$. (Notice the flip from $<$ to $>$. Also true for $>, \le, \ge$ where the symbol flips to its opposite).
2.  **Division Property (Negative Number):** If $a < b$ and $c < 0$, then $\frac{a}{c} > \frac{b}{c}$. (Notice the flip from $<$ to $>$. Also true for $>, \le, \ge$ where the symbol flips to its opposite).

**What could go wrong:** Forgetting to flip the sign! This is the single most common error students make when solving inequalities. Always double-check if you've multiplied or divided by a negative number.

### Step 5: Representing Solutions on a Number Line

**Plain English:** Once you've solved an inequality, you have a range of numbers. A number line is a great visual way to show exactly which numbers are included in your solution set.

**Example:**
If your solution is $x > 3$:
*   We need to show all numbers to the right of 3.
*   Since $x$ cannot be *equal* to 3 (it's strictly greater than), we use an "open circle" at 3.
*   Then we draw an arrow extending to the right, indicating all numbers larger than 3.

If your solution is $x \le 2$:
*   We need to show all numbers to the left of 2, including 2 itself.
*   Since $x$ *can* be equal to 2, we use a "closed circle" (or filled-in dot) at 2.
*   Then we draw an arrow extending to the left, indicating all numbers smaller than or equal to 2.

**Formal/Mathematical Version:**
*   For strict inequalities ($<$ or $>$), use an **open circle** (or parenthesis in interval notation) on the number line at the boundary point. This indicates the boundary point itself is *not* included in the solution set.
*   For non-strict inequalities ($\le$ or $\ge$), use a **closed circle** (or square bracket in interval notation) on the number line at the boundary point. This indicates the boundary point *is* included in the solution set.
*   Shade the portion of the number line that corresponds to the solution. An arrow indicates the solution extends infinitely in that direction.

**Interval Notation:** This is another way to write solution sets, often used in higher math.
*   $x > 3$ is written as $(3, \infty)$. The parenthesis means "not including 3". $\infty$ (infinity) always gets a parenthesis.
*   $x \le 2$ is written as $(-\infty, 2]$. The bracket means "including 2". $-\infty$ (negative infinity) always gets a parenthesis.

**What could go wrong:** Mixing up open/closed circles or parentheses/brackets. Remember: "equal to" means "closed/bracket," "not equal to" means "open/parenthesis."

### Step 6: Solving Multi-Step Linear Inequalities

**Plain English:** This is where we combine all the previous steps. You treat an inequality almost exactly like an equation, using inverse operations to get the variable alone. Just remember that one crucial rule about flipping the sign.

**Example:**
Solve $2x - 4 \ge 6$.
1.  Add 4 to both sides: $2x \ge 10$. (Did not multiply/divide by negative, so sign stays)
2.  Divide by 2 (a positive number): $x \ge 5$. (Did not multiply/divide by negative, so sign stays)

**Formal/Mathematical Version:**
To solve a linear inequality of the form $Ax + B \lesseqqgtr C$:
1.  Use the addition or subtraction property of inequality to move constant terms to one side and variable terms to the other.
2.  Use the multiplication or division property of inequality to isolate the variable. Remember to reverse the inequality symbol if you multiply or divide by a negative number.
3.  Represent the solution on a number line and/or using interval notation.

**What could go wrong:** Forgetting to apply operations to *both* sides of the inequality. Also, making arithmetic errors.

## 5. Worked examples — multiple, with every step shown

### Example 1: Basic Addition/Subtraction

**Problem:** Solve the inequality $x - 7 < 10$ and represent the solution on a number line.

**Given:** An inequality $x - 7 < 10$.
**Want:** The solution set for $x$, and its representation on a number line.

$$x - 7 < 10$$
$$x - 7 + 7 < 10 + 7$$
This step adds 7 to both sides of the inequality. We do this to isolate the variable $x$ on the left side. Adding a number to both sides does not change the direction of the inequality sign.
$$x < 17$$
This is our solution. Any number less than 17 will satisfy the original inequality.

**Number Line Representation:**
```text
<-------------------------------------------------------------------->
          0         5        10        15   ( 17 )      20
                                            <------
                                            (Open circle at 17, arrow to the left)
```
**Interval Notation:** $(-\infty, 17)$

**Reflection:** This was a straightforward example. The key was simply applying the addition property of inequality. No sign flipping was necessary because we only added, not multiplied or divided.

### Example 2: Basic Multiplication/Division (Positive Coefficient)

**Problem:** Solve the inequality $3y \ge 12$ and represent the solution on a number line.

**Given:** An inequality $3y \ge 12$.
**Want:** The solution set for $y$, and its representation on a number line.

$$3y \ge 12$$
$$\frac{3y}{3} \ge \frac{12}{3}$$
This step divides both sides of the inequality by 3. We do this to isolate $y$. Since we are dividing by a positive number (3), the direction of the inequality sign remains the same.
$$y \ge 4$$
This is our solution. Any number greater than or equal to 4 will satisfy the original inequality.

**Number Line Representation:**
```text
<-------------------------------------------------------------------->
          0         1         2         3  [ 4 )------->
                                                (Closed circle at 4, arrow to the right)
```
**Interval Notation:** $[4, \infty)$

**Reflection:** Still relatively simple, but introduced multiplication/division. The crucial point here was recognizing that dividing by a positive number means the inequality sign does *not* flip.

### Example 3: Basic Multiplication/Division (Negative Coefficient)

**Problem:** Solve the inequality $-2z < 8$ and represent the solution on a number line.

**Given:** An inequality $-2z < 8$.
**Want:** The solution set for $z$, and its representation on a number line.

$$-2z < 8$$
$$\frac{-2z}{-2} > \frac{8}{-2}$$
This step divides both sides of the inequality by -2. We do this to isolate $z$. **Crucially, because we are dividing by a negative number (-2), we must reverse the direction of the inequality sign.** The original $<$ becomes $>$.
$$z > -4$$
This is our solution. Any number greater than -4 will satisfy the original inequality.

**Number Line Representation:**
```text
<-------------------------------------------------------------------->
         -5        -4        -3        -2        -1         0
                   ( )--------------------------------------->
                   (Open circle at -4, arrow to the right)
```
**Interval Notation:** $(-4, \infty)$

**Reflection:** This example highlights the most common trap: flipping the inequality sign. It's easy to forget, but essential for a correct answer. Always pause and check if you're multiplying or dividing by a negative number.

### Example 4: Multi-Step Inequality with Variables on Both Sides

**Problem:** Solve the inequality $5x - 3 \le 2x + 9$ and represent the solution using interval notation.

**Given:** An inequality $5x - 3 \le 2x + 9$.
**Want:** The solution set for $x$, and its representation in interval notation.

$$5x - 3 \le 2x + 9$$
$$5x - 3 - 2x \le 2x + 9 - 2x$$
First, we want to gather all terms with $x$ on one side. We subtract $2x$ from both sides. Subtracting does not change the inequality sign.
$$3x - 3 \le 9$$
$$3x - 3 + 3 \le 9 + 3$$
Next, we want to gather all constant terms on the other side. We add 3 to both sides. Adding does not change the inequality sign.
$$3x \le 12$$
$$\frac{3x}{3} \le \frac{12}{3}$$
Finally, we divide both sides by 3 to isolate $x$. Since 3 is a positive number, the inequality sign remains unchanged.
$$x \le 4$$
This is our solution.

**Interval Notation:** $(-\infty, 4]$

**Reflection:** This example required multiple steps, similar to solving a multi-step equation. No sign flipping was needed because all divisions/multiplications were by positive numbers. The strategy is to move variable terms to one side and constant terms to the other, then isolate the variable.

### Example 5: Inequality with Fractions and Negative Multiplication

**Problem:** Solve the inequality $\frac{1}{2}(4x - 6) > -\frac{1}{3}(9x + 3)$ and represent the solution on a number line.

**Given:** An inequality $\frac{1}{2}(4x - 6) > -\frac{1}{3}(9x + 3)$.
**Want:** The solution set for $x$, and its representation on a number line.

$$\frac{1}{2}(4x - 6) > -\frac{1}{3}(9x + 3)$$
$$2x - 3 > -3x - 1$$
First, distribute the constants into the parentheses on both sides. $\frac{1}{2} \times 4x = 2x$, $\frac{1}{2} \times -6 = -3$. On the right, $-\frac{1}{3} \times 9x = -3x$, and $-\frac{1}{3} \times 3 = -1$. Distribution does not change the inequality sign.
$$2x - 3 + 3x > -3x - 1 + 3x$$
Now, collect all terms with $x$ on one side. Add $3x$ to both sides. Adding does not change the inequality sign.
$$5x - 3 > -1$$
$$5x - 3 + 3 > -1 + 3$$
Next, collect all constant terms on the other side. Add 3 to both sides. Adding does not change the inequality sign.
$$5x > 2$$
$$\frac{5x}{5} > \frac{2}{5}$$
Finally, divide both sides by 5 to isolate $x$. Since 5 is a positive number, the inequality sign remains unchanged.
$$x > \frac{2}{5}$$
This is our solution.

**Number Line Representation:**
```text
<-------------------------------------------------------------------->
          0    (2/5)       1
               ( )--------->
               (Open circle at 2/5, arrow to the right)
```
**Interval Notation:** $(\frac{2}{5}, \infty)$

**Reflection:** This example combined distribution, collecting like terms, and working with fractions. Although a negative number was involved in the initial distribution, we did not multiply or divide the *entire inequality* by a negative number in the isolation steps, so the sign never flipped. This emphasizes that the sign flip only occurs when the *entire inequality* is multiplied or divided by a negative number.

## 6. Common mistakes and traps

1.  **Forgetting to flip the inequality sign:** This is by far the most common and significant error. When multiplying or dividing *both sides* of an inequality by a negative number, the inequality symbol MUST be reversed.
2.  **Arithmetic errors:** Simple addition, subtraction, multiplication, or division mistakes, especially with negative numbers, can lead to incorrect solutions.
3.  **Incorrectly distributing negative signs:** When an expression like $-(x-3)$ appears, students sometimes forget to distribute the negative to *both* terms, resulting in $-x-3$ instead of $-x+3$.
4.  **Misinterpreting "equal to" in symbols:** Confusing $<$ with $\le$ or $>$ with $\ge$. This impacts whether the boundary point is included in the solution and thus whether to use an open or closed circle (or parenthesis/bracket) in the representation.
5.  **Shading the wrong direction on the number line:** After solving for $x$, say $x < 5$, some students might shade to the right (greater than) instead of the left (less than).
6.  **Writing interval notation incorrectly:** Forgetting that infinity symbols always use parentheses, or mixing up parentheses and brackets for finite endpoints.

## 7. Textbook-precise explanation

An **inequality** is a mathematical statement that compares two expressions, indicating that one is less than, greater than, less than or equal to, or greater than or equal to the other. Unlike an equation, which asserts equality, an inequality asserts a relative order.

**Basic Inequality Symbols:**
*   $a < b$: $a$ is less than $b$.
*   $a > b$: $a$ is greater than $b$.
*   $a \le b$: $a$ is less than or equal to $b$.
*   $a \ge b$: $a$ is greater than or equal to $b$.

A **linear inequality** in one variable is an inequality that can be written in the form $Ax + B < C$, $Ax + B > C$, $Ax + B \le C$, or $Ax + B \ge C$, where $A, B, C$ are real numbers and $A \ne 0$.

The **solution set** of an inequality is the set of all real numbers that satisfy the inequality.

**Properties of Inequalities:**
Let $a, b, c$ be real numbers.

1.  **Addition Property:** If $a < b$, then $a+c < b+c$. (This property also holds for $>, \le, \ge$)
2.  **Subtraction Property:** If $a < b$, then $a-c < b-c$. (This property also holds for $>, \le, \ge$)
3.  **Multiplication Property:**
    *   If $a < b$ and $c > 0$, then $ac < bc$.
    *   If $a < b$ and $c < 0$, then $ac > bc$. (The inequality symbol reverses.)
    (These properties also hold for $>, \le, \ge$, with the symbol reversing when multiplied by a negative number.)
4.  **Division Property:**
    *   If $a < b$ and $c > 0$, then $\frac{a}{c} < \frac{b}{c}$.
    *   If $a < b$ and $c < 0$, then $\frac{a}{c} > \frac{b}{c}$. (The inequality symbol reverses.)
    (These properties also hold for $>, \le, \ge$, with the symbol reversing when divided by a negative number.)

**Solving Linear Inequalities:**
To solve a linear inequality, one applies the properties of inequalities to isolate the variable, similar to solving linear equations. The crucial distinction is the reversal of the inequality symbol when multiplying or dividing by a negative number.

**Representation of Solution Sets:**

1.  **Number Line:**
    *   An **open circle** (or parenthesis) at a boundary point indicates that the point is *not* included in the solution (for $<$ or $>$).
    *   A **closed circle** (or square bracket) at a boundary point indicates that the point *is* included in the solution (for $\le$ or $\ge$).
    *   The appropriate portion of the number line is shaded to show the range of values in the solution set. An arrow indicates extension to infinity.

2.  **Interval Notation:** A concise way to express the solution set.
    *   Parentheses `()` denote that an endpoint is *not* included (for $<$ or $>$).
    *   Square brackets `[]` denote that an endpoint *is* included (for $\le$ or $\ge$).
    *   Infinity symbols ($\infty, -\infty$) always use parentheses.
    *   Examples:
        *   $x > a \implies (a, \infty)$
        *   $x < a \implies (-\infty, a)$
        *   $x \ge a \implies [a, \infty)$
        *   $x \le a \implies (-\infty, a]$

*Reference: Stewart, Calculus: Early Transcendentals, 9e, Appendix A.1, "Real Numbers and Inequalities"*

## 9. Memory technique — never forget this

1.  **Mnemonic/Visual Hook:**
    *   **The "Negative Ninja Flip":** Imagine a ninja (the negative number you're multiplying or dividing by) doing a backflip! When the ninja performs its action, the inequality sign also does a flip. If you're not dealing with a negative ninja (i.e., adding, subtracting, or multiplying/dividing by a positive number), the sign stays put.
    *   **The "Balance Scale with Weights":** Picture a balance scale. If you add or remove equal weights from both sides, the balance (or imbalance) stays the same. If you multiply or divide the weights by a positive number, the balance also stays the same. But if you try to make everything "negative" (e.g., imagine owing money instead of having it), the "heavier" side might become the "lighter" side in terms of debt. If you owe \$5 and your friend owes \$2, your friend's debt is "less negative" (closer to zero) than yours. If you compare $5 > 2$, then multiplying by $-1$ gives $-5 < -2$. The relationship flips.

2.  **The 1-3 formulas/facts they MUST overlearn:**
    *   **The "Flip Rule":** When multiplying or dividing *both sides* of an inequality by a negative number, *always reverse the inequality sign*.
    *   **Open vs. Closed:** Use open circles/parentheses for strict inequalities ($<, >$) and closed circles/brackets for inclusive inequalities ($\le, \ge$).
    *   **Isolate the Variable:** The overall goal is the same as equations: get the variable by itself.

3.  **Spaced-repetition schedule:**
    *   Review the concept and the "Flip Rule" today (Day 0).
    *   Review again in **1 day**.
    *   Review again in **3 days**.
    *   Review again in **7 days**.
    *   Review again in **16 days**.
    *   Review again in **35 days**.
    *   Practice problems during each review session, specifically focusing on examples that involve multiplying/dividing by negative numbers.

4.  **The first-principles re-derivation pathway:**
    If you forget the "Flip Rule," you can always re-derive it with concrete numbers.
    *   Start with a simple, true inequality, like $2 < 5$.
    *   Perform an operation that involves a negative number, e.g., multiply both sides by $-1$.
    *   $2 \times (-1) = -2$
    *   $5 \times (-1) = -5$
    *   Now, compare the new numbers: Is $-2 < -5$ true? No, $-2$ is greater than $-5$.
    *   Therefore, the original inequality $2 < 5$ must become $-2 > -5$. This demonstrates that the sign *must* flip.
    *   You can do the same with division: Start with $10 > 4$. Divide by $-2$. $10/(-2) = -5$, $4/(-2) = -2$. Is $-5 > -2$? No, $-5 < -2$. So the sign flips.
    This simple numerical test is your ultimate safety net if you ever doubt the rule.

## 10. Connections — what this leads to

Understanding linear inequalities is a foundational skill that unlocks many advanced topics in mathematics:

1.  **Systems of Linear Inequalities:** Just as you can solve systems of linear equations, you can solve systems of linear inequalities. This involves finding the region on a coordinate plane where all inequalities are simultaneously true. This is crucial for optimization problems.
2.  **Linear Programming:** This is a powerful mathematical technique used to optimize (maximize or minimize) a linear objective function, subject to a set of linear inequality constraints. It's widely used in business, economics, engineering, and logistics (e.g., optimizing resource allocation, production schedules, or delivery routes).
3.  **Absolute Value Inequalities:** Inequalities involving absolute values (e.g., $|x-3| < 5$) often break down into compound linear inequalities.
4.  **Quadratic and Polynomial Inequalities:** Extending the concepts to higher-degree polynomials (e.g., $x^2 - 4 < 0$) requires understanding critical points and testing intervals, building directly on the number line representation.
5.  **Functions and Domains/Ranges:** Inequalities are used to define the domain (input values) and range (output values) of functions, especially when dealing with square roots, logarithms, or rational functions where certain values might be undefined.
6.  **Calculus:** Inequalities are fundamental in calculus for:
    *   **Limits:** The formal definition of a limit ($\epsilon-\delta$ definition) is entirely based on inequalities.
    *   **Optimization:** Finding maximum or minimum values of functions, often constrained by inequalities.
    *   **Intervals of Increase/Decrease and Concavity:** Determining where a function is increasing, decreasing, concave up, or concave down involves solving inequalities based on the first and second derivatives.
7.  **Computer Science (Algorithms):** Inequalities are used in algorithms for decision-making, setting thresholds, defining loops (e.g., `while (i < n)`), and analyzing computational complexity (e.g., an algorithm runs in time $O(n^2)$, meaning its runtime is *less than or equal to* a constant times $n^2$).
