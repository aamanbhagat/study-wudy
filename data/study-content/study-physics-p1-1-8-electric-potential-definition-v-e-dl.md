## 1. What it is — in plain English

Imagine you're on a hill. The higher you are, the more "potential" you have to roll down or for something to fall. This "height" isn't energy itself, but it tells you how much energy a specific object (like a ball) would have if placed there. Electric potential is exactly like that, but for electricity.

Instead of height in a gravity field, electric potential (often called "voltage") is like an "electric height" in an electric field. It's a property of a location in space, telling you how much electric potential energy a *unit* of electric charge would have if placed at that spot.

So, if you have a positive charge, it's like a ball on a hill. It "wants" to roll from a high electric potential (high "electric height") to a low electric potential (low "electric height"). A negative charge, however, is like a special anti-gravity ball that wants to roll *uphill* to higher electric potentials!

The formal definition we're studying, $V = -\int E \cdot dl$, is simply the mathematical way of calculating this "electric height difference" between two points. It tells you how much "push" or "pull" from the electric field you accumulate as you move a tiny step from one point to another, and then sums all those tiny pushes/pulls along a path. The negative sign is crucial: it means moving *with* the electric field (downhill for positive charges) decreases the potential, while moving *against* it (uphill) increases the potential.

## 2. Why it matters — real-world applications

Electric potential is not just an abstract concept; it's the bedrock of almost all modern technology. Understanding it is fundamental to grasping how electricity works in the real world.

1.  **Batteries and Power Supplies**: Every battery, from the AA in your remote to the massive packs in electric vehicles (like those from Tesla or Rivian), creates a potential difference (voltage) between its terminals. This potential difference is the "push" that drives electrons through circuits, powering devices. The voltage rating (e.g., 1.5V, 9V, 12V, 400V) tells you the electric potential difference available to do work.

2.  **Microelectronics and Computing**: The transistors in your computer's CPU and GPU (like NVIDIA's H100 or AMD's Instinct MI300X, crucial for AI/ML) operate by switching on and off based on tiny electric potential differences. These potential differences control the flow of electrons, enabling the logic gates that perform calculations. Without precise control over electric potential, modern computing as we know it simply wouldn't exist.

3.  **Particle Accelerators and Ion Thrusters**: In particle accelerators (e.g., CERN's Large Hadron Collider), electric potential differences are used to accelerate charged particles (protons, electrons) to incredibly high speeds, allowing scientists to probe the fundamental nature of matter. Similarly, in aerospace, ion thrusters (like those used on NASA's Dawn spacecraft) utilize electric fields created by potential differences to accelerate ions, providing highly efficient propulsion for deep-space missions.

4.  **Medical Diagnostics**: Techniques like Electrocardiography (ECG) and Electroencephalography (EEG) measure the tiny electric potential differences generated by the heart and brain, respectively, on the surface of the body. These potential maps provide vital diagnostic information about organ function, helping doctors detect abnormalities.

5.  **High-Voltage Transmission Lines**: Electrical power generated at power plants (e.g., from GE or Siemens turbines) is transmitted across vast distances at very high voltages (hundreds of kilovolts) to minimize energy loss. Understanding electric potential is critical for designing these systems safely and efficiently, ensuring power reaches homes and industries.

## 3. Prerequisites — what you must know first

Before diving deep into electric potential, ensure you have a solid grasp of these foundational concepts:

*   **Vectors**: Quantities with both magnitude and direction (e.g., force, displacement, electric field).
*   **Electric Field ($\vec{E}$)**: A vector field that describes the electric force per unit charge at all points in space, defined as $\vec{E} = \vec{F}/q$.
*   **Work ($W$)**: The energy transferred by a force acting over a distance. For a constant force, $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}$. For a variable force, $W = \int \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{l}$.
*   **Line Integrals**: A type of integral that calculates the accumulation of a scalar quantity along a specific path or curve. Essential for calculating work done by a variable force.
*   **Conservative Forces**: Forces for which the work done in moving an object between two points is independent of the path taken (e.g., gravity, electrostatic force). This implies that potential energy can be defined.
*   **Potential Energy ($U$)**: Energy stored in an object due to its position or configuration within a force field. For a conservative force, $\Delta U = -W$.
*   **Scalar Product (Dot Product)**: An operation between two vectors that results in a scalar. $\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = |\vec{A}||\vec{B}|\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the vectors.

## 4. The core idea — step by step

Let's build the concept of electric potential from the ground up, step by step.

### ### Step 1: Revisiting Work and Potential Energy (Gravitational Analogy)

*   **Plain-English Statement**: Imagine lifting a book. You do work against gravity. This work isn't lost; it's stored as gravitational potential energy in the book. If you let go, gravity does positive work, and the book's potential energy decreases as it converts to kinetic energy.
*   **Small Concrete Example**: You lift a 1 kg book 1 meter high. The force you apply is roughly $mg = 1 \text{ kg} \times 9.8 \text{ m/s}^2 = 9.8 \text{ N}$. The work you do is $W_{you} = F \times d = 9.8 \text{ N} \times 1 \text{ m} = 9.8 \text{ J}$. This energy is stored as gravitational potential energy, so $\Delta U_g = 9.8 \text{ J}$. The work done *by gravity* is $W_g = -9.8 \text{ J}$.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version**: For any conservative force $\vec{F}$, the work done by the force in moving an object from point A to point B is:
    $$W_{AB} = \int_A^B \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{l}$$
    The change in potential energy, $\Delta U = U_B - U_A$, is defined as the negative of the work done by the conservative force:
    $$\Delta U = -W_{AB} = -\int_A^B \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{l}$$
*   **What Could Go Wrong**: Confusing the work done *by* the field/force with the work done *against* the field/force. If *you* lift the book, *you* do positive work, and the potential energy *increases*. If gravity pulls the book down, gravity does positive work, and the potential energy *decreases*. The definition $\Delta U = -W_{field}$ means potential energy increases when the field does negative work (i.e., you do positive work against it).

### ### Step 2: Work Done by an Electric Field

*   **Plain-English Statement**: Just like gravity pulls on mass, an electric field "pushes" or "pulls" on electric charges. If a charge moves in an electric field, the field does work on it.
*   **Small Concrete Example**: A positive charge $q$ is released in a uniform electric field $\vec{E}$ pointing to the right. The charge will accelerate to the right. The electric field is doing positive work on the charge, increasing its kinetic energy.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version**: The electric force on a test charge $q_0$ is $\vec{F}_E = q_0 \vec{E}$. The work done by the electric field in moving this charge from point A to point B is:
    $$W_{AB} = \int_A^B \vec{F}_E \cdot d\vec{l} = \int_A^B (q_0 \vec{E}) \cdot d\vec{l}$$
*   **What Could Go Wrong**: Forgetting that the force depends on the magnitude and sign of the charge $q_0$. If $q_0$ is negative, the force is in the opposite direction to $\vec{E}$.

### ### Step 3: Electric Potential Energy ($\Delta U_E$)

*   **Plain-English Statement**: If you move a charge against the natural "push" or "pull" of an electric field, you are storing energy in it, just like lifting the book against gravity. This stored energy is called electric potential energy.
*   **Small Concrete Example**: You push a positive charge closer to another positive charge. You have to do work against the repulsive electric field. This work is stored as electric potential energy. If you release it, the stored energy converts to kinetic energy as it flies away.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version**: Following the definition from Step 1, the change in electric potential energy $\Delta U_E$ for a charge $q_0$ moving from A to B is the negative of the work done *by the electric field*:
    $$\Delta U_E = U_B - U_A = -W_{AB} = - \int_A^B (q_0 \vec{E}) \cdot d\vec{l}$$
*   **What Could Go Wrong**: Getting the sign wrong. If the electric field does positive work (e.g., a positive charge moving with the field), the potential energy *decreases* ($\Delta U_E$ is negative). If the electric field does negative work (e.g., a positive charge moving against the field), the potential energy *increases* ($\Delta U_E$ is positive).

### ### Step 4: Defining Electric Potential (V)

*   **Plain-English Statement**: Electric potential energy depends on the charge you're moving. To get a property of the *location itself* (independent of the specific charge), we divide the potential energy by the charge. This gives us "electric potential" or "voltage" – potential energy *per unit charge*.
*   **Small Concrete Example**: If moving a +2 Coulomb charge from point A to point B increases its potential energy by 10 Joules, then the potential difference between B and A is $10 \text{ J} / 2 \text{ C} = 5 \text{ Volts}$. This means any 1 Coulomb charge moving from A to B would gain 5 Joules of potential energy.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version**: Electric potential $V$ is defined as electric potential energy $U_E$ per unit charge $q_0$:
    $$V \equiv \frac{U_E}{q_0}$$
    Therefore, the change in electric potential (potential difference or voltage) between two points A and B is:
    $$\Delta V = V_B - V_A = \frac{U_B - U_A}{q_0} = \frac{\Delta U_E}{q_0}$$
    The unit of electric potential is the Volt (V), where $1 \text{ V} = 1 \text{ Joule/Coulomb} (J/C)$.
*   **What Could Go Wrong**: Confusing electric potential ($V$, a scalar property of space) with electric potential energy ($U_E$, energy possessed by a charge). $V$ is like "electric height," $U_E$ is like "gravitational potential energy" of a specific object at that height.

### ### Step 5: The Definition $V = -\int E \cdot dl$

*   **Plain-English Statement**: Now we combine everything. The "electric height difference" (potential difference) between two points is found by summing up all the tiny "electric pushes" (components of the electric field) you encounter along a path, multiplied by the tiny distance you travel, and then taking the negative of that sum. The negative sign ensures that if the electric field pushes you "downhill" (does positive work), your potential decreases.
*   **Small Concrete Example**: Imagine a uniform electric field pointing downwards. If you move a positive test charge upwards, you are moving against the field. The dot product $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ will be negative (since $\vec{E}$ is down, $d\vec{l}$ is up). Integrating this negative value and then taking the negative of the integral results in a positive potential difference, meaning you've moved to a higher electric potential.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version**: Substitute the expression for $\Delta U_E$ from Step 3 into the definition of $\Delta V$ from Step 4:
    $$\Delta V = V_B - V_A = \frac{\Delta U_E}{q_0} = \frac{- \int_A^B (q_0 \vec{E}) \cdot d\vec{l}}{q_0}$$
    The $q_0$ terms cancel out, leading to the fundamental definition of electric potential difference:
    $$\Delta V = V_B - V_A = - \int_A^B \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$$
    This equation tells us that the potential difference between two points B and A is the negative of the line integral of the electric field from A to B.
*   **What Could Go Wrong**: Forgetting the negative sign is the most common error. Remember: moving *with* the electric field means going to *lower* potential. If $\vec{E}$ and $d\vec{l}$ are in the same direction, $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ is positive, so $-\int (\text{positive}) = \text{negative}$, meaning $V_B < V_A$.

### ### Step 6: Path Independence and Reference Point

*   **Plain-English Statement**: Because the electrostatic force is a conservative force (like gravity), the work done by the electric field (and thus the potential difference) between two points does not depend on the specific path you take to get from one point to the other. It only depends on the starting and ending points. This allows us to define a unique potential for every point in space. To have an absolute value for potential, we pick a "zero" point, much like sea level for gravitational height.
*   **Small Concrete Example**: Whether you climb a mountain straight up or take a winding path, the change in your gravitational potential energy (and thus your height) is the same. Similarly, for electric potential, the "voltage drop" from point A to point B is the same regardless of the route taken through the electric field. A common reference point for $V=0$ is infinity, especially for isolated charges, or the ground for circuit analysis.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version**: Since $\vec{F}_E$ (and thus $\vec{E}$) is a conservative field, the line integral $\int_A^B \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ is path-independent. This means we can define a unique scalar function $V(\vec{r})$ such that $\Delta V = V_B - V_A$.
    Often, we define the electric potential at a point $\vec{r}$ as the work done *by an external agent* per unit charge to bring a test charge from infinity (where $V=0$) to that point, or equivalently, the negative of the work done *by the electric field*:
    $$V(\vec{r}) = - \int_{\infty}^{\vec{r}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$$
*   **What Could Go Wrong**: Assuming that the potential *depends* on the path. This is incorrect for static electric fields. Also, forgetting to define a reference point for absolute potential values. Without a reference, only potential *differences* are meaningful.

## 5. Worked examples — multiple, with every step shown

### Example 1: Uniform Electric Field

**Problem**: A uniform electric field $\vec{E}$ of magnitude $100 \text{ N/C}$ points in the positive x-direction. Calculate the electric potential difference $V_B - V_A$ between point A at $(0,0,0)$ and point B at $(5 \text{ m}, 0,0)$.

**Given**:
*   $\vec{E} = 100 \hat{i} \text{ N/C}$
*   Point A = $(0,0,0)$
*   Point B = $(5,0,0)$

**Want**: $V_B - V_A$

**Solution**:

1.  **State the definition of potential difference**:
    $$V_B - V_A = - \int_A^B \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$$
    This is the fundamental equation we use to find the potential difference.

2.  **Define the electric field vector and differential displacement vector**:
    *   The electric field is given as $\vec{E} = 100 \hat{i}$.
    *   Since we are moving along the x-axis, the differential displacement vector $d\vec{l}$ will be in the x-direction: $d\vec{l} = dx \hat{i}$.
    This specifies the vectors needed for the dot product.

3.  **Calculate the dot product $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$**:
    $$\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = (100 \hat{i}) \cdot (dx \hat{i})$$
    Since $\hat{i} \cdot \hat{i} = 1$:
    $$\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 100 \, dx$$
    The dot product simplifies to a scalar quantity we can integrate.

4.  **Set up the integral with the correct limits**:
    We are integrating from point A ($x=0$) to point B ($x=5 \text{ m}$).
    $$V_B - V_A = - \int_{x=0}^{x=5} 100 \, dx$$
    The limits of integration correspond to the starting and ending x-coordinates.

5.  **Perform the integration**:
    $$V_B - V_A = - [100x]_{x=0}^{x=5}$$
    $$V_B - V_A = - (100 \times 5 - 100 \times 0)$$
    $$V_B - V_A = - (500 - 0)$$
    $$V_B - V_A = -500$$
    This is a straightforward definite integral.

6.  **State the final answer with units**:
    $$\boxed{V_B - V_A = -500 \text{ V}}$$
    The unit for electric potential difference is Volts.

**Reflection**: This example was easy because the electric field was uniform and aligned with the path of integration. The dot product was straightforward, and the integral was simple. The negative sign indicates that moving in the direction of the electric field (from A to B) leads to a decrease in electric potential.

### Example 2: Electric Potential due to a Point Charge

**Problem**: Calculate the electric potential $V(r)$ at a distance $r$ from a positive point charge $Q$, assuming the potential is zero at infinity ($V(\infty) = 0$).

**Given**:
*   Point charge $Q$ at the origin.
*   Reference point $V(\infty) = 0$.
*   We want $V(r)$ at any point at distance $r$.

**Want**: $V(r)$

**Solution**:

1.  **State the definition of potential at a point relative to infinity**:
    $$V(r) - V(\infty) = - \int_{\infty}^{r} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$$
    Since $V(\infty) = 0$, this simplifies to:
    $$V(r) = - \int_{\infty}^{r} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$$
    This is the general formula for potential with a reference at infinity.

2.  **Define the electric field vector for a point charge**:
    The electric field produced by a point charge $Q$ at the origin is radially outward (for positive $Q$) and given by Coulomb's Law:
    $$\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{r}$$
    where $\hat{r}$ is the unit vector in the radial direction. We'll use $k_e = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}$ for simplicity.
    $$\vec{E} = k_e \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{r}$$
    This is the E-field we need to integrate.

3.  **Define the differential displacement vector for a radial path**:
    To simplify the integral, we choose a path directly along the radial direction. In spherical coordinates, $d\vec{l} = dr \hat{r}$.
    Choosing a radial path makes the dot product simple because $\vec{E}$ is also radial.

4.  **Calculate the dot product $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$**:
    $$\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = \left( k_e \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{r} \right) \cdot (dr \hat{r})$$
    Since $\hat{r} \cdot \hat{r} = 1$:
    $$\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = k_e \frac{Q}{r^2} dr$$
    The dot product simplifies nicely due to the choice of path.

5.  **Set up the integral with the correct limits**:
    We are integrating from $r = \infty$ to a generic distance $r$.
    $$V(r) = - \int_{\infty}^{r} k_e \frac{Q}{r'^2} dr'$$
    We use $r'$ as the integration variable to distinguish it from the upper limit $r$.

6.  **Perform the integration**:
    $$V(r) = - k_e Q \int_{\infty}^{r} r'^{-2} dr'$$
    The integral of $r'^{-2}$ is $-r'^{-1}$:
    $$V(r) = - k_e Q \left[ -\frac{1}{r'} \right]_{\infty}^{r}$$
    $$V(r) = k_e Q \left[ \frac{1}{r'} \right]_{\infty}^{r}$$
    $$V(r) = k_e Q \left( \frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{\infty} \right)$$
    Since $\frac{1}{\infty} = 0$:
    $$V(r) = k_e Q \left( \frac{1}{r} - 0 \right)$$
    $$V(r) = k_e \frac{Q}{r}$$
    The integration yields the well-known formula.

7.  **Substitute $k_e$ back and state the final answer**:
    $$\boxed{V(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r}}$$
    This is the electric potential at a distance $r$ from a point charge $Q$.

**Reflection**: This example is fundamental. It demonstrates how to derive the potential formula for a point charge, which is a building block for many other problems. The choice of a radial path greatly simplified the dot product and integral. The negative sign in the definition of potential difference correctly leads to a positive potential for a positive charge and $V \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$.

### Example 3: Potential due to a System of Point Charges

**Problem**: Two point charges, $q_1 = +2.0 \text{ nC}$ and $q_2 = -3.0 \text{ nC}$, are placed on the x-axis. $q_1$ is at $x=0$ and $q_2$ is at $x=4.0 \text{ m}$. Calculate the electric potential at point P located at $(3.0 \text{ m}, 0)$ on the x-axis. Assume $V=0$ at infinity.

**Given**:
*   $q_1 = +2.0 \times 10^{-9} \text{ C}$ at $x_1 = 0$
*   $q_2 = -3.0 \times 10^{-9} \text{ C}$ at $x_2 = 4.0 \text{ m}$
*   Point P at $x_P = 3.0 \text{ m}$
*   $k_e = 8.99 \times 10^9 \text{ N m}^2/\text{C}^2$

**Want**: $V_P$

**Solution**:

1.  **Recall the principle of superposition for electric potential**:
    Electric potential is a scalar quantity. For a system of point charges, the total electric potential at any point is the algebraic sum of the potentials due to each individual charge.
    $$V_P = V_{P,1} + V_{P,2}$$
    This simplifies the problem from integrating an E-field to summing scalar potentials.

2.  **Calculate the distance from each charge to point P**:
    *   Distance from $q_1$ to P ($r_1$):
        $r_1 = |x_P - x_1| = |3.0 \text{ m} - 0 \text{ m}| = 3.0 \text{ m}$
    *   Distance from $q_2$ to P ($r_2$):
        $r_2 = |x_P - x_2| = |3.0 \text{ m} - 4.0 \text{ m}| = |-1.0 \text{ m}| = 1.0 \text{ m}$
    These distances are crucial for applying the point charge potential formula.

3.  **Calculate the potential due to $q_1$ at point P ($V_{P,1}$)**:
    Using the formula $V = k_e \frac{Q}{r}$ derived in Example 2:
    $$V_{P,1} = k_e \frac{q_1}{r_1}$$
    $$V_{P,1} = (8.99 \times 10^9 \text{ N m}^2/\text{C}^2) \frac{(+2.0 \times 10^{-9} \text{ C})}{3.0 \text{ m}}$$
    $$V_{P,1} = \frac{8.99 \times 2.0}{3.0} \text{ V}$$
    $$V_{P,1} = 5.993 \text{ V}$$
    This is the scalar potential contribution from the first charge.

4.  **Calculate the potential due to $q_2$ at point P ($V_{P,2}$)**:
    Using the formula $V = k_e \frac{Q}{r}$:
    $$V_{P,2} = k_e \frac{q_2}{r_2}$$
    $$V_{P,2} = (8.99 \times 10^9 \text{ N m}^2/\text{C}^2) \frac{(-3.0 \times 10^{-9} \text{ C})}{1.0 \text{ m}}$$
    $$V_{P,2} = \frac{8.99 \times (-3.0)}{1.0} \text{ V}$$
    $$V_{P,2} = -26.97 \text{ V}$$
    Note the negative sign due to the negative charge $q_2$.

5.  **Sum the individual potentials to find the total potential at P**:
    $$V_P = V_{P,1} + V_{P,2}$$
    $$V_P = 5.993 \text{ V} + (-26.97 \text{ V})$$
    $$V_P = -20.977 \text{ V}$$

6.  **State the final answer with appropriate significant figures**:
    $$\boxed{V_P = -21.0 \text{ V}}$$
    Rounding to three significant figures.

**Reflection**: This example highlights the scalar nature of electric potential, which makes superposition much simpler than for electric fields (where vector addition is required). It also reinforces that negative charges contribute negative potential.

### Example 4: Potential Difference in a Non-Uniform Electric Field along a Specific Path

**Problem**: An electric field is given by $\vec{E} = (3x^2 \hat{i} + 2y \hat{j}) \text{ N/C}$. Calculate the potential difference $V_B - V_A$ from point A $(0,0)$ to point B $(1,1)$ along two different paths:
    a) Path 1: Straight line from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$.
    b) Path 2: Along the x-axis from $(0,0)$ to $(1,0)$, then along the line $x=1$ from $(1,0)$ to $(1,1)$.
    (This field is conservative, so the results should be the same).

**Given**:
*   $\vec{E} = (3x^2 \hat{i} + 2y \hat{j}) \text{ N/C}$
*   Point A = $(0,0)$
*   Point B = $(1,1)$

**Want**: $V_B - V_A$ for both paths.

**Solution**:

The general formula is $V_B - V_A = - \int_A^B \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$.
The differential displacement vector $d\vec{l}$ in Cartesian coordinates is $d\vec{l} = dx \hat{i} + dy \hat{j} + dz \hat{k}$.
Since the problem is 2D, $d\vec{l} = dx \hat{i} + dy \hat{j}$.
The dot product $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ is:
$\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = (3x^2 \hat{i} + 2y \hat{j}) \cdot (dx \hat{i} + dy \hat{j})$
$\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 3x^2 dx + 2y dy$
This is the expression we need to integrate.

---

**a) Path 1: Straight line from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$**

1.  **Parameterize the path**:
    A straight line from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ can be described by $y=x$.
    Along this path, $dy = dx$.
    The variable $x$ goes from $0$ to $1$.
    This step converts the line integral into a single-variable integral.

2.  **Substitute parameterization into $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$**:
    $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 3x^2 dx + 2y dy$
    Substitute $y=x$ and $dy=dx$:
    $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 3x^2 dx + 2x dx$
    $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = (3x^2 + 2x) dx$
    Now the integrand is solely in terms of $x$.

3.  **Set up and perform the integral**:
    $$V_B - V_A = - \int_{x=0}^{x=1} (3x^2 + 2x) dx$$
    $$V_B - V_A = - \left[ \frac{3x^3}{3} + \frac{2x^2}{2} \right]_{0}^{1}$$
    $$V_B - V_A = - \left[ x^3 + x^2 \right]_{0}^{1}$$
    $$V_B - V_A = - ((1^3 + 1^2) - (0^3 + 0^2))$$
    $$V_B - V_A = - ( (1 + 1) - 0)$$
    $$V_B - V_A = -2$$
    The integration is straightforward.

4.  **State the result for Path 1**:
    $$\boxed{V_B - V_A = -2 \text{ V}}$$

---

**b) Path 2: Along the x-axis from $(0,0)$ to $(1,0)$, then along the line $x=1$ from $(1,0)$ to $(1,1)$**

This path consists of two segments. We'll calculate the integral for each segment and sum them.

**Segment 1: From $(0,0)$ to $(1,0)$ (along x-axis)**

1.  **Parameterize Segment 1**:
    Along the x-axis, $y=0$, so $dy=0$.
    The variable $x$ goes from $0$ to $1$.
    This simplifies the displacement vector for the first part of the path.

2.  **Substitute parameterization into $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ for Segment 1**:
    $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 3x^2 dx + 2y dy$
    Substitute $y=0$ and $dy=0$:
    $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 3x^2 dx + 2(0)(0)$
    $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 3x^2 dx$
    The integrand is now only dependent on $x$.

3.  **Set up and perform the integral for Segment 1**:
    Let $W_1 = \int_{(0,0)}^{(1,0)} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$:
    $$W_1 = \int_{x=0}^{x=1} 3x^2 dx$$
    $$W_1 = \left[ x^3 \right]_{0}^{1}$$
    $$W_1 = (1^3 - 0^3) = 1$$
    This is the work done by the field along the first segment.

**Segment 2: From $(1,0)$ to $(1,1)$ (along line $x=1$)**

1.  **Parameterize Segment 2**:
    Along the line $x=1$, $dx=0$.
    The variable $y$ goes from $0$ to $1$.
    This simplifies the displacement vector for the second part of the path.

2.  **Substitute parameterization into $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ for Segment 2**:
    $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 3x^2 dx + 2y dy$
    Substitute $x=1$ and $dx=0$:
    $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 3(1)^2 (0) + 2y dy$
    $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 2y dy$
    The integrand is now only dependent on $y$.

3.  **Set up and perform the integral for Segment 2**:
    Let $W_2 = \int_{(1,0)}^{(1,1)} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$:
    $$W_2 = \int_{y=0}^{y=1} 2y dy$$
    $$W_2 = \left[ y^2 \right]_{0}^{1}$$
    $$W_2 = (1^2 - 0^2) = 1$$
    This is the work done by the field along the second segment.

4.  **Sum the integrals for both segments and apply the negative sign**:
    The total work done by the electric field from A to B is $W_{AB} = W_1 + W_2$.
    $W_{AB} = 1 + 1 = 2 \text{ J/C}$
    Now, apply the definition for potential difference:
    $$V_B - V_A = - W_{AB}$$
    $$V_B - V_A = -2$$

5.  **State the result for Path 2**:
    $$\boxed{V_B - V_A = -2 \text{ V}}$$

**Reflection**: This example is more challenging as it involves a non-uniform field and line integrals along specific paths. The key is correctly parameterizing each path segment and performing the integration. The fact that both paths yielded the same result ($V_B - V_A = -2 \text{ V}$) confirms that the given electric field is conservative, as expected for electrostatic fields, and that electric potential is path-independent.

## 6. Common mistakes and traps

1.  **Sign Errors**: The most frequent mistake is forgetting or misinterpreting the negative sign in $V_B - V_A = - \int_A^B \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$. Remember, moving *with* the electric field (where $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ is positive) results in a *decrease* in potential ($V_B - V_A$ is negative).
2.  **Confusing Electric Potential ($V$) with Electric Potential Energy ($U_E$)**: Potential is a property of the *field* or *location* (energy per unit charge, J/C = V), while potential energy is the energy possessed by a specific *charge* placed at that location (energy, J). They are related by $\Delta U_E = q \Delta V$.
3.  **Ignoring the Dot Product**: Treating $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ simply as $E \, dl$. The dot product $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = |\vec{E}||d\vec{l}|\cos\theta$ means only the component of $\vec{E}$ parallel to the displacement $d\vec{l}$ contributes to the change in potential. If $\vec{E}$ is perpendicular to $d\vec{l}$, then $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 0$, and there is no change in potential along that segment.
4.  **Assuming Path Dependence**: For electrostatic fields, electric potential is *path-independent*. The integral $\int_A^B \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ yields the same value regardless of the path taken between A and B. If you get different answers for different paths, you've made an error in calculation or the field is not electrostatic (e.g., time-varying).
5.  **Incorrect Integration Limits**: Ensuring the limits of integration correspond to the start and end points of the path, and that the integration variable correctly represents the displacement along that path.
6.  **Forgetting the Reference Point**: When calculating absolute potential $V$ (not just potential difference $\Delta V$), it's crucial to define a reference point where $V=0$. The most common reference is $V=0$ at infinity, especially for isolated charge distributions. Without a reference, only potential differences are physically meaningful.

## 7. Textbook-precise explanation

The electric potential difference, $\Delta V$, between two points A and B in an electric field $\vec{E}$ is defined as the negative of the work done by the electric field per unit positive test charge $q_0$ as it moves from A to B. Mathematically, this is expressed as the negative of the line integral of the electric field from A to B:

$$V_B - V_A = \Delta V = - \int_A^B \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$$

Here:
*   $V_B$ and $V_A$ are the electric potentials at points B and A, respectively.
*   $\vec{E}$ is the electric field vector.
*   $d\vec{l}$ is an infinitesimal displacement vector along the path of integration from A to B.
*   The dot product $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ represents the component of the electric field parallel to the displacement, multiplied by the magnitude of the displacement.

**Units**: The SI unit for electric potential is the **Volt (V)**, which is equivalent to Joules per Coulomb (J/C). This means that a potential difference of 1 Volt implies that 1 Joule of work is done by the electric field when 1 Coulomb of charge moves between the two points.

**Scalar Nature**: Electric potential is a scalar quantity, meaning it only has magnitude and no direction. This simplifies calculations for systems of charges, as potentials can be added algebraically (superposition principle), unlike electric fields which require vector addition.

**Connection to Potential Energy**: The change in electric potential energy $\Delta U_E$ of a charge $q$ moving through a potential difference $\Delta V$ is given by:
$$\Delta U_E = q \Delta V$$
If a positive charge moves from higher potential to lower potential ($\Delta V < 0$), its potential energy decreases ($\Delta U_E < 0$), and this energy is converted into kinetic energy (work done by the field is positive). Conversely, if a positive charge moves from lower potential to higher potential ($\Delta V > 0$), its potential energy increases ($\Delta U_E > 0$), meaning an external agent must do positive work against the field.

**Conservative Field**: The electrostatic field is a conservative field. Consequently, the line integral $\int_A^B \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$ is path-independent; its value depends only on the initial and final points A and B, not on the specific path taken between them. This property allows for the definition of a unique scalar potential function $V(\vec{r})$ for every point in space.

**Reference Point**: To define an absolute value for electric potential, a reference point where $V=0$ must be established. For isolated charge distributions, the standard convention is to set $V=0$ at infinity. For circuits, the ground connection is often designated as $V=0$.

**Relationship to Electric Field (Gradient)**: The electric field can also be derived from the electric potential using the gradient operator:
$$\vec{E} = - \nabla V$$
This signifies that the electric field points in the direction of the steepest decrease in electric potential. (This is a more advanced concept, usually covered after potential is introduced).

**Citations**: This definition and its implications are standard in all university-level physics textbooks. For example, see:
*   Serway, Raymond A., and John W. Jewett Jr. *Physics for Scientists and Engineers*. 9th ed., Cengage Learning, 2014. (Chapter 25: Electric Potential)
*   Griffiths, David J. *Introduction to Electrodynamics*. 4th ed., Pearson, 2017. (Chapter 2: Electrostatics)
*   Purcell, Edward M., and David J. Morin. *Electricity and Magnetism*. 3rd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2013. (Chapter 2: Electric Potential)
