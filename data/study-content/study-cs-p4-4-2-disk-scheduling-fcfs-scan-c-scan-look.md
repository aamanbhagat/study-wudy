## 1. What it is — in plain English

Imagine you're at a very busy library, and there's only one librarian to fetch books. Many people come up at the same time, each asking for a different book located on a different shelf. If the librarian just serves people in the order they ask, they might have to run from one end of the library to the other, then back, then across, wasting a lot of time just walking.

"Disk scheduling" is like that librarian's strategy for fetching books. In a computer, the "books" are pieces of data, and they're stored on a "disk" (like a hard drive). The "librarian" is the disk's read/write head, which moves across the disk to find the data. When many programs or users ask for data at the same time, the operating system gets a list of these requests.

The job of disk scheduling is to decide the *order* in which these requests should be handled. Instead of just doing them one by one as they arrive, the operating system tries to be smart. It looks at all the pending requests and figures out a path for the read/write head that minimizes how much it has to move.

Why bother? Because moving the disk head takes time – it's a mechanical process. If the head moves less, the data can be accessed faster, making your computer feel snappier and more responsive. Different "strategies" or "algorithms" exist, like FCFS, SCAN, C-SCAN, and LOOK, each trying to optimize this movement in a different way.

## 2. Why it matters — real-world applications

Disk scheduling is a foundational concept that impacts the performance and responsiveness of virtually any system that relies on persistent storage.

1.  **Database Management Systems (DBMS):** Large-scale databases, especially those handling Online Transaction Processing (OLTP) like banking systems or e-commerce platforms, process thousands of read/write requests per second. Efficient disk scheduling is crucial for minimizing query response times and maximizing transaction throughput. A slow disk I/O can bottleneck the entire system, leading to frustrated users and lost revenue. For instance, a financial trading platform needs to access and update market data with extremely low latency; poor disk scheduling could mean missed opportunities or even incorrect trades.
2.  **Operating Systems and File Systems:** Every time you open a file, save a document, or even load an application, the operating system interacts with the disk. Paging and swapping, where parts of memory are moved to and from disk, heavily rely on efficient disk I/O. Without intelligent disk scheduling, your computer would feel sluggish, applications would take longer to load, and multitasking would be a painful experience. Modern file systems like NTFS or ext4 internally leverage or interact with the OS's disk scheduler to optimize data access patterns.
3.  **Video Editing and Streaming Platforms:** Applications that deal with large media files, such as professional video editing software (e.g., Adobe Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve) or video streaming services (e.g., Netflix, YouTube), require continuous, high-bandwidth data access from storage. When multiple video clips are being accessed simultaneously for editing, or when multiple users are streaming different parts of a movie, efficient disk scheduling ensures smooth playback and editing without stuttering or buffering.
4.  **Scientific Computing and Machine Learning:** In fields like computational physics or machine learning, datasets can be enormous, often terabytes in size. Training a machine learning model might involve repeatedly reading through vast amounts of data stored on disk. Efficient disk scheduling helps reduce the time spent on data loading, allowing researchers and engineers to iterate faster on experiments and model training. For example, loading a large image dataset for a deep learning model could be significantly sped up by intelligently ordering the disk reads.
5.  **Aerospace and Data Logging:** In aerospace, systems like flight data recorders ("black boxes") continuously log critical flight parameters. While these often use solid-state storage now, the principles of optimizing write patterns to ensure data integrity and timely recording are paramount. For older systems or specialized data logging units that might still use spinning disks, efficient scheduling ensures that high-frequency sensor data can be written without loss, even under high load, which is critical for post-incident analysis and safety.

## 3. Prerequisites — what you must know first

Before diving deep into disk scheduling, ensure you have a solid grasp of these fundamental concepts:

*   **Operating System Basics:** Understand what an operating system is, its primary roles (resource management, process management, memory management, I/O management), and the concept of the kernel.
*   **I/O Management:** How the operating system interacts with peripheral devices (like disks) to perform input and output operations.
*   **Disk Structure:** The physical components of a traditional Hard Disk Drive (HDD), including:
    *   **Platters:** The circular disks that store data.
    *   **Tracks:** Concentric circles on each platter, like grooves on a record.
    *   **Sectors:** Smallest units of data storage on a track.
    *   **Cylinders:** A set of tracks that are at the same radial distance on all platters. All tracks within a cylinder can be accessed without moving the read/write head. This is the most important concept for disk scheduling.
    *   **Read/Write Head:** The mechanical arm that moves across the platters to read or write data.
    *   **Seek Time:** The time it takes for the read/write head to move to the correct cylinder. This is the dominant factor disk scheduling aims to minimize.
    *   **Rotational Latency:** The time it takes for the desired sector to rotate under the read/write head once the head is at the correct track.
    *   **Transfer Time:** The time it takes to actually read or write the data once the head is at the correct sector.
*   **Queues (Data Structure):** A basic understanding of a First-In, First-Out (FIFO) queue, where elements are added to the rear and removed from the front.

## 4. The core idea — step by step

The core idea behind disk scheduling is to minimize the total time the disk's read/write head spends moving across the platters (seek time) to fulfill a series of I/O requests. Each request specifies a particular cylinder where the data resides. The challenge is that these requests arrive in an arbitrary order, and simply serving them as they come can lead to excessive head movement.

Let's assume a disk with cylinders numbered from 0 to 199. Suppose the current position of the disk head is at cylinder 53. We have a queue of pending requests for data at the following cylinders: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$. We will use this set of requests to illustrate each algorithm.

### Step 1: FCFS (First-Come, First-Served)

*   **Plain-English Statement:** This is the simplest strategy. The disk head services requests strictly in the order they arrive in the queue, without any regard for their physical location on the disk. It's like the librarian serving people exactly as they line up, even if the next person in line wants a book from the opposite side of the library.
*   **Small Concrete Example:**
    *   Current head position: 53
    *   Request queue (arrival order): $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
    *   The head will move from 53 to 98, then to 183, then to 37, and so on, in that exact sequence.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:**
    Given a sequence of requests $R = [r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n]$ and an initial head position $H_0$. The head moves from $H_0$ to $r_1$, then to $r_2$, and so on.
    The total seek distance is calculated as:
    $$ \sum_{i=1}^{n} |r_i - H_{i-1}| $$
    where $H_{i-1}$ is the cylinder of the previously serviced request (or $H_0$ for the first request).
*   **What Could Go Wrong:** FCFS is simple to implement but can be very inefficient. If requests are scattered widely across the disk, the head will frequently move back and forth across large distances, leading to high seek times and poor performance. This phenomenon is sometimes called "thrashing" if the head movement is extremely erratic.

### Step 2: SCAN (Elevator Algorithm)

*   **Plain-English Statement:** Imagine an elevator in a building. It goes up, stopping at floors where people are waiting, until it reaches the top floor (or the highest request in its current direction). Then it reverses direction and goes down, stopping at floors where people are waiting, until it reaches the bottom floor (or the lowest request). The disk head works similarly: it moves in one direction (e.g., towards increasing cylinder numbers), servicing all requests in its path, until it reaches the end of the disk (or the furthest request in that direction). Then, it reverses direction and moves towards the other end, servicing requests along the way.
*   **Small Concrete Example:**
    *   Current head position: 53
    *   Request queue: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
    *   Assume the head initially moves towards the higher cylinder numbers (199).
    *   It will pass 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183. After servicing 183, it continues to 199 (the end of the disk).
    *   Then it reverses and moves towards 0, servicing 37 and 14 on its way.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:**
    The head traverses the disk in one direction (e.g., from 0 to MAX\_CYLINDER), servicing all requests in that direction. Once it reaches the end (or the furthest request in that direction), it reverses and traverses the disk in the opposite direction (e.g., from MAX\_CYLINDER to 0), servicing requests.
    The total seek distance is the sum of distances between consecutive requests in the chosen path. The path includes movement to the end of the disk in each direction.
*   **What Could Go Wrong:** Requests just passed by the head when it reverses direction will have to wait for almost a full sweep of the disk before they are serviced. This can lead to a bias: requests in the middle of the disk might be serviced more frequently than those at the ends, and new requests arriving just behind the head's current direction might experience long wait times.

### Step 3: C-SCAN (Circular SCAN)

*   **Plain-English Statement:** C-SCAN is a variation of SCAN designed to provide a more uniform wait time. Think of it as a one-way elevator. It goes up, stopping at all requested floors. When it reaches the top floor, instead of reversing and serving on the way down, it quickly "jumps" back to the ground floor without stopping and then starts another upward sweep. This ensures that all requests get serviced once per sweep in a predictable manner.
*   **Small Concrete Example:**
    *   Current head position: 53
    *   Request queue: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
    *   Assume the head initially moves towards higher cylinder numbers (199).
    *   It will pass 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183. After servicing 183, it continues to 199 (the end of the disk).
    *   Crucially, instead of reversing and servicing, it *jumps* directly from 199 to 0 (the other end) without servicing any requests.
    *   Then, it starts moving from 0 towards 199 again, servicing 14 and 37 on its way.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:**
    The head traverses the disk in only one direction (e.g., from 0 to MAX\_CYLINDER), servicing all requests in that direction. When it reaches MAX\_CYLINDER, it immediately "jumps" to 0 (or the lowest cylinder with a pending request) and resumes its traversal in the same direction. It does *not* service requests during the jump back.
    The total seek distance includes the distance for the jump from MAX\_CYLINDER to 0.
*   **What Could Go Wrong:** While it provides more uniform wait times, the jump from one end of the disk to the other can incur a significant seek distance if the disk is large. Requests that are just past the "jump-back" point might have to wait almost a full sweep.

### Step 4: LOOK

*   **Plain-English Statement:** LOOK is an optimization of the SCAN algorithm. Instead of always going all the way to the absolute end of the disk (0 or 199) before reversing, the head "looks" to see if there are any pending requests in its current direction. If there are no more requests in that direction, it reverses immediately without going to the very end. It's like an elevator that only goes as high as the highest requested floor, then turns around, rather than always going to the top of the building.
*   **Small Concrete Example:**
    *   Current head position: 53
    *   Request queue: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
    *   Assume the head initially moves towards higher cylinder numbers. The highest request is 183.
    *   It will pass 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183. After servicing 183, it *immediately reverses* because there are no more requests higher than 183. It does NOT go to 199.
    *   Then it moves towards lower cylinder numbers, servicing 37 and 14. After servicing 14, it *immediately reverses* because there are no more requests lower than 14. It does NOT go to 0.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:**
    Similar to SCAN, but the head only moves as far as the furthest request in its current direction before reversing. It does not sweep the entire disk if there are no requests at the extreme ends.
    The total seek distance is calculated similarly to SCAN, but without the extra movement to the absolute disk ends.
*   **What Could Go Wrong:** It still suffers from the same potential for starvation for requests just passed by the head when it reverses, similar to SCAN, though to a lesser extent due to reduced overall travel.

### Step 5: C-LOOK

*   **Plain-English Statement:** C-LOOK is the circular version of LOOK, combining the efficiencies of both C-SCAN and LOOK. Like C-SCAN, it only services requests in one direction. But like LOOK, it doesn't go all the way to the absolute end of the disk. Instead, when it reaches the highest requested cylinder in its current direction, it immediately "jumps" back to the lowest requested cylinder in the *opposite* direction, without servicing requests during the jump, and then continues its sweep in the original direction.
*   **Small Concrete Example:**
    *   Current head position: 53
    *   Request queue: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
    *   Assume the head initially moves towards higher cylinder numbers. The highest request is 183. The lowest request is 14.
    *   It will pass 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183. After servicing 183, it *immediately jumps* from 183 back to 14 (the lowest request) without servicing any requests during the jump. It does NOT go to 199 or 0.
    *   Then, it starts moving from 14 towards 183 again, servicing 37 on its way (14 was already serviced in the "jump to" step, or more precisely, the jump is *to* the first request to be serviced in the next sweep).
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:**
    The head traverses the disk in one direction, servicing requests, but only up to the highest requested cylinder in that direction. Once it services the highest request, it immediately "jumps" to the lowest requested cylinder and resumes its traversal in the *original* direction. It does not service requests during the jump.
    The total seek distance includes the distance for the jump from the highest serviced request to the lowest pending request.
*   **What Could Go Wrong:** While generally very efficient, there's still a "jump" that incurs seek time, and requests arriving during the jump might have to wait for the next full sweep.

## 5. Worked examples — multiple, with every step shown

We will use the following common scenario for all examples:
*   **Disk cylinders:** 0 to 199
*   **Current head position:** 53
*   **Pending request queue:** $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$

We need to calculate the total number of head movements (seek distance).

### Example 1: FCFS (First-Come, First-Served)

**Problem:** Calculate the total seek distance using the FCFS algorithm.

**Given:**
*   Cylinders: 0-199
*   Current head position: 53
*   Request queue (in arrival order): $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$

**What we want:** Total seek distance.

**Solution:**

1.  **Start at 53.**
    *   Current position: 53
    *   Requests to service: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$

2.  **Service 98.**
    *   Move from 53 to 98.
    *   Seek distance: $|98 - 53| = 45$
    *   Total seek distance so far: 45
    *   Current position: 98
    *   Requests remaining: $[183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$

3.  **Service 183.**
    *   Move from 98 to 183.
    *   Seek distance: $|183 - 98| = 85$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $45 + 85 = 130$
    *   Current position: 183
    *   Requests remaining: $[37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$

4.  **Service 37.**
    *   Move from 183 to 37.
    *   Seek distance: $|37 - 183| = 146$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $130 + 146 = 276$
    *   Current position: 37
    *   Requests remaining: $[122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$

5.  **Service 122.**
    *   Move from 37 to 122.
    *   Seek distance: $|122 - 37| = 85$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $276 + 85 = 361$
    *   Current position: 122
    *   Requests remaining: $[14, 124, 65, 67]$

6.  **Service 14.**
    *   Move from 122 to 14.
    *   Seek distance: $|14 - 122| = 108$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $361 + 108 = 469$
    *   Current position: 14
    *   Requests remaining: $[124, 65, 67]$

7.  **Service 124.**
    *   Move from 14 to 124.
    *   Seek distance: $|124 - 14| = 110$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $469 + 110 = 579$
    *   Current position: 124
    *   Requests remaining: $[65, 67]$

8.  **Service 65.**
    *   Move from 124 to 65.
    *   Seek distance: $|65 - 124| = 59$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $579 + 59 = 638$
    *   Current position: 65
    *   Requests remaining: $[67]$

9.  **Service 67.**
    *   Move from 65 to 67.
    *   Seek distance: $|67 - 65| = 2$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $638 + 2 = 640$
    *   Current position: 67
    *   Requests remaining: []

The sequence of head movements is: $53 \to 98 \to 183 \to 37 \to 122 \to 14 \to 124 \to 65 \to 67$.

**Total seek distance: $\mathbf{640}$ cylinders.**

**Reflection:** FCFS is straightforward but results in a high total seek distance because the head frequently changes direction across large spans of the disk, driven purely by arrival order.

### Example 2: SCAN (Elevator Algorithm)

**Problem:** Calculate the total seek distance using the SCAN algorithm. Assume the head initially moves towards higher cylinder numbers (towards 199).

**Given:**
*   Cylinders: 0-199
*   Current head position: 53
*   Request queue: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
*   Initial direction: Towards 199

**What we want:** Total seek distance.

**Solution:**

1.  **Start at 53.**
    *   Current position: 53
    *   Requests: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
    *   Sorted requests: $[14, 37, 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183]$
    *   Requests in the initial direction (towards 199) from 53: $[65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183]$
    *   Requests in the opposite direction (towards 0) from 53: $[14, 37]$

2.  **Move towards 199, servicing requests.**
    *   The head moves from 53, servicing requests in increasing order:
        *   To 65: $|65 - 53| = 12$
        *   To 67: $|67 - 65| = 2$
        *   To 98: $|98 - 67| = 31$
        *   To 122: $|122 - 98| = 24$
        *   To 124: $|124 - 122| = 2$
        *   To 183: $|183 - 124| = 59$
    *   Current position after servicing 183: 183
    *   Total seek distance so far: $12 + 2 + 31 + 24 + 2 + 59 = 130$
    *   Requests remaining (those below 53): $[14, 37]$

3.  **Continue to end of disk (199).**
    *   Move from 183 to 199 (the end of the disk).
    *   Seek distance: $|199 - 183| = 16$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $130 + 16 = 146$
    *   Current position: 199

4.  **Reverse direction and move towards 0, servicing requests.**
    *   The head moves from 199, servicing requests in decreasing order:
        *   To 37: $|37 - 199| = 162$ (servicing 37)
        *   To 14: $|14 - 37| = 23$ (servicing 14)
    *   Total seek distance so far: $146 + 162 + 23 = 331$
    *   Current position: 14

The sequence of head movements is: $53 \to 65 \to 67 \to 98 \to 122 \to 124 \to 183 \to 199 \to 37 \to 14$.

**Total seek distance: $\mathbf{331}$ cylinders.**

**Reflection:** SCAN significantly reduces the total seek distance compared to FCFS by organizing movements. The head makes a full sweep of the disk, ensuring all requests are eventually met, but the movement to 199 even when 183 was the last request in that direction, and then the long sweep back, still adds considerable distance.

### Example 3: C-SCAN (Circular SCAN)

**Problem:** Calculate the total seek distance using the C-SCAN algorithm. Assume the head initially moves towards higher cylinder numbers (towards 199).

**Given:**
*   Cylinders: 0-199
*   Current head position: 53
*   Request queue: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
*   Initial direction: Towards 199

**What we want:** Total seek distance.

**Solution:**

1.  **Start at 53.**
    *   Current position: 53
    *   Requests: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
    *   Sorted requests: $[14, 37, 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183]$
    *   Requests to be serviced in the initial sweep (towards 199) from 53: $[65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183]$
    *   Requests to be serviced after the jump (from 0): $[14, 37]$

2.  **Move towards 199, servicing requests.**
    *   The head moves from 53, servicing requests in increasing order:
        *   To 65: $|65 - 53| = 12$
        *   To 67: $|67 - 65| = 2$
        *   To 98: $|98 - 67| = 31$
        *   To 122: $|122 - 98| = 24$
        *   To 124: $|124 - 122| = 2$
        *   To 183: $|183 - 124| = 59$
    *   Current position after servicing 183: 183
    *   Total seek distance so far: $12 + 2 + 31 + 24 + 2 + 59 = 130$

3.  **Continue to end of disk (199).**
    *   Move from 183 to 199 (the end of the disk).
    *   Seek distance: $|199 - 183| = 16$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $130 + 16 = 146$
    *   Current position: 199

4.  **Jump from 199 to 0 (without servicing).**
    *   Move from 199 to 0.
    *   Seek distance: $|0 - 199| = 199$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $146 + 199 = 345$
    *   Current position: 0

5.  **Move towards 199 again, servicing remaining requests.**
    *   The head moves from 0, servicing requests in increasing order:
        *   To 14: $|14 - 0| = 14$
        *   To 37: $|37 - 14| = 23$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $345 + 14 + 23 = 382$
    *   Current position: 37

The sequence of head movements is: $53 \to 65 \to 67 \to 98 \to 122 \to 124 \to 183 \to 199 \to 0 \to 14 \to 37$.

**Total seek distance: $\mathbf{382}$ cylinders.**

**Reflection:** C-SCAN provides more uniform wait times but often results in a higher total seek distance than SCAN due to the "jump" from one end of the disk to the other. In this case, it's higher than SCAN (382 vs 331). The large jump of 199 cylinders contributes significantly.

### Example 4: LOOK

**Problem:** Calculate the total seek distance using the LOOK algorithm. Assume the head initially moves towards higher cylinder numbers.

**Given:**
*   Cylinders: 0-199
*   Current head position: 53
*   Request queue: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
*   Initial direction: Towards higher cylinder numbers

**What we want:** Total seek distance.

**Solution:**

1.  **Start at 53.**
    *   Current position: 53
    *   Requests: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
    *   Sorted requests: $[14, 37, 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183]$
    *   Requests in the initial direction (towards higher numbers) from 53: $[65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183]$. The highest of these is 183.
    *   Requests in the opposite direction (towards lower numbers) from 53: $[14, 37]$. The lowest of these is 14.

2.  **Move towards the highest request (183), servicing requests.**
    *   The head moves from 53, servicing requests in increasing order:
        *   To 65: $|65 - 53| = 12$
        *   To 67: $|67 - 65| = 2$
        *   To 98: $|98 - 67| = 31$
        *   To 122: $|122 - 98| = 24$
        *   To 124: $|124 - 122| = 2$
        *   To 183: $|183 - 124| = 59$
    *   Current position after servicing 183: 183
    *   Total seek distance so far: $12 + 2 + 31 + 24 + 2 + 59 = 130$

3.  **Reverse direction (from 183) and move towards the lowest request (14), servicing requests.**
    *   The head moves from 183, servicing requests in decreasing order:
        *   To 37: $|37 - 183| = 146$
        *   To 14: $|14 - 37| = 23$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $130 + 146 + 23 = 299$
    *   Current position: 14

The sequence of head movements is: $53 \to 65 \to 67 \to 98 \to 122 \to 124 \to 183 \to 37 \to 14$.

**Total seek distance: $\mathbf{299}$ cylinders.**

**Reflection:** LOOK is more efficient than SCAN because it avoids unnecessary movement to the absolute ends of the disk (0 and 199) if there are no requests there. This significantly reduces total seek time, as seen by 299 vs 331 for SCAN.

### Example 5: C-LOOK

**Problem:** Calculate the total seek distance using the C-LOOK algorithm. Assume the head initially moves towards higher cylinder numbers.

**Given:**
*   Cylinders: 0-199
*   Current head position: 53
*   Request queue: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
*   Initial direction: Towards higher cylinder numbers

**What we want:** Total seek distance.

**Solution:**

1.  **Start at 53.**
    *   Current position: 53
    *   Requests: $[98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67]$
    *   Sorted requests: $[14, 37, 65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183]$
    *   Highest request: 183
    *   Lowest request: 14
    *   Requests to be serviced in the initial sweep (towards highest) from 53: $[65, 67, 98, 122, 124, 183]$
    *   Requests to be serviced after the jump (from lowest request): $[14, 37]$

2.  **Move towards the highest request (183), servicing requests.**
    *   The head moves from 53, servicing requests in increasing order:
        *   To 65: $|65 - 53| = 12$
        *   To 67: $|67 - 65| = 2$
        *   To 98: $|98 - 67| = 31$
        *   To 122: $|122 - 98| = 24$
        *   To 124: $|124 - 122| = 2$
        *   To 183: $|183 - 124| = 59$
    *   Current position after servicing 183: 183
    *   Total seek distance so far: $12 + 2 + 31 + 24 + 2 + 59 = 130$

3.  **Jump from the highest serviced request (183) to the lowest pending request (14) without servicing.**
    *   Move from 183 to 14.
    *   Seek distance: $|14 - 183| = 169$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $130 + 169 = 299$
    *   Current position: 14

4.  **Move towards higher numbers again, servicing remaining requests.**
    *   The head moves from 14, servicing requests in increasing order:
        *   To 37: $|37 - 14| = 23$
    *   Total seek distance so far: $299 + 23 = 322$
    *   Current position: 37

The sequence of head movements is: $53 \to 65 \to 67 \to 98 \to 122 \to 124 \to 183 \to 14 \to 37$.

**Total seek distance: $\mathbf{322}$ cylinders.**

**Reflection:** C-LOOK is generally very efficient in terms of average seek time and provides good fairness. It avoids the full sweep of C-SCAN and the double-direction service of SCAN/LOOK. In this example, it's better than FCFS and C-SCAN, but slightly worse than LOOK. The efficiency of LOOK and C-LOOK often depends on the distribution of requests.

## 6. Common mistakes and traps

1.  **Forgetting the initial head position:** The starting point for calculating the first seek is always the current head position, not the first request in the queue.
2.  **Incorrectly calculating seek distance:** Seek distance is always the absolute difference between the current cylinder and the target cylinder, e.g., $|C_2 - C_1|$. It's a distance, so it must be positive.
3.  **Misunderstanding initial direction for SCAN/C-SCAN/LOOK/C-LOOK:** The problem statement *must* specify the initial direction (e.g., "towards 0" or "towards 199"). If not specified, you might need to state your assumption or calculate both possibilities. This direction determines which requests are serviced first.
4.  **Not going to the disk end for SCAN/C-SCAN:** For SCAN, the head *must* go to the absolute end of the disk (0 or MAX\_CYLINDER) in its current direction before reversing, even if there are no requests there. For C-SCAN, it *must* go to the absolute end before jumping to the other end. Forgetting this extra movement is a common error.
5.  **Confusing LOOK/C-LOOK with SCAN/C-SCAN:** LOOK and C-LOOK are optimizations that *do not* go to the absolute ends of the disk. They only go as far as the furthest request in their current direction. Mixing these rules up is a frequent trap.
6.  **Servicing requests during the "jump" in C-SCAN/C-LOOK:** In C-SCAN and C-LOOK, the head jumps from one end (or furthest request) to the other end (or lowest request) *without servicing any requests* during this return trip. Only after the jump does it resume servicing in its designated direction.
7.  **Ignoring requests: ** Ensure all requests in the initial queue are eventually serviced. It's easy to miss one, especially with complex paths.

## 7. Textbook-precise explanation

Disk scheduling algorithms are strategies employed by operating systems to optimize the order of disk I/O requests. The primary objective is to minimize the total seek time, which is the time taken for the disk's read/write head to move to the cylinder containing the requested data. Minimizing seek time improves disk throughput and reduces average response time for I/O-bound processes.

Consider a set of pending disk I/O requests, each specifying a logical block address that translates to a specific cylinder on the disk. Let $C_0$ be the current cylinder position of the disk head, and $Q = \{q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n\}$ be the set of cylinders for which I/O requests are pending. The total seek distance for a sequence of head movements from $p_0$ to $p_1, \ldots, p_k$ is given by $\sum_{i=1}^{k} |p_i - p_{i-1}|$.

### First-Come, First-Served (FCFS)
**Definition:** This algorithm processes requests in the exact order they arrive in the disk queue. It is a non-preemptive scheduling policy.
**Mechanism:** Given an initial head position $H_{current}$ and a queue of requests $R = [r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n]$, the head moves sequentially from $H_{current}$ to $r_1$, then to $r_2$, and so on, until all requests are serviced.
**Characteristics:** Simple to implement. Fair in terms of arrival order. However, it can lead to very high total seek times if requests are randomly distributed across the disk, resulting in significant head thrashing.

### SCAN (Elevator Algorithm)
**Definition:** The disk head starts at one end of the disk and moves towards the other end, servicing all requests in its path. Upon reaching the other end, it reverses direction and continues servicing requests.
**Mechanism:** Let $H_{current}$ be the current head position and $D_{initial}$ be the initial direction (e.g., towards increasing cylinder numbers, $MAX\_CYLINDER$). The head traverses in $D_{initial}$, servicing all requests $q \in Q$ such that $q \ge H_{current}$ (if moving towards $MAX\_CYLINDER$) or $q \le H_{current}$ (if moving towards $0$). Once it reaches $MAX\_CYLINDER$ (or $0$), it reverses direction and sweeps across the disk, servicing all remaining requests.
**Characteristics:** Provides better average seek time than FCFS. Reduces head thrashing. However, requests just passed by the head might experience long wait times as they have to wait for a full sweep of the disk.

### C-SCAN (Circular SCAN)
**Definition:** A variant of SCAN designed to provide a more uniform wait time. The head moves in only one direction (e.g., from 0 to MAX\_CYLINDER), servicing requests. When it reaches the end of the disk in that direction, it immediately jumps back to the other end (e.g., from MAX\_CYLINDER to 0) without servicing any requests during the jump, and then begins another sweep in the same direction.
**Mechanism:** The head moves from $H_{current}$ towards $MAX\_CYLINDER$, servicing requests in increasing order. Upon reaching $MAX\_CYLINDER$, it performs a fast seek (jump) to $0$. It then continues to move from $0$ towards $MAX\_CYLINDER$, servicing any remaining requests. The return trip (jump) from $MAX\_CYLINDER$ to $0$ is typically assumed to incur seek time but no service.
**Characteristics:** Provides more uniform wait times than SCAN because each cylinder is visited once per sweep. Less prone to starvation for requests at the ends of the disk. Can have higher total seek time than SCAN due to the large jump, but the variance in response time is lower.

### LOOK
**Definition:** An optimization of the SCAN algorithm. Instead of sweeping all the way to the absolute ends of the disk (0 or MAX\_CYLINDER), the head only goes as far as the furthest request in its current direction before reversing.
**Mechanism:** Similar to SCAN, but the head's traversal in a given direction stops when there are no more requests in that direction. It reverses course from the last serviced request in that direction, not from the absolute end of the disk.
**Characteristics:** Reduces unnecessary movement compared to SCAN, leading to lower total seek times. Maintains the fairness and reduced thrashing benefits of SCAN.

### C-LOOK
**Definition:** An optimization of the C-SCAN algorithm. The head moves in one direction, servicing requests, but only up to the furthest request in that direction. When it reaches this furthest request, it immediately jumps back to the lowest requested cylinder (not necessarily 0) and resumes its traversal in the original direction.
**Mechanism:** The head moves from $H_{current}$ towards the highest cylinder with a pending request, servicing requests. Once it reaches this highest request, it jumps to the lowest cylinder with a pending request. It then moves from this lowest request back towards the highest, servicing requests. The jump from the highest to the lowest request does not involve servicing.
**Characteristics:** Combines the benefits of C-SCAN's uniform service with LOOK's reduced movement. Generally offers excellent average seek time and low variance in response times.
