## 1. What it is — in plain English

Imagine you're sticking your hand out of a car window. If you hold it flat, nothing much happens. If you tilt it slightly upwards, you feel a force pushing it up – that's lift! An airplane wing, or "airfoil," does the same thing, but much more efficiently.

"Thin airfoil theory" is a simplified way to understand how these wings generate lift. It's like drawing a simple stick figure of a person instead of a detailed portrait. We pretend the wing is super thin, almost like a flat line, which makes the math much easier. This simplification works surprisingly well for many real-world wings, especially at small angles.

The formula $L' = \pi\rho V^2(\alpha + 2\beta/\pi c)$ is a key result from this theory. It tells us the amount of lift generated by each foot or meter of a wing (that's what "lift per unit span" means). It says that lift depends on: the air density ($\rho$), how fast the wing is moving ($V$), how much the wing is tilted into the airflow (its "angle of attack," $\alpha$), and the wing's curvature (represented by $\beta$) and chord length ($c$).

While the formula provided $L' = \pi\rho V^2(\alpha + 2\beta/\pi c)$ captures the essence, it's actually missing a crucial factor of the chord length ($c$) to be dimensionally consistent for "lift per unit span." The standard, dimensionally correct form is $L' = \pi\rho V^2 c (\alpha + 2\beta/\pi c)$, where the term $2\beta/\pi c$ represents the contribution of the wing's curvature (camber) to its effective angle of attack for lift. We will use this dimensionally correct form in our examples and detailed explanations, while acknowledging the prompt's specific phrasing.

## 2. Why it matters — real-world applications

Thin airfoil theory is a cornerstone of aerodynamic design, providing quick and powerful insights without needing complex computer simulations.

1.  **Aircraft Design & Optimization:** Aerospace engineers use this theory for initial wing design. Before running expensive computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations or building physical prototypes, they can quickly estimate the lift characteristics of various wing shapes, determine optimal angles of attack for cruising, and understand how changes in wing curvature affect performance. Companies like Boeing and Airbus use these foundational principles in the conceptual design phases of their aircraft.
2.  **Wind Turbine Blade Design:** The blades of wind turbines are essentially airfoils. Thin airfoil theory helps engineers design blades that efficiently extract energy from the wind. By optimizing the blade's shape (camber and twist) and its angle relative to the incoming wind, they can maximize power generation, which is crucial for renewable energy companies like Vestas or Siemens Gamesa.
3.  **Hydrofoil Design:** Boats equipped with hydrofoils lift out of the water at speed, drastically reducing drag. The design of these underwater wings relies on the same principles as airfoils. Thin airfoil theory helps designers at companies like America's Cup sailing teams or Candela (electric hydrofoil boats) to create efficient foils for high-speed water travel.
4.  **Racing Car Aerodynamics:** Spoilers and wings on high-performance racing cars (e.g., Formula 1 cars) are inverted airfoils designed to generate "downforce," pushing the car onto the track for better grip. Thin airfoil theory provides a quick way to estimate the downforce generated by different spoiler designs, aiding in the rapid iteration cycles of motorsport engineering.

## 3. Prerequisites — what you must know first

Before diving deep into thin airfoil theory, ensure you have a solid grasp of these fundamental concepts:

*   **Fluid Properties:** Understanding density ($\rho$), pressure ($P$), and viscosity ($\mu$) of fluids.
*   **Velocity Field:** How to describe the motion of fluid particles using velocity vectors $\vec{V}(x,y,z,t)$.
*   **Streamlines:** Lines everywhere tangent to the velocity vector, representing the path a massless particle would take in steady flow.
*   **Incompressible Flow:** The assumption that fluid density does not change significantly with pressure or temperature (e.g., air at low speeds).
*   **Inviscid Flow:** The assumption that fluid has no viscosity, meaning no friction between fluid layers or with solid surfaces. This simplifies the equations dramatically.
*   **Potential Flow:** A special type of inviscid, incompressible flow where the velocity field can be described as the gradient of a scalar potential function ($\vec{V} = \nabla\phi$). This allows for powerful mathematical tools like complex analysis.
*   **Circulation ($\Gamma$):** A measure of the "swirl" or rotation in a fluid flow, defined as the line integral of velocity around a closed loop. Crucial for understanding lift.
*   **Kutta-Joukowski Theorem:** A fundamental theorem stating that the lift per unit span ($L'$) on a 2D body in an inviscid, incompressible flow is directly proportional to the fluid density ($\rho$), freestream velocity ($V$), and the circulation ($\Gamma$) around the body: $L' = \rho V \Gamma$.
*   **Angle of Attack ($\alpha$):** The angle between the freestream velocity vector and the chord line of an airfoil.
*   **Airfoil Geometry:** Familiarity with terms like chord ($c$), camber line ($y_c(x)$), leading edge, and trailing edge.
*   **Basic Calculus:** Derivatives (for slopes, rates of change) and Integrals (for summing up effects, calculating areas/volumes).
*   **Superposition Principle:** For linear systems (like potential flow), the total effect is the sum of individual effects. This is key to breaking down complex problems.

## 4. The core idea — step by step

Thin airfoil theory simplifies the complex problem of fluid flow over a wing into a manageable, solvable form by making several key assumptions. The goal is to determine the circulation around the airfoil, which, by the Kutta-Joukowski theorem, directly gives us the lift.

### Step 1: The Airfoil as a Vortex Sheet

*   **Plain English:** Instead of dealing with the actual thick airfoil shape, we imagine it as a super-thin line (the camber line) along which tiny, swirling fluid elements (vortices) are continuously distributed. Each tiny vortex contributes to the overall flow around the airfoil. The strength of these vortices varies along the line.
*   **Concrete Example:** Think of a long, thin string of miniature whirlpools lined up from the front (leading edge) to the back (trailing edge) of the wing. The strength of each whirlpool determines how much it influences the surrounding flow.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:** We replace the airfoil with a continuous distribution of vortices, called a vortex sheet, along its chord line. The strength of this sheet is denoted by $\gamma(x)$, where $x$ is the position along the chord. The total circulation $\Gamma$ around the airfoil is the integral of this vortex strength along the chord:
    $$ \Gamma = \int_0^c \gamma(x) dx $$
    The velocity induced by this vortex sheet, combined with the freestream velocity, must satisfy the boundary conditions (flow tangent to the airfoil surface, or in our thin approximation, tangent to the camber line).
*   **What could go wrong:** Misunderstanding that this vortex sheet is a *mathematical model* to represent the airfoil's influence on the flow, not a literal physical sheet of vortices. The actual fluid doesn't form a sheet of discrete vortices along the wing.

### Step 2: Superposition Principle for Linearized Flow

*   **Plain English:** We can break down the complicated flow over a cambered airfoil at an angle of attack into two simpler problems:
    1.  Flow over a flat plate (no curvature) at the given angle of attack.
    2.  Flow over a curved plate (cambered) at zero angle of attack.
    We then add the solutions from these two simpler problems to get the solution for the original, more complex problem. This is possible because the governing equations for potential flow are linear.
*   **Concrete Example:** Imagine designing a house. Instead of drawing the whole complex structure at once, you might first draw the basic rectangular frame (like the flat plate part) and then add the curved roof and decorative elements (like the camber part). The final design is the sum of these simpler components.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:** The total velocity potential $\phi$ (or stream function $\psi$) for the flow over the airfoil is the sum of the freestream potential $\phi_\infty$, the potential due to the angle of attack $\phi_\alpha$, and the potential due to the camber $\phi_{camber}$. This linearity allows us to solve for the vortex sheet strength $\gamma(x)$ by superimposing the effects of $\alpha$ and the camber line slope $dy_c/dx$.
*   **What could go wrong:** Applying superposition when the underlying physics are non-linear (e.g., at high angles of attack where flow separation occurs, or in compressible flow where shock waves form). Thin airfoil theory is a linear theory.

### Step 3: Satisfying the Boundary Condition

*   **Plain English:** The fluid cannot flow *through* the wing. It must flow *tangent* to its surface. In our thin airfoil approximation, this means the flow must be tangent to the camber line. This condition helps us determine the strength of the vortex sheet.
*   **Concrete Example:** If you're driving a boat, the water can't go through the hull; it has to flow around it. If the hull is slightly angled, the water must follow that angle.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:** The velocity normal to the camber line must be zero. For a thin airfoil, this simplifies to requiring the vertical component of the induced velocity ($v$) to cancel out the vertical component of the freestream velocity ($V \sin\alpha$) and match the vertical component due to the camber line slope ($V \frac{dy_c}{dx}$).
    $$ v(x) = V \left( \alpha - \frac{dy_c}{dx} \right) $$
    This induced velocity $v(x)$ is generated by the vortex sheet $\gamma(x)$. There's an integral relationship between $\gamma(x)$ and $v(x)$:
    $$ v(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^c \frac{\gamma(\xi)}{x-\xi} d\xi $$
    Solving this integral equation for $\gamma(x)$ is the core mathematical challenge of thin airfoil theory.
*   **What could go wrong:** Incorrectly applying the boundary condition, especially at the leading or trailing edges, can lead to unphysical results like infinite velocities.

### Step 4: The Kutta Condition

*   **Plain English:** For a real wing, the flow *must* leave the sharp trailing edge smoothly. It can't wrap around and create a swirling mess there. This "smooth exit" condition is critical because it uniquely determines the total circulation around the airfoil and thus the lift. Without it, there would be infinitely many possible solutions for the circulation.
*   **Concrete Example:** When you pour water from a pitcher, it flows smoothly off the lip. It doesn't curl back underneath the lip. The Kutta condition ensures a similar smooth departure of air from the wing's trailing edge.
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:** The Kutta condition states that the velocity at the trailing edge must be finite. For a sharp trailing edge, this implies that the vortex strength $\gamma(x)$ must be zero at $x=c$ (the trailing edge). Mathematically, it resolves the non-uniqueness of solutions to the integral equation in Step 3.
*   **What could go wrong:** Ignoring the Kutta condition leads to physically impossible predictions, such as infinite velocity at the trailing edge, and an indeterminate amount of lift. It's the physical constraint that makes the mathematical problem solvable.

### Step 5: Solving for Circulation and Lift

*   **Plain English:** By combining the idea of the vortex sheet, the boundary condition, and the Kutta condition, we can mathematically solve for the distribution of vortex strengths along the wing. Once we know the total strength of all these vortices (the total circulation), we can use the Kutta-Joukowski theorem to directly calculate the lift.
*   **Concrete Example:** Imagine you're building a bridge. You calculate the forces on each beam (vortex strength distribution), and then sum them up to find the total load the bridge can carry (total circulation, then total lift).
*   **Formal/Mathematical Version:** The solution for $\gamma(x)$ typically involves a change of variables $x = \frac{c}{2}(1-\cos\theta)$ and expressing $\gamma(x)$ as a Fourier series. After integrating $\gamma(x)$ to find $\Gamma$, and applying the Kutta-Joukowski theorem $L' = \rho V \Gamma$, we arrive at the lift per unit span formula.
    The lift coefficient $C_l$ is found to be:
    $$ C_l = 2\pi\left(\alpha - \alpha_{L=0}\right) $$
    where $\alpha_{L=0}$ is the zero-lift angle of attack, which depends on the camber line $y_c(x)$:
    $$ \alpha_{L=0} = -\frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\pi \frac{dy_c}{dx} (\cos\theta - 1) d\theta $$
    Then, using $L' = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 c C_l$, we get:
    $$ L' = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 c \left[2\pi\left(\alpha - \alpha_{L=0}\right)\right] = \pi\rho V^2 c (\alpha - \alpha_{L=0}) $$
    Now, let's relate this to the formula given in the prompt: $L' = \pi\rho V^2(\alpha + 2\beta/\pi c)$.
    For dimensional consistency, the prompt's formula *must* implicitly include the chord $c$. Assuming the intended formula is $L' = \pi\rho V^2 c (\alpha + 2\beta/\pi c)$, then by comparison:
    $$ -\alpha_{L=0} = \frac{2\beta}{\pi c} $$
    This means $\beta = -\frac{\pi c}{2} \alpha_{L=0}$. Therefore, $\beta$ is a parameter with units of length (since $\alpha_{L=0}$ is dimensionless and $c$ is length), representing the camber's contribution to lift.
*   **What could go wrong:** Forgetting the assumptions of the theory (inviscid, incompressible, thin, small $\alpha$) means the formula will give inaccurate results outside its valid range. Also, misinterpreting the units or the meaning of $\beta$ can lead to incorrect calculations.

## 5. Worked examples — multiple, with every step shown

As discussed in Step 5 of "The core idea," the given formula $L' = \pi\rho V^2(\alpha + 2\beta/\pi c)$ is dimensionally inconsistent for lift per unit span unless $(\alpha + 2\beta/\pi c)$ is interpreted as having units of length. The standard, dimensionally correct form derived from thin airfoil theory is $L' = \pi\rho V^2 c (\alpha - \alpha_{L=0})$, where $\alpha_{L=0}$ is the zero-lift angle of attack. For these examples, we will use the standard form, and clarify how $2\beta/\pi c$ relates to $-\alpha_{L=0}$.

Let's define the standard form we will use for calculations:
$$ L' = \pi\rho V^2 c (\alpha - \alpha_{L=0}) $$
where $\alpha_{L=0}$ is the zero-lift angle of attack (in radians).
From the prompt's formula, if we assume the missing 'c' (chord length) is implied:
$$ \alpha_{L=0} = - \frac{2\beta}{\pi c} $$
So the prompt's formula can be written as $L' = \pi\rho V^2 c (\alpha - \alpha_{L=0})$.

### Example 1: Lift on a Flat Plate Airfoil

**Problem:** A flat plate airfoil with a chord length of 1.5 meters is flying at an angle of attack of 5 degrees through air with a density of $1.225 \, \text{kg/m}^3$ at a speed of $50 \, \text{m/s}$. Calculate the lift per unit span.

**Given:**
*   Chord length, $c = 1.5 \, \text{m}$
*   Angle of attack, $\alpha = 5^\circ$
*   Air density, $\rho = 1.225 \, \text{kg/m}^3$
*   Freestream velocity, $V = 50 \, \text{m/s}$
*   For a flat plate, there is no camber, so the zero-lift angle of attack $\alpha_{L=0} = 0$. (This implies $\beta=0$ in the prompt's formula, as $2\beta/\pi c = 0$).

**What we want:** Lift per unit span, $L'$.

**Solution:**

1.  **Convert angle of attack to radians:**
    $$ \alpha_{\text{rad}} = \alpha_{\text{deg}} \times \frac{\pi}{180^\circ} $$
    $$ \alpha_{\text{rad}} = 5^\circ \times \frac{\pi}{180^\circ} = \frac{5\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{36} \approx 0.08727 \, \text{radians} $$
    *Explanation:* Thin airfoil theory equations are derived using calculus, which assumes angles are in radians. Always convert degrees to radians for calculations involving $\alpha$.

2.  **Identify the zero-lift angle of attack:**
    For a flat plate airfoil, there is no curvature (camber), so it generates zero lift when the angle of attack is zero.
    $$ \alpha_{L=0} = 0 \, \text{radians} $$
    *Explanation:* The camber term in the lift equation accounts for lift generated purely by the airfoil's shape. A flat plate has no shape-induced lift at zero angle of attack.

3.  **Apply the thin airfoil theory lift formula (standard form):**
    $$ L' = \pi\rho V^2 c (\alpha - \alpha_{L=0}) $$
    *Explanation:* This is the fundamental equation for lift per unit span derived from thin airfoil theory, incorporating the chord length for dimensional correctness.

4.  **Substitute the known values into the formula:**
    $$ L' = \pi \times (1.225 \, \text{kg/m}^3) \times (50 \, \text{m/s})^2 \times (1.5 \, \text{m}) \times (0.08727 \, \text{radians} - 0 \, \text{radians}) $$
    *Explanation:* We're plugging in the numerical values for density, velocity, chord, and angle of attack.

5.  **Calculate the square of the velocity:**
    $$ (50 \, \text{m/s})^2 = 2500 \, \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 $$
    *Explanation:* This is a straightforward arithmetic step.

6.  **Perform the multiplication:**
    $$ L' = \pi \times 1.225 \times 2500 \times 1.5 \times 0.08727 $$
    $$ L' \approx 3.14159 \times 1.225 \times 2500 \times 1.5 \times 0.08727 $$
    $$ L' \approx 1500.2 \, \text{N/m} $$
    *Explanation:* Multiplying all the terms together yields the final lift per unit span. The units work out as: $\text{kg/m}^3 \times \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 \times \text{m} = \text{kg m/s}^2 / \text{m} = \text{N/m}$.

**Final Answer:**
$$ \boxed{L' \approx 1500.2 \, \text{N/m}} $$

**Reflection:** This example was straightforward because the flat plate has no camber, simplifying the $\alpha_{L=0}$ term to zero. The main trick was ensuring the angle of attack was converted to radians.

### Example 2: Lift on a Cambered Airfoil

**Problem:** An airfoil with a chord length of $2 \, \text{m}$ has a zero-lift angle of attack $\alpha_{L=0} = -3^\circ$. It is flying at $V = 70 \, \text{m/s}$ at an angle of attack $\alpha = 4^\circ$ in air with $\rho = 1.0 \, \text{kg/m}^3$. Calculate the lift per unit span.

**Given:**
*   Chord length, $c = 2 \, \text{m}$
*   Zero-lift angle of attack, $\alpha_{L=0} = -3^\circ$
*   Freestream velocity, $V = 70 \, \text{m/s}$
*   Angle of attack, $\alpha = 4^\circ$
*   Air density, $\rho = 1.0 \, \text{kg/m}^3$

**What we want:** Lift per unit span, $L'$.

**Solution:**

1.  **Convert angles to radians:**
    $$ \alpha_{\text{rad}} = 4^\circ \times \frac{\pi}{180^\circ} = \frac{4\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{45} \approx 0.06981 \, \text{radians} $$
    $$ \alpha_{L=0, \text{rad}} = -3^\circ \times \frac{\pi}{180^\circ} = -\frac{3\pi}{180} = -\frac{\pi}{60} \approx -0.05236 \, \text{radians} $$
    *Explanation:* Both the angle of attack and the zero-lift angle of attack must be in radians for the formula.

2.  **Apply the thin airfoil theory lift formula (standard form):**
    $$ L' = \pi\rho V^2 c (\alpha - \alpha_{L=0}) $$
    *Explanation:* This is the core formula. Note that a negative $\alpha_{L=0}$ means the airfoil generates lift even at zero geometric angle of attack, which is typical for positively cambered airfoils.

3.  **Substitute the known values into the formula:**
    $$ L' = \pi \times (1.0 \, \text{kg/m}^3) \times (70 \, \text{m/s})^2 \times (2 \, \text{m}) \times (0.06981 \, \text{radians} - (-0.05236 \, \text{radians})) $$
    *Explanation:* Carefully substitute all values, paying attention to the negative sign of $\alpha_{L=0}$.

4.  **Calculate the term inside the parenthesis:**
    $$ (\alpha - \alpha_{L=0}) = (0.06981 - (-0.05236)) = (0.06981 + 0.05236) = 0.12217 \, \text{radians} $$
    *Explanation:* Subtracting a negative number is equivalent to adding its positive counterpart. This combined term represents the *effective* angle of attack for lift generation.

5.  **Calculate the square of the velocity:**
    $$ (70 \, \text{m/s})^2 = 4900 \, \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 $$
    *Explanation:* Standard arithmetic.

6.  **Perform the final multiplication:**
    $$ L' = \pi \times 1.0 \times 4900 \times 2 \times 0.12217 $$
    $$ L' \approx 3.14159 \times 1.0 \times 4900 \times 2 \times 0.12217 $$
    $$ L' \approx 3769.8 \, \text{N/m} $$
    *Explanation:* Multiply all terms to get the lift per unit span.

**Final Answer:**
$$ \boxed{L' \approx 3769.8 \, \text{N/m}} $$

**Reflection:** This example highlights the importance of the zero-lift angle of attack for cambered airfoils. A negative $\alpha_{L=0}$ means the airfoil is effectively "more angled" into the flow than its geometric angle of attack suggests, leading to more lift.

## 6. Common mistakes and traps

1.  **Forgetting to convert angles to radians:** All trigonometric functions and small-angle approximations in physics equations (like those in thin airfoil theory) assume angles are in radians. Using degrees directly will lead to incorrect numerical results.
2.  **Ignoring the "thin" and "small angle" assumptions:** Thin airfoil theory is a linear approximation. It breaks down at high angles of attack (where flow separation occurs, leading to stall) or for very thick airfoils. Applying it outside its valid range yields inaccurate predictions.
3.  **Dimensional inconsistency:** As highlighted in our discussion, the formula as presented in the prompt ($L' = \pi\rho V^2(\alpha + 2\beta/\pi c)$) is dimensionally incorrect for "lift per unit span" unless $(\alpha + 2\beta/\pi c)$ is interpreted as having units of length. Always perform a unit check; the standard form $L' = \pi\rho V^2 c (\alpha - \alpha_{L=0})$ correctly yields N/m.
4.  **Confusing $\alpha$ and $\alpha_{L=0}$:** $\alpha$ is the geometric angle of attack, while $\alpha_{L=0}$ is the *zero-lift angle of attack*, which is non-zero for cambered airfoils. It's crucial to subtract $\alpha_{L=0}$ from $\alpha$ to get the effective angle that produces lift.
5.  **Misinterpreting the camber parameter $\beta$:** In the context of the prompt's formula, $\beta$ is a parameter (with units of length) that quantifies the effect of camber on the zero-lift angle of attack. It is not an angle itself and should not be confused with the angle of sideslip (also sometimes denoted $\beta$).
6.  **Applying to compressible or viscous flow:** Thin airfoil theory assumes incompressible and inviscid flow. For high speeds (Mach numbers > 0.3) or situations where viscous effects (like boundary layers and flow separation) are significant, this theory is insufficient and more advanced methods are needed.

## 7. Textbook-precise explanation

Thin airfoil theory is a linearized analytical method used to determine the pressure distribution, lift, and pitching moment for two-dimensional, incompressible, inviscid flow over thin airfoils at small angles of attack. The theory simplifies the airfoil geometry by collapsing it onto its camber line, which is assumed to be nearly flat. The thickness of the airfoil is neglected in the primary lift calculation, though its effect on pressure drag can be considered separately.

The fundamental approach involves modeling the airfoil's influence on the flow by distributing a continuous sheet of vortices, $\gamma(x)$, along the chord line ($0 \le x \le c$). The freestream flow is assumed to be uniform, $V_\infty$. The total velocity field $\vec{V}$ is then the superposition of the freestream velocity $\vec{V}_\infty$ and the velocity induced by the vortex sheet, $\vec{v}_{ind}$.

The key boundary condition is that the flow must be tangent to the airfoil surface. For a thin airfoil, this translates to the flow being tangent to the camber line $y_c(x)$. The slope of the camber line is $dy_c/dx$. Thus, the vertical component of the total velocity at the camber line must match the local slope:
$$ v(x) = V_\infty \left( \alpha - \frac{dy_c}{dx} \right) $$
where $v(x)$ is the induced vertical velocity at a point $x$ on the chord line due to the vortex sheet, and $\alpha$ is the geometric angle of attack (in radians).
The induced velocity $v(x)$ from a vortex sheet $\gamma(\xi)$ is given by the integral:
$$ v(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^c \frac{\gamma(\xi)}{x-\xi} d\xi $$
Equating these two expressions for $v(x)$ yields an integral equation for $\gamma(x)$:
$$ \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^c \frac{\gamma(\xi)}{x-\xi} d\xi = V_\infty \left( \alpha - \frac{dy_c}{dx} \right) $$
To solve this singular integral equation, a change of variables is typically employed: $x = \frac{c}{2}(1-\cos\theta)$, where $0 \le \theta \le \pi$. The vortex strength $\gamma(x)$ is then expressed as a Fourier series in $\theta$:
$$ \gamma(\theta) = 2V_\infty \left( A_0 \frac{1+\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty A_n \sin(n\theta) \right) $$
The coefficients $A_0$ and $A_n$ are determined by applying the boundary condition and the Kutta condition.

The **Kutta condition** states that the velocity must be finite at the trailing edge ($x=c$, or $\theta=0$). This implies that $\gamma(c)=0$, which simplifies the solution for the coefficients. Specifically, it ensures that the coefficient of the $\frac{1+\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$ term (which would lead to infinite velocity at the trailing edge if $A_0$ were not uniquely determined) is correctly handled.
