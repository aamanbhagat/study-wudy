## 1. What it is — in plain English

Imagine you have a big bowl of popcorn kernels. These aren't just any kernels; they're "magic" kernels, and each one has a tiny, unpredictable timer inside it. You don't know exactly when any *single* kernel will pop, but you know that over time, they *will* pop.

Now, imagine that every few minutes, a certain *fraction* of the unpopped kernels decides to pop. If you start with 100 kernels, maybe 10% pop in the first minute, leaving 90. Then, in the next minute, 10% of those 90 pop (which is 9 kernels), leaving 81. See how the *number* of kernels popping decreases as the total number of unpopped kernels shrinks?

This is exactly how radioactive decay works. Unstable atomic nuclei are like those magic kernels. They are "radioactive" because they spontaneously transform into a more stable form by emitting particles or energy. We can't predict when a *single* atom will decay, but for a large group of identical atoms, we can accurately predict how many will decay over a specific period. This predictable decline in the number of unstable atoms over time is what the decay law describes.

The "half-life" is just a special way to talk about how fast this popping happens. It's the time it takes for exactly half of your initial magic kernels (or radioactive atoms) to pop. If the half-life is one minute, then after one minute, half are gone. After *another* minute, half of the *remaining* half are gone, and so on.

Finally, "activity" is simply how many kernels are popping *right now*, every second. A bowl with lots of kernels popping quickly has high activity, while a bowl where kernels pop slowly has low activity. It's a measure of how "radioactive" a sample is at any given moment.

## 2. Why it matters — real-world applications

The principles of radioactive decay, half-life, and activity are fundamental to understanding many natural phenomena and are indispensable in a vast array of modern technologies.

1.  **Carbon Dating (Archaeology & Geology):** Perhaps the most famous application. Living organisms absorb carbon-14, a radioactive isotope, from the atmosphere. When an organism dies, it stops absorbing carbon-14, and the existing carbon-14 begins to decay with a known half-life of approximately 5,730 years. By measuring the remaining carbon-14 activity in an ancient artifact (like a wooden tool or a fossil bone) and comparing it to the initial activity (estimated from atmospheric levels), scientists can accurately determine its age. This has revolutionized our understanding of human history and geological timelines.
2.  **Medical Imaging and Therapy (Medicine):** Radioactive isotopes (radiopharmaceuticals) are crucial in modern medicine. For example, in Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scans, a patient is injected with a glucose molecule containing a short-lived radioactive isotope like Fluorine-18 (half-life ~110 minutes). This isotope decays, emitting positrons that interact with electrons, producing gamma rays detected by the scanner. This allows doctors to visualize metabolic activity in tissues, helping diagnose cancers, neurological disorders, and heart conditions. The short half-life ensures the patient isn't exposed to radiation for too long. Similarly, targeted radiation therapy uses isotopes to destroy cancer cells.
3.  **Nuclear Power Generation (Energy):** Nuclear reactors generate electricity by controlling the fission (splitting) of heavy, unstable nuclei, typically Uranium-235. The decay products of these fissions are often radioactive and have varying half-lives. Understanding decay laws is vital for designing safe reactors, managing nuclear waste (which can remain radioactive for thousands of years), and predicting the behavior of spent fuel rods. Companies like Westinghouse Electric Company and EDF Energy heavily rely on this physics.
4.  **Space Exploration (Aerospace):** Many deep-space probes, like the Voyager probes or the Perseverance rover on Mars, use Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators (RTGs) for power. RTGs convert the heat generated by the natural radioactive decay of Plutonium-238 (half-life ~87.7 years) into electricity. This provides a reliable, long-lasting power source for missions far from the sun where solar panels are impractical. NASA and its contractors, like Lockheed Martin, are key users of this technology.

## 3. Prerequisites — what you must know first

Before diving deep into the decay law, ensure you have a solid grasp of these fundamental concepts:

*   **Basic Atomic Structure:** Understanding of protons, neutrons, electrons, atomic number, mass number, and isotopes.
*   **Nuclear Stability:** General idea that some atomic nuclei are unstable and undergo transformations to become more stable.
*   **Exponential Functions:** Familiarity with the function $e^x$, its properties, and how it represents growth or decay.
*   **Natural Logarithms:** Understanding of $\ln(x)$ as the inverse of $e^x$ and its properties, particularly $\ln(e^x) = x$ and $e^{\ln(x)} = x$.
*   **Derivatives (Calculus):** Knowledge of how to calculate derivatives, especially of exponential functions ($d/dx (e^{ax}) = ae^{ax}$).
*   **Integrals (Calculus):** Knowledge of how to perform basic integration, particularly integrating $1/x$ and $e^{ax}$.
*   **Differential Equations (Basic):** An intuitive understanding that equations involving derivatives describe rates of change, and solving them gives the function itself.
*   **Probability (Basic):** The idea that individual events can be random, but patterns emerge in large numbers.

## 4. The core idea — step by step

### Step 1: The Randomness of Individual Decay

**Plain English:** Imagine you have a single, unstable atom. You absolutely cannot predict *when* that specific atom will decay. It could decay in the next second, or it could last for billions of years. It's purely a matter of chance, like rolling a die where you don't know how many sides it has or what number will come up.

**Small concrete example:** If you have one atom of Carbon-14, you can't say, "This atom will decay in exactly 5,730 years." It's like saying, "This specific lottery ticket *will* win the jackpot on Tuesday." You just don't know.

**The formal/mathematical version:** At the quantum level, radioactive decay is a stochastic (random) process. The probability of a single nucleus decaying in a small time interval $dt$ is proportional to $dt$.
$$ P(\text{decay in } dt) = \lambda dt $$
Here, $\lambda$ is the *decay constant*, which represents the intrinsic probability per unit time for a single nucleus to decay. It has units of inverse time (e.g., s⁻¹).

**What could go wrong:** Students often misunderstand this randomness. They might think that if the half-life is 10 years, an atom *must* decay within 20 years. This is incorrect. An atom *could* theoretically exist indefinitely, though the probability becomes astronomically small after many half-lives.

### Step 2: The Predictability of Large Numbers

**Plain English:** While you can't predict when one kernel of popcorn will pop, if you have a huge bag of kernels, you *can* predict quite accurately how many will pop in the next minute. The same applies to radioactive atoms. When you have an enormous number of them (like $10^{23}$ atoms in a gram of material), the individual randomness averages out, and a very precise pattern emerges for the *entire group*.

**Small concrete example:** If you start with $10^{20}$ atoms of a radioactive isotope, and its decay constant means 1% of them decay per second, you can reliably say that after one second, about $10^{18}$ atoms will have decayed, leaving $0.99 \times 10^{20}$ atoms. You wouldn't expect exactly $10^{18}$ every time, but for such large numbers, the deviation is negligible.

**The formal/mathematical version:** If $N$ is the number of undecayed radioactive nuclei at time $t$, then the rate of change of $N$ with respect to time, $dN/dt$, is proportional to the number of nuclei present. The more nuclei you have, the more decays will occur in a given time. Since $N$ is decreasing, the rate is negative:
$$ \frac{dN}{dt} \propto -N $$
Introducing the decay constant $\lambda$ (from Step 1) as the proportionality constant:
$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N $$
This is a first-order linear differential equation.

**What could go wrong:** Confusing the *rate* of decay with the *number* of atoms. The rate of decay is how *fast* the number of atoms is changing, not the number of atoms itself.

### Step 3: The Decay Law — $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$

**Plain English:** This is the core formula that tells you exactly how many radioactive atoms are left ($N$) after a certain amount of time ($t$), if you started with a known number of atoms ($N_0$) and know the decay constant ($\lambda$) for that specific type of atom. It shows an exponential decrease: the number of atoms shrinks rapidly at first, then more slowly as fewer atoms remain to decay.

**Small concrete example:** Let's say you start with $N_0 = 1000$ atoms, and the decay constant $\lambda = 0.1 \text{ s}^{-1}$. After $t=5$ seconds, how many atoms are left?
$N = 1000 \cdot e^{(-0.1 \text{ s}^{-1} \cdot 5 \text{ s})} = 1000 \cdot e^{-0.5} \approx 1000 \cdot 0.6065 = 606.5$ atoms. (Since atoms are discrete, we'd round to 607 or 606).

**The formal/mathematical version:** We solve the differential equation from Step 2:
$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N $$
This is a separable differential equation. We can rearrange it to:
$$ \frac{dN}{N} = -\lambda dt $$
Now, integrate both sides. Let $N_0$ be the number of nuclei at time $t=0$, and $N(t)$ be the number at time $t$:
$$ \int_{N_0}^{N(t)} \frac{1}{N'} dN' = \int_{0}^{t} -\lambda dt' $$
$$ [\ln(N')]_{N_0}^{N(t)} = [-\lambda t']_{0}^{t} $$
$$ \ln(N(t)) - \ln(N_0) = -\lambda t - (-\lambda \cdot 0) $$
$$ \ln\left(\frac{N(t)}{N_0}\right) = -\lambda t $$
To isolate $N(t)$, we exponentiate both sides using the base $e$:
$$ e^{\ln\left(\frac{N(t)}{N_0}\right)} = e^{-\lambda t} $$
$$ \frac{N(t)}{N_0} = e^{-\lambda t} $$
Finally, we get the **Radioactive Decay Law**:
$$ N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$
Where:
*   $N(t)$ is the number of undecayed nuclei remaining at time $t$.
*   $N_0$ is the initial number of undecayed nuclei at $t=0$.
*   $\lambda$ (lambda) is the decay constant, a positive value specific to each isotope, representing the probability of decay per unit time. Its units are usually inverse seconds (s⁻¹), inverse minutes (min⁻¹), or inverse years (yr⁻¹).
*   $t$ is the elapsed time.

**What could go wrong:** Forgetting that $N_0$ is the *initial* number of atoms, not the total number ever present. Also, ensure units for $\lambda$ and $t$ are consistent (e.g., if $\lambda$ is in s⁻¹, $t$ must be in seconds).

### Step 4: Half-life ($T_{1/2}$)

**Plain English:** The half-life is simply the amount of time it takes for half of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay. It's a very intuitive way to describe how quickly a substance decays. If an isotope has a half-life of 10 years, then after 10 years, you'll have half of your original sample left. After *another* 10 years (total 20 years), you'll have half of *that* half, meaning one-quarter of your original sample.

**Small concrete example:** If you start with 1 kg of a radioactive substance with a half-life of 5 days:
*   After 5 days: 0.5 kg remains.
*   After 10 days: 0.25 kg remains.
*   After 15 days: 0.125 kg remains.
Notice it's always half of what was present *at the beginning of that half-life period*.

**The formal/mathematical version:** We can derive the relationship between half-life ($T_{1/2}$) and the decay constant ($\lambda$) using the decay law. By definition, when $t = T_{1/2}$, the number of remaining nuclei $N(t)$ is exactly half of the initial number, i.e., $N(t) = N_0/2$.
Substitute these into the decay law:
$$ \frac{N_0}{2} = N_0 e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} $$
Divide both sides by $N_0$:
$$ \frac{1}{2} = e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} $$
To solve for $T_{1/2}$, take the natural logarithm of both sides:
$$ \ln\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \ln(e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}) $$
Using logarithm properties ($\ln(1/x) = -\ln(x)$ and $\ln(e^y) = y$):
$$ -\ln(2) = -\lambda T_{1/2} $$
$$ \ln(2) = \lambda T_{1/2} $$
Finally, rearrange to find $T_{1/2}$:
$$ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} $$
Since $\ln(2) \approx 0.693$, we often see:
$$ T_{1/2} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda} $$
This equation shows that a larger decay constant (faster decay probability) means a shorter half-life.

**What could go wrong:** Students sometimes think that after two half-lives, *all* of the substance is gone. No, after two half-lives, $1/2 \times 1/2 = 1/4$ remains. After $n$ half-lives, $(1/2)^n$ of the original amount remains. Also, ensure $\lambda$ and $T_{1/2}$ units are consistent (e.g., if $\lambda$ is in s⁻¹, $T_{1/2}$ will be in seconds).

### Step 5: Activity ($A$)

**Plain English:** Activity is a measure of how "radioactive" a sample is at a given moment. It tells you how many nuclei are decaying *per second*. A sample with high activity is decaying rapidly, emitting lots of radiation. A sample with low activity is decaying slowly. It's like the "popping rate" of our popcorn kernels.

**Small concrete example:** If a sample has an activity of 100 Becquerels (Bq), it means 100 atoms are decaying every second. If another sample has an activity of 1 Curie (Ci), that's $3.7 \times 10^{10}$ decays per second – a much more active (and potentially dangerous) sample.

**The formal/mathematical version:** Activity, $A$, is defined as the magnitude of the rate of decay of the number of nuclei, $|dN/dt|$.
From Step 2, we know:
$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N $$
So, the activity $A$ is:
$$ A = \left|\frac{dN}{dt}\right| = \lambda N $$
Since $N$ itself decays exponentially ($N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$), the activity also decays exponentially:
$$ A(t) = \lambda N_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$
We can define the initial activity, $A_0$, as the activity at $t=0$:
$$ A_0 = \lambda N_0 $$
Substituting this into the equation for $A(t)$:
$$ A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$
This means that activity follows the same exponential decay law as the number of nuclei.

**Units of Activity:**
*   The SI unit of activity is the **Becquerel (Bq)**, defined as one decay per second ($1 \text{ Bq} = 1 \text{ decay/s}$).
*   A common older unit is the **Curie (Ci)**, defined as the activity of 1 gram of Radium-226.
    $$ 1 \text{ Ci} = 3.7 \times 10^{10} \text{ Bq} $$
    This is a very large unit, often used for strong sources or in industrial/medical contexts. Millicuries (mCi) or microcuries ($\mu$Ci) are more common for diagnostic medical applications.

**What could go wrong:** Confusing activity ($A$) with the number of nuclei ($N$). While they are related ($A = \lambda N$), they are distinct concepts. Activity describes the *rate* of decay, while $N$ describes the *quantity* of undecayed material. Also, be careful with unit conversions between Bq and Ci.

## 5. Worked examples — multiple, with every step shown

### Example 1: Basic Decay Calculation (Easy)

**Problem:** A sample of Iodine-131 has an initial number of $N_0 = 5.0 \times 10^{15}$ nuclei. The decay constant for Iodine-131 is $\lambda = 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ s}^{-1}$. How many Iodine-131 nuclei remain after 10 days?

**Given:**
*   Initial number of nuclei, $N_0 = 5.0 \times 10^{15}$
*   Decay constant, $\lambda = 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ s}^{-1}$
*   Time elapsed, $t = 10 \text{ days}$

**Want:** Number of remaining nuclei, $N(t)$

**Solution:**

1.  **Ensure consistent units for time:** The decay constant $\lambda$ is given in s⁻¹, so we must convert the time $t$ from days to seconds.
    $$ t = 10 \text{ days} \times \frac{24 \text{ hours}}{1 \text{ day}} \times \frac{60 \text{ minutes}}{1 \text{ hour}} \times \frac{60 \text{ seconds}}{1 \text{ minute}} $$
    $$ t = 10 \times 24 \times 60 \times 60 \text{ s} = 864,000 \text{ s} $$
    *Explanation: This step is crucial because the exponential function $e^{-\lambda t}$ requires the product $\lambda t$ to be unitless. If $\lambda$ is in s⁻¹, $t$ must be in seconds.*

2.  **Apply the radioactive decay law:** The formula is $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$.
    $$ N(t) = (5.0 \times 10^{15}) \cdot e^{-(1.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ s}^{-1}) \cdot (864,000 \text{ s})} $$
    *Explanation: We plug in the given values for $N_0$, $\lambda$, and the converted $t$ into the decay law equation.*

3.  **Calculate the exponent:**
    $$ -\lambda t = -(1.0 \times 10^{-6}) \times (8.64 \times 10^5) = -0.864 $$
    *Explanation: Multiply the decay constant by the time. Notice the units (s⁻¹ and s) cancel out, resulting in a dimensionless exponent, as expected.*

4.  **Calculate the exponential term:**
    $$ e^{-0.864} \approx 0.4214 $$
    *Explanation: Use a calculator to evaluate $e$ raised to the power of -0.864.*

5.  **Calculate the final number of nuclei:**
    $$ N(t) = (5.0 \times 10^{15}) \times 0.4214 $$
    $$ N(t) = 2.107 \times 10^{15} $$
    *Explanation: Multiply the initial number of nuclei by the fraction remaining (the exponential term).*

6.  **Round to appropriate significant figures:**
    $$ \boxed{N(t) = 2.1 \times 10^{15} \text{ nuclei}} $$
    *Explanation: The initial number $N_0$ and decay constant $\lambda$ were given with two significant figures, so the answer should also be rounded to two significant figures.*

**Reflection:** This example highlights the importance of unit consistency, especially for time. It's a direct application of the decay law.

### Example 2: Calculating Half-Life (Medium)

**Problem:** A radioactive isotope has a decay constant of $\lambda = 0.045 \text{ min}^{-1}$. What is its half-life in minutes?

**Given:**
*   Decay constant, $\lambda = 0.045 \text{ min}^{-1}$

**Want:** Half-life, $T_{1/2}$ in minutes.

**Solution:**

1.  **Recall the relationship between half-life and decay constant:**
    $$ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} $$
    *Explanation: This is the derived formula linking half-life to the decay constant. It's a fundamental relationship.*

2.  **Substitute the given decay constant:**
    $$ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{0.045 \text{ min}^{-1}} $$
    *Explanation: Plug in the value of $\lambda$. Since $\lambda$ is in min⁻¹, $T_{1/2}$ will naturally come out in minutes.*

3.  **Calculate the value of $\ln(2)$:**
    $$ \ln(2) \approx 0.693147 $$
    *Explanation: Use a calculator for the natural logarithm of 2.*

4.  **Perform the division:**
    $$ T_{1/2} = \frac{0.693147}{0.045 \text{ min}^{-1}} $$
    $$ T_{1/2} \approx 15.403 \text{ min} $$
    *Explanation: Divide $\ln(2)$ by the decay constant. The units (1 / (min⁻¹)) correctly result in minutes.*

5.  **Round to appropriate significant figures:**
    $$ \boxed{T_{1/2} = 15 \text{ min}} $$
    *Explanation: The decay constant was given with two significant figures, so the answer is rounded to two significant figures.*

**Reflection:** This example demonstrates how to find half-life directly from the decay constant. It's a straightforward application of a derived formula.

### Example 3: Time for a Certain Percentage Decay (Medium)

**Problem:** How long will it take for 75% of a sample of Technetium-99m (half-life $T_{1/2} = 6.0 \text{ hours}$) to decay?

**Given:**
*   Half-life, $T_{1/2} = 6.0 \text{ hours}$
*   Percentage decayed = 75%

**Want:** Time elapsed, $t$

**Solution:**

1.  **Determine the remaining percentage:** If 75% has decayed, then $100\% - 75\% = 25\%$ remains.
    *Explanation: The decay law $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ deals with the *remaining* amount, not the decayed amount.*

2.  **Express the remaining amount as a fraction of the initial amount:**
    $$ \frac{N(t)}{N_0} = 0.25 $$
    *Explanation: $N(t)/N_0$ is the fraction of the initial nuclei that are still undecayed.*

3.  **Calculate the decay constant ($\lambda$) from the half-life:**
    $$ \lambda = \frac{\ln(2)}{T_{1/2}} $$
    $$ \lambda = \frac{0.693}{6.0 \text{ hours}} $$
    $$ \lambda \approx 0.1155 \text{ hours}^{-1} $$
    *Explanation: We need $\lambda$ for the decay law. Since $T_{1/2}$ is in hours, $\lambda$ will be in hours⁻¹, which is consistent for calculating time in hours.*

4.  **Apply the radioactive decay law in ratio form:**
    $$ \frac{N(t)}{N_0} = e^{-\lambda t} $$
    $$ 0.25 = e^{-(0.1155 \text{ hours}^{-1}) t} $$
    *Explanation: Substitute the known values into the decay law. We are solving for $t$.*

5.  **Take the natural logarithm of both sides:**
    $$ \ln(0.25) = \ln(e^{-0.1155 t}) $$
    $$ \ln(0.25) = -0.1155 t $$
    *Explanation: Taking the natural logarithm allows us to bring the exponent down and solve for $t$. Recall $\ln(e^x) = x$.*

6.  **Calculate $\ln(0.25)$:**
    $$ \ln(0.25) \approx -1.386 $$
    *Explanation: Evaluate the natural logarithm.*

7.  **Solve for $t$:**
    $$ -1.386 = -0.1155 t $$
    $$ t = \frac{-1.386}{-0.1155 \text{ hours}^{-1}} $$
    $$ t \approx 12.00 \text{ hours} $$
    *Explanation: Divide both sides by $-\lambda$. The negative signs cancel, and the units (1 / (hours⁻¹)) correctly result in hours.*

8.  **Round to appropriate significant figures:**
    $$ \boxed{t = 12 \text{ hours}} $$
    *Explanation: The half-life was given with two significant figures, so the answer is rounded to two significant figures.*

**Reflection:** This problem requires an extra step of calculating $\lambda$ first and then solving for time using logarithms. It also implicitly connects to the idea that 75% decay means 25% remaining, which is equivalent to two half-lives ($1/2 \times 1/2 = 1/4 = 25\%$). So, $2 \times 6 \text{ hours} = 12 \text{ hours}$. This mental check is useful.

### Example 4: Carbon Dating (Hard)

**Problem:** A fossil bone is found to have a Carbon-14 activity of 0.125 Bq per gram of carbon. Living organisms typically have a Carbon-14 activity of 0.250 Bq per gram of carbon. Given that the half-life of Carbon-14 is 5730 years, estimate the age of the fossil bone.

**Given:**
*   Current activity, $A(t) = 0.125 \text{ Bq/g}$
*   Initial activity, $A_0 = 0.250 \text{ Bq/g}$
*   Half-life, $T_{1/2} = 5730 \text{ years}$

**Want:** Age of the fossil bone, $t$

**Solution:**

1.  **Calculate the decay constant ($\lambda$) from the half-life:**
    $$ \lambda = \frac{\ln(2)}{T_{1/2}} $$
    $$ \lambda = \frac{0.6931}{5730 \text{ years}} $$
    $$ \lambda \approx 1.2096 \times 10^{-4} \text{ years}^{-1} $$
    *Explanation: Since the half-life is in years, the decay constant will be in years⁻¹, which is suitable for calculating the age in years.*

2.  **Apply the activity decay law:** The activity $A(t)$ also follows an exponential decay similar to the number of nuclei $N(t)$.
    $$ A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$
    *Explanation: This is the same form as the decay law, but applied to activity instead of the number of nuclei.*

3.  **Substitute the given activities and $\lambda$:**
    $$ 0.125 \text{ Bq/g} = (0.250 \text{ Bq/g}) e^{-(1.2096 \times 10^{-4} \text{ years}^{-1}) t} $$
    *Explanation: Plug in the known values. We are solving for $t$.*

4.  **Isolate the exponential term:** Divide both sides by $A_0$.
    $$ \frac{0.125}{0.250} = e^{-(1.2096 \times 10^{-4}) t} $$
    $$ 0.5 = e^{-(1.2096 \times 10^{-4}) t} $$
    *Explanation: Simplify the ratio of activities. This ratio is the fraction of activity remaining, which is also the fraction of nuclei remaining.*

5.  **Take the natural logarithm of both sides:**
    $$ \ln(0.5) = \ln(e^{-(1.2096 \times 10^{-4}) t}) $$
    $$ \ln(0.5) = -(1.2096 \times 10^{-4}) t $$
    *Explanation: This step brings the exponent down, allowing us to solve for $t$.*

6.  **Calculate $\ln(0.5)$:**
    $$ \ln(0.5) \approx -0.6931 $$
    *Explanation: Note that $\ln(0.5) = \ln(1/2) = -\ln(2)$. This is a good check.*

7.  **Solve for $t$:**
    $$ -0.6931 = -(1.2096 \times 10^{-4} \text{ years}^{-1}) t $$
    $$ t = \frac{-0.6931}{-1.2096 \times 10^{-4} \text{ years}^{-1}} $$
    $$ t \approx 5730.0 \text{ years} $$
    *Explanation: Divide by $-\lambda$. The negative signs cancel, and units are years.*

8.  **Round to appropriate significant figures:**
    $$ \boxed{t = 5730 \text{ years}} $$
    *Explanation: The half-life was given with four significant figures, and the activities with three, so four significant figures is reasonable for the age.*

**Reflection:** This example is a classic carbon dating problem. Notice that the remaining activity is exactly half of the initial activity ($0.125/0.250 = 0.5$). This immediately tells us that one half-life has passed, so the age must be equal to the half-life, 5730 years. This is a great shortcut for problems where the ratio is a simple power of 1/2.

## 6. Common mistakes and traps

1.  **Inconsistent Units:** This is the most frequent error. If $\lambda$ is in s⁻¹, $t$ must be in seconds; if $T_{1/2}$ is in years, $\lambda$ should be in years⁻¹, and $t$ in years. The product $\lambda t$ must always be dimensionless.
2.  **Confusing $N$ with $A$:** While both follow the same exponential decay, $N$ is the *number of atoms* and $A$ is the *rate of decay* ($A = \lambda N$). Using $N_0$ when $A_0$ is given (or vice versa) without the $\lambda$ factor is a common trap.
3.  **Linear vs. Exponential Decay:** Assuming that a sample decays linearly. For example, thinking that if 50% decays in 10 years, then 100% will decay in 20 years. This is incorrect due to the exponential nature; after 20 years, only 75% would have decayed (25% remaining).
4.  **Misinterpreting Half-Life:** Believing that after two half-lives, *all* the material has decayed. As shown, after two half-lives, one-quarter (25%) of the original material still remains.
5.  **Forgetting $\ln(2)$:** When converting between $\lambda$ and $T_{1/2}$, forgetting the $\ln(2)$ factor or placing it incorrectly (e.g., $T_{1/2} = \lambda / \ln(2)$). Remember $T_{1/2} = \ln(2) / \lambda$.
6.  **Rounding Too Early:** Rounding intermediate calculation steps can lead to significant errors in the final answer, especially in exponential calculations. Keep extra significant figures during calculations and round only the final answer.
7.  **Confusing "Decayed" with "Remaining":** The decay law $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ gives the *remaining* amount. If a problem asks for the *decayed* amount, you must subtract the remaining amount from the initial amount: $N_{decayed} = N_0 - N(t)$.

## 7. Textbook-precise explanation

Radioactive decay is the spontaneous process by which an unstable atomic nucleus loses energy by emitting radiation. This process is governed by the **Radioactive Decay Law**, which states that the rate of decay of a sample of radioactive nuclei is directly proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present in the sample.

Mathematically, if $N(t)$ represents the number of undecayed nuclei at time $t$, and $N_0$ is the initial number of nuclei at $t=0$, then the rate of change of $N$ with respect to time is given by:
$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N $$
where $\lambda$ is the **decay constant**, a characteristic constant for a specific radionuclide, representing the probability per unit time for a single nucleus to decay. The negative sign indicates that the number of undecayed nuclei decreases over time.

Solving this first-order linear differential equation yields the exponential decay law:
$$ N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$
This equation describes the exponential decrease in the number of parent nuclei over time.

The **half-life ($T_{1/2}$)** of a radioactive isotope is defined as the time required for half of the initial number of radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. By setting $N(t) = N_0/2$ in the decay law and solving for $t$, we derive the relationship:
$$ \frac{N_0}{2} = N_0 e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} $$
$$ \frac{1}{2} = e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} $$
Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:
$$ \ln\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\lambda T_{1/2} $$
$$ -\ln(2) = -\lambda T_{1/2} $$
$$ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} $$
This shows that isotopes with larger decay constants have shorter half-lives, implying faster decay.

The **activity ($A$)** of a radioactive sample is defined as the rate at which nuclei decay in the sample. It is the magnitude of the rate of change of the number of nuclei:
$$ A = \left|\frac{dN}{dt}\right| $$
From the decay law, we know $dN/dt = -\lambda N$, so:
$$ A = \lambda N $$
Since $N$ decays exponentially, activity also decays exponentially:
$$ A(t) = \lambda (N_0 e^{-\lambda t}) $$
Defining the initial activity $A_0 = \lambda N_0$, we can write:
$$ A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$
The SI unit for activity is the **Becquerel (Bq)**, defined as one disintegration per second ($1 \text{ Bq} = 1 \text{ s}^{-1}$). A common non-SI unit is the **Curie (Ci)**, where $1 \text{ Ci} = 3.7 \times 10^{10} \text{ Bq}$.

(See: Krane, Kenneth S. *Introductory Nuclear Physics*. Wiley, 1988, Chapter 6. Serway, Raymond A., and John W. Jewett Jr. *Physics for Scientists and Engineers*. 10th ed. Cengage, 2018, Chapter 31.)

4.  **First-Principles Re-derivation Pathway:**
    If you ever forget the formulas, you can always build them back up from the fundamental idea:

    *   **Start with the core assumption:** The rate of decay of radioactive nuclei is proportional to the number of nuclei present.
        $$ \frac{dN}{dt} \propto -N $$
    *   **Introduce the decay constant:**
        $$ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N $$
    *   **Separate variables and integrate:**
        $$ \int_{N_0}^{N(t)} \frac{1}{N'} dN' = \int_{0}^{t} -\lambda dt' $$
        $$ \ln(N(t)) - \ln(N_0) = -\lambda t $$
        $$ \ln\left(\frac{N(t)}{N_0}\right) = -\lambda t $$
    *   **Exponentiate to get the Decay Law:**
        $$ N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$
    *   **Derive Half-Life from the Decay Law:** Set $N(t) = N_0/2$ and $t = T_{1/2}$:
        $$ \frac{N_0}{2} = N_0 e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} $$
        $$ \frac{1}{2} = e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}} $$
        $$ \ln\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\lambda T_{1/2} $$
        $$ -\ln(2) = -\lambda T_{1/2} $$
        $$ T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} $$
    *   **Derive Activity from the rate of decay:**
        $$ A = \left|\frac{dN}{dt}\right| $$
        $$ A = |-\lambda N| = \lambda N $$
        Substitute $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$:
        $$ A(t) = \lambda N_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$
        Recognize $A_0 = \lambda N_0$:
        $$ A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t} $$

## 10. Connections — what this leads to

Understanding the decay law, half-life, and activity is foundational for many advanced topics in physics and related fields:

*   **Nuclear Fission and Fusion:** While decay is spontaneous, fission is often induced. However, the products of fission are almost always radioactive, and their subsequent decay chains (and associated half-lives) are crucial for understanding reactor safety, waste management, and the behavior of nuclear weapons.
*   **Radiation Dosimetry and Protection:** Knowledge of activity and decay rates is essential for calculating radiation exposure, designing shielding, and establishing safety protocols in nuclear facilities, hospitals, and for space travel (where astronauts are exposed to cosmic radiation).
*   **Particle Physics and Fundamental Interactions:** Radioactive decay processes (alpha, beta, gamma decay) are manifestations of the strong, weak, and electromagnetic fundamental forces. Studying the decay constant and decay modes provides insights into the nature of these interactions.
*   **Cosmology and Nucleosynthesis:** The abundance of various elements in the universe, including radioactive isotopes, helps scientists understand the processes that occurred during the Big Bang and in stars (stellar nucleosynthesis). The decay of long-lived isotopes like Uranium and Thorium drives much of Earth's internal heat.
*   **Medical Physics and Radiotherapy:** The precise control of radioactive isotopes with specific half-lives is critical for diagnostic imaging (PET, SPECT) and targeted cancer therapies. This topic directly feeds into understanding how different isotopes are chosen for their medical applications.
*   **Geochronology (Advanced Dating Techniques):** Beyond carbon dating, other radiometric dating methods (e.g., Uranium-Lead, Potassium-Argon) use the decay of very long-lived isotopes to date rocks, minerals, and the Earth itself, providing a timeline for geological and planetary evolution.
*   **Advanced Statistical Mechanics:** The probabilistic nature of decay and the emergence of macroscopic predictability from microscopic randomness is a powerful example often used in statistical mechanics.
*   **Astrophysics (Supernovae and Neutron Stars):** The decay of radioactive elements produced in supernovae (e.g., Nickel-56) powers the light curves of these cosmic explosions.
