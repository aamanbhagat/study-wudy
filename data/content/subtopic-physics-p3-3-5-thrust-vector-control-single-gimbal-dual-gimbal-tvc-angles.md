## What it is
Thrust Vector Control (TVC) is the mechanical system used to physically tilt a rocket's engine nozzle, redirecting the exhaust jet to generate steering torque. A single-gimbal system pivots the nozzle along one axis to control either pitch or yaw, while a dual-gimbal system pivots along two orthogonal axes to control both pitch and yaw simultaneously. 

## Why it matters
TVC is the primary method of steering launch vehicles and spacecraft in a vacuum or thin atmosphere, where aerodynamic control surfaces (like fins) are useless. In aerospace engineering, deriving the TVC angles is the foundational step for designing the Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GNC) autopilot loops; the flight computer must continuously calculate and command these angles to fight aerodynamic disturbances (like max-Q) and keep the rocket on its target trajectory.

## When to study it
You must have a firm grasp of rigid body dynamics before studying TVC. Specifically, you need to understand:
1. Center of Mass (CoM) calculations.
2. Torque as a cross product ($\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}$).
3. 3D kinematics, specifically rotation matrices and Euler angles.
If you cannot confidently set up a free body diagram for a rigid body in 3D space and calculate the moments about its CoM, review those topics first.

## How to study it (step by step)
1. **Draw the geometry:** Sketch a 2D free body diagram of a rocket. Label the Center of Mass (CoM), the engine gimbal pivot, the distance $L$ between them, and the thrust vector $\vec{T}$ deflected by an angle $\delta$.
2. **Derive the 2D torque:** Use $\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}$ to write the equation for the torque generated by a single-gimbal system. 
3. **Expand to 3D (Dual-Gimbal):** Define a body coordinate frame (e.g., $x$ is the longitudinal axis). Write the rotation matrix that transforms the thrust vector by a pitch angle $\delta_y$ and a yaw angle $\delta_z$. 
4. **Apply the small-angle approximation:** In flight, TVC angles are usually small ($< 5^\circ$). Simplify your 3D thrust vector using $\sin\delta \approx \delta$ and $\cos\delta \approx 1$. Recalculate the torque vector.
5. **Analyze roll control:** Prove to yourself mathematically why a single dual-gimbal engine cannot provide roll control (torque about the $x$-axis), and sketch a multi-engine configuration that can.

## Key ideas, with intuition

**1. The Lever Arm**
A rocket steers like balancing a broomstick on your hand. The thrust acts at the gimbal pivot. The torque generated depends entirely on the distance $L$ from the rocket's CoM to that pivot. 
$$ \vec{\tau}_{TVC} = \vec{r}_{gimbal} \times \vec{T} $$
Because the CoM shifts upward as propellant is burned, $L$ increases, meaning the same thrust deflection generates *more* torque later in the flight.

**2. Thrust Components and Gimbal Angles**
Let the rocket body's longitudinal axis be $x$. A dual-gimbal engine deflects the thrust vector by pitch ($\delta_y$) and yaw ($\delta_z$). The thrust vector $\vec{T}$ in the body frame is found by rotating the nominal thrust $[-T, 0, 0]^T$ by these angles. Assuming small angles ($\sin\delta \approx \delta, \cos\delta \approx 1$), the lateral forces that actually cause rotation are directly proportional to the angles:
$$ \vec{T}_{body} \approx T \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ \delta_z \\ -\delta_y \end{bmatrix} $$
*(Note: Sign conventions vary by aerospace organization; always define your axes rigorously).*

**3. Control Authority**
The maximum torque the vehicle can produce is its "control authority": $\tau_{max} = T \cdot L \cdot \sin(\delta_{max})$. If an external force (like a wind shear) creates an aerodynamic torque greater than $\tau_{max}$, the rocket cannot recover and will tumble. 

## Worked example
**Problem:** A rocket has a thrust $T = 1000 \text{ N}$ and a CoM-to-gimbal distance $L = 2 \text{ m}$. A wind gust creates an aerodynamic pitch torque of $100 \text{ Nm}$. Find the required single-gimbal TVC pitch angle $\delta$ to perfectly counteract this disturbance.

**Step 1: Set up the torque balance.**
For the rocket to not tumble, the sum of torques about the CoM must be zero.
$$ \sum \tau = 0 \implies \tau_{aero} + \tau_{TVC} = 0 $$
$$ 100 \text{ Nm} + \tau_{TVC} = 0 \implies \tau_{TVC} = -100 \text{ Nm} $$

**Step 2: Express the TVC torque.**
The thrust component perpendicular to the lever arm creates the torque. 
$$ \tau_{TVC} = -L \cdot (T \sin\delta) $$
*(The negative sign indicates the restorative direction).*

**Step 3: Solve for $\delta$.**
$$ -100 = -2 \cdot (1000 \sin\delta) $$
$$ 100 = 2000 \sin\delta $$
$$ \sin\delta = \frac{100}{2000} = 0.05 $$

**Step 4: Calculate the angle.**
$$ \delta = \arcsin(0.05) \approx 0.05 \text{ rad} \approx 2.86^\circ $$

**Reflection:** The required angle is small ($2.86^\circ$), which justifies the small-angle approximation ($\sin\delta \approx \delta$). The engine must gimbal slightly in the direction of the wind disturbance to push the tail back under the CoM, keeping the rocket upright.

## Memory technique — remember this forever
**1. The visual hook:** "Push the tail to move the nose." If the rocket nose is falling to the right, you must gimbal the engine to point the exhaust to the right. This pushes the tail to the right, swinging the nose back to the left. 

**2. The formulas to overlearn:**
$$ \vec{\tau}_{TVC} = \vec{r}_{CoM \to gimbal} \times \vec{T} $$
$$ \tau_{pitch} \approx T \cdot L \cdot \delta_{pitch} \quad \text{(Small angle approximation)} $$

**3. Spaced-repetition schedule:** Review this derivation and the visual hook at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, and 35 days.

**4. First principles pathway:** If you forget the formula, draw the rocket. Place the CoM at the origin $(0,0,0)$. Place the gimbal at $(-L, 0, 0)$. Use basic SOH-CAH-TOA to find the $x$ and $y$ components of the deflected thrust vector. Take the 3D cross product $\vec{r} \times \vec{T}$. The result is your torque.

## Common mistakes
1. **Sign errors in control loops:** A positive gimbal angle $\delta$ often results in a negative torque, depending on your coordinate system. If you drop the negative sign in your autopilot code, the rocket will use positive feedback and instantly rip itself apart.
2. **Assuming one engine can roll:** Students often think a single dual-gimbal engine provides full 3-axis control. It does not. The thrust vector passes through the longitudinal axis, meaning the lever arm for roll is zero. You need at least two engines (or aerodynamic fins/cold gas thrusters) for roll control.
3. **Treating $L$ as a constant:** The distance from the CoM to the gimbal changes continuously as propellant is consumed. Using a static $L$ in a flight simulation will result in an under-damped or over-damped control system later in the flight.

## Self-check
1. If a rocket's CoM moves *closer* to the engine gimbal as fuel burns, does the TVC control authority increase or decrease for a given maximum gimbal angle?
2. Derive the exact 3D torque vector $\vec{\tau}$ for a dual-gimbal engine located at $\vec{r} = [-L, 0, 0]^T$ with pitch angle $\delta_y$ and yaw angle $\delta_z$. Do not use the small-angle approximation.
3. A rocket has two single-gimbal engines mounted side-by-side on the $y$-axis at $y = d$ and $y = -d$. How must they be gimbaled to produce pure roll torque without inducing pitch or yaw? Prove this using the cross product of their respective lever arms and thrust vectors.
