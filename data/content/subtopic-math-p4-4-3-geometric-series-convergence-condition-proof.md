## What it is
A geometric series is the sum of the terms of a geometric sequence. Each term in the sequence is generated by multiplying the preceding term by a fixed, non-zero number called the common ratio, $r$. The series has the form $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} ar^n = a + ar + ar^2 + \dots$, where $a$ is the first term.

## Why it matters
Geometric series are foundational to understanding more complex series and functions, such as Taylor series. In physics, they model processes involving repeated, fractional loss or gain, like radioactive decay, damped oscillations, or the energy states of quantum systems. In computer science, they are used to analyze the complexity of recursive algorithms where the problem size is reduced by a constant factor at each step.

## When to study it
You must be comfortable with the following concepts before proceeding:
1.  **Sequences and their limits:** You need to know what $\lim_{n \to \infty} c^n$ evaluates to for different values of $c$.
2.  **Summation notation ($\Sigma$):** You should be able to read and write series using sigma notation.
3.  **The definition of a convergent series:** A series converges if its sequence of partial sums, $S_n$, approaches a finite limit. That is, $\sum a_n$ converges if $\lim_{n \to \infty} S_n = L$, where $L$ is a finite number.

If you are not solid on these, review them first. Otherwise, you will struggle with the derivation.

## How to study it (step by step)
1.  **Write it out.** For $a=1$, write out the first 5 terms of the geometric series for $r = \frac{1}{2}$, $r = 2$, and $r = -1$. Observe how the terms behave. Do they shrink, grow, or oscillate? This builds intuition.
2.  **Derive the partial sum formula.** This is the crucial algebraic manipulation. Let $S_n = a + ar + ar^2 + \dots + ar^{n-1}$. Write out $rS_n$. Now compute $S_n - rS_n$ and notice the telescoping cancellation. Solve for $S_n$.
3.  **Take the limit.** With the closed-form expression for $S_n$ from step 2, compute $\lim_{n \to \infty} S_n$. Your goal is to find for which values of $r$ this limit is a finite number.
4.  **Analyze the cases for $r$.** The limit from step 3 depends entirely on the term $r^n$. Systematically analyze what happens to $\lim_{n \to \infty} r^n$ when $|r| < 1$, $|r| > 1$, $r=1$, and $r=-1$.
5.  **State the theorem.** Synthesize your findings from step 4 into a formal statement: "The geometric series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} ar^n$ converges if and only if $|r|<1$. If it converges, its sum is $\frac{a}{1-r}$."
6.  **Solve problems.** Find the sum of $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (\frac{3}{4})^n$. Then determine if $\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} (-1.01)^n$ converges. The first reinforces the formula, the second reinforces the condition.

## Key ideas, with intuition
1.  **The Ratio Controls Everything.** The convergence of the series depends exclusively on the common ratio, $r$. Think of it as a feedback loop. If you repeatedly multiply by a number with magnitude less than 1, the result shrinks towards zero. If you multiply by a number with magnitude greater than or equal to 1, the result either grows infinitely or fails to shrink, preventing the sum from settling.

2.  **The Partial Sum Trick.** The formula for the sum is not magic; it comes from a simple, powerful trick. Consider the $n$-th partial sum:
    $$ S_n = a + ar + ar^2 + \dots + ar^{n-1} $$
    Multiply by $r$:
    $$ rS_n = \ \ \ \ \ ar + ar^2 + \dots + ar^{n-1} + ar^n $$
    Subtract the second equation from the first. Nearly all terms cancel:
    $$ S_n - rS_n = a - ar^n $$
    $$ S_n(1-r) = a(1-r^n) \implies S_n = a\frac{1-r^n}{1-r} $$
    This formula for $S_n$ is the key to the entire proof.

3.  **Convergence is about the "tail".** For the infinite sum to be finite, the terms you are adding must eventually become zero. Specifically, for a series $\sum a_n$ to converge, it is *necessary* that $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0$. For a geometric series, $a_n = ar^{n-1}$. This limit is zero only if $|r|<1$. This is the "Test for Divergence" applied to the geometric series, and it explains *why* the condition $|r| \ge 1$ must lead to divergence.

## Worked example
**Problem:** Determine if the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{5}{2^n}$ converges, and if so, find its sum.

**Solution:**
1.  **Identify the form.** The series is $ \frac{5}{2} + \frac{5}{4} + \frac{5}{8} + \dots $. We need to write it in the standard form $\sum ar^k$.
    We can rewrite the general term $\frac{5}{2^n}$ as $5(\frac{1}{2})^n$. So the series is $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 5(\frac{1}{2})^n$.

2.  **Identify the first term $a$ and the ratio $r$.**
    The first term of the series occurs at the starting index, $n=1$. So, $a = 5(\frac{1}{2})^1 = \frac{5}{2}$.
    The common ratio $r$ is the base of the exponent, so $r = \frac{1}{2}$.

3.  **Check the convergence condition.** The condition for convergence is $|r|<1$. Here, $|r| = |\frac{1}{2}| = \frac{1}{2}$, which is less than 1. Therefore, the series converges.

4.  **Apply the sum formula.** The sum of a convergent geometric series is $S = \frac{a}{1-r}$.
    $$ S = \frac{5/2}{1 - 1/2} = \frac{5/2}{1/2} = 5 $$

The series converges to 5.

**Reflection:**
- Step 1 was crucial to match the problem to the known form.
- Step 2 required careful identification of the *actual* first term, not just the coefficient $5$. The starting index $n=1$ matters.
- Step 3 is a mandatory check; without it, applying the formula in step 4 would be invalid.
- Step 4 is a direct application of the derived formula.

## Memory technique — remember this forever
1.  **The Story: Zeno's Walk.** You need to walk to a wall 1 meter away. You take a step that is half the distance ($1/2$ m). Then you take a step that is half of the *remaining* distance ($1/4$ m). Then half of that ($1/8$ m), and so on. Your ratio of step size is $r=1/2$. You take infinitely many steps, but because each step is shrinking, you never pass the wall. You just get infinitesimally close to it. The sum of your steps is $\frac{a}{1-r} = \frac{1/2}{1-1/2} = 1$ meter. If your steps were *not* shrinking ($|r| \ge 1$), you would blow past the wall and walk to infinity.

2.  **Must Overlearn:**
    *   **Convergence Condition:** Converges if and only if $|r| < 1$.
    *   **Sum Formula:** $S = \frac{a}{1-r}$ (where $a$ is the *first term*).

3.  **Spaced Repetition Schedule:**

    During each review, try to re-derive the partial sum formula from scratch.

4.  **First Principles Pathway:** If you forget everything, rebuild it.
    *   Write $S_n = a + ar + \dots + ar^{n-1}$.
    *   Write $rS_n = ar + \dots + ar^n$.
    *   Subtract: $S_n(1-r) = a - ar^n$.
    *   Solve: $S_n = \frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}$.
    *   Take the limit: $\lim_{n \to \infty} S_n$. This limit only exists if $r^n \to 0$, which requires $|r|<1$. The limit is then $\frac{a(1-0)}{1-r} = \frac{a}{1-r}$.

## Common mistakes
1.  **The "a" is not always the coefficient.** For the series $\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} c \cdot r^n$, the first term is $a = c \cdot r^2$, not $c$. The formula requires the actual first term of the sum.
2.  **Using the formula when it doesn't apply.** Students calculate $\frac{a}{1-r}$ for a series where $|r| \ge 1$. This value is meaningless. You *must* check the condition $|r|<1$ first.
3.  **Misidentifying r.** For a series like $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{3^{n+1}}{5^n}$, rewrite the term as $3 \cdot \frac{3^n}{5^n} = 3 \cdot (\frac{3}{5})^n$. Here, $r = 3/5$, not 3 or 5. Be fluent with your exponent rules.
