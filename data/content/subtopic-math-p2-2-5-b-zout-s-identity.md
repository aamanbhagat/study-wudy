## What it is
Bézout's identity states that for any two integers $a$ and $b$ (not both zero), you can always multiply them by some integers $x$ and $y$ and add them together to yield their greatest common divisor (GCD). In mathematical notation, there always exist integers $x$ and $y$ such that $ax + by = \gcd(a, b)$. 

## Why it matters
This is the bedrock theorem for modular arithmetic and cryptography. In computer science, Bézout's identity guarantees the existence of modular multiplicative inverses, which are strictly required to generate and decrypt RSA public-key codes. In physics and mechanical engineering, it is used to solve linear Diophantine equations—allowing you to calculate exact integer gear ratios or map discrete lattice points in crystallography.

## When to study it
You must already possess a rock-solid understanding of:
1. Integer divisibility.
2. The definition of the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD).
3. The standard Euclidean algorithm. 

If you cannot confidently compute $\gcd(252, 105)$ using successive division steps in under two minutes, go back and master the Euclidean algorithm before proceeding.

## How to study it (step by step)
1. Write out the standard Euclidean algorithm (forward pass) for two small coprime numbers, such as 14 and 9.
2. Rewrite every step of that algorithm as an equation solving for the remainder ($r = a - qb$).
3. Starting from the last non-zero remainder (which is the GCD), substitute the previous remainders back into the equation one by one (the backward pass).
4. Simplify the expression algebraically after every single substitution. *Crucial:* Treat your original numbers $a$ and $b$ as immutable variables. Do not multiply them out.
5. Practice on a pair of non-coprime numbers (e.g., 24 and 18) to verify the process holds when the GCD is greater than 1.

## Key ideas, with intuition
**The Linear Combination:** 
An expression of the form $ax + by$ (where $x, y$ are integers) is called a linear combination of $a$ and $b$. Bézout's identity proves that the *smallest positive* linear combination of $a$ and $b$ is exactly $\gcd(a,b)$.

**The Set of All Combinations:** 
The set of all possible values generated by $ax + by$ over all integers $x,y$ is exactly the set of all multiples of $\gcd(a,b)$. You can never generate an integer smaller than the GCD (other than 0), and you can never generate a number that isn't a multiple of the GCD. If $\gcd(a,b) = d$, then $ax + by = c$ only has solutions if $d$ divides $c$.

**The Extended Euclidean Algorithm:** 
The standard Euclidean algorithm finds the GCD by repeatedly taking remainders: $a = qb + r$. By rearranging this to $r = a - qb$, you can work backwards from the GCD to express it entirely in terms of your starting numbers $a$ and $b$. This constructive proof is how we actually find $x$ and $y$.

## Worked example
**Problem:** Find integers $x$ and $y$ such that $73x + 25y = 1$. (Here, $a=73, b=25$).

**Step 1: The Forward Pass (Euclidean Algorithm)**
$$73 = 2(25) + 23$$
$$25 = 1(23) + 2$$
$$23 = 11(2) + 1$$
$$2 = 2(1) + 0 \implies \gcd(73, 25) = 1$$

**Step 2: Isolate the remainders**
$$23 = 73 - 2(25)$$
$$2 = 25 - 1(23)$$
$$1 = 23 - 11(2)$$

**Step 3: The Backward Pass (Substitution)**
Start with the equation for the GCD ($1$):
$$1 = 23 - 11(2)$$

Substitute the remainder $2$ with its isolated equation:
$$1 = 23 - 11(25 - 1(23))$$
Group the $23$s and $25$s (do not compute $11 \times 25$):
$$1 = 12(23) - 11(25)$$

Substitute the remainder $23$ with its isolated equation:
$$1 = 12(73 - 2(25)) - 11(25)$$
Group the $73$s and $25$s:
$$1 = 12(73) - 24(25) - 11(25)$$
$$1 = 12(73) - 35(25)$$

**Result:** $x = 12, y = -35$. 

*Reflection:* By rigorously isolating the remainders and substituting them backward, we systematically reduced the expression until only the original $a$ and $b$ remained. Grouping terms at each step prevents the math from exploding into unmanageable arithmetic.

## Diagrams
We can visualize Bézout's identity as taking discrete "jumps" of length $a$ and $b$ on a number line to reach the GCD. 
For $3x + 5y = 1$, we want to reach $1$ using jumps of $+5$ and $-3$.
$2(5) - 3(3) = 10 - 9 = 1$.

```text
      -3      -3      -3       +5       +5
  <-------<-------<-------|-------->-------->
  |       |       |       |        |        |
 -9      -6      -3       0        5       10
  
  Net result of two +5 jumps and three -3 jumps:
  0 ----------------------------------------> 10
  1 <---------------------------------------- 10
  
  Total displacement = 1 (which is gcd(3,5))
```

## Memory technique — remember this forever
1. **The Mnemonic:** "Bézout Backtracks." To find the coefficients $x$ and $y$, you run the Euclidean algorithm forward, then *backtrack* through the remainders.
2. **The Equation:** $ax + by = \gcd(a, b)$. Overlearn this. It is the definition of the identity.
3. **Spaced Repetition Schedule:** Review this derivation and solve one new pair of numbers at intervals of 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, and 35 days.
4. **First Principles Pathway:** If you forget how to find $x$ and $y$, remember the division algorithm: $a = qb + r$. The remainder $r$ is just a linear combination of $a$ and $b$ ($r = a - qb$). Since the Euclidean algorithm is just a chain of remainders ending in the GCD, the GCD must also be a linear combination of $a$ and $b$.

## Common mistakes
* **Losing the base variables:** Students often multiply out the terms during back-substitution (e.g., turning $11(25)$ into $275$). Treat your original $a$ and $b$, and the intermediate remainders, as algebraic variables like $X$ and $Y$. 
* **Sign errors:** Dropping a negative sign when distributing coefficients during the substitution steps. Always use parentheses when substituting: $-(a - qb) = -a + qb$.
* **Assuming $x$ and $y$ are unique:** They are not. If $(x,y)$ is a solution, there are infinitely many solutions given by $(x + \frac{b}{d}k, y - \frac{a}{d}k)$ for any integer $k$, where $d = \gcd(a,b)$.
