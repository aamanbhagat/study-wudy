## What it is
The Newton-Raphson method is an iterative algorithm for finding successively better approximations to the roots (or zeros) of a real-valued function. Starting from an initial guess, each new guess is generated by finding the x-intercept of the tangent line to the function at the current guess. It is a root-finding algorithm, but it can be repurposed for optimization by finding the roots of the derivative.

## Why it matters
This method is the foundation for many advanced numerical solvers. In machine learning, training a model often involves minimizing a loss function; Newton's method (in higher dimensions) is used to find where the gradient of the loss function is zero. In aerospace engineering, it's used to solve implicit equations in orbital mechanics and fluid dynamics, such as finding the eccentric anomaly in Kepler's equation, which has no closed-form solution.

## When to study it
Before tackling this, you must have a firm grasp of single-variable calculus. Specifically, you need to understand:
1.  **Derivatives:** What they represent (slope of the tangent line) and how to compute them for various functions.
2.  **Tangent Lines:** The equation of a line tangent to a curve $f(x)$ at a point $(x_0, f(x_0))$.
3.  **Taylor Series:** At least the first-order expansion of a function around a point. This is essential for understanding the convergence rate.

If you are not comfortable with these, review them first. The derivation will be opaque otherwise.

## How to study it (step by step)
1.  **Geometric Derivation:** Draw a simple curve $y=f(x)$ that crosses the x-axis. Pick a point $x_0$ on the x-axis. Go up to the curve at $(x_0, f(x_0))$, draw the tangent line, and see where it intersects the x-axis. Derive the formula for this intersection point, which becomes $x_1$.
2.  **Algebraic Derivation:** Write down the first-order Taylor expansion of $f(x)$ around a point $x_n$: $f(x) \approx f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x - x_n)$. Set $f(x) = 0$ (since we are looking for a root) and solve for $x$. This $x$ will be our next approximation, $x_{n+1}$.
3.  **Work a canonical example:** Use the method to approximate $\sqrt{3}$. This is equivalent to finding the positive root of $f(x) = x^2 - 3$. Start with $x_0 = 2$ and perform three iterations by hand. Observe how quickly the digits stabilize.
4.  **Derive Quadratic Convergence:** Let $r$ be the true root, so $f(r)=0$. Define the error at step $n$ as $e_n = x_n - r$. Use the second-order Taylor expansion of $f(r)$ around $x_n$ to find a relationship between $e_{n+1}$ and $e_n$. Show that $e_{n+1} \propto e_n^2$.
5.  **Explore failure modes:** Find or construct examples where the method fails: a starting point with a zero derivative, a starting point that leads to oscillations, and a starting point that diverges to infinity.

## Key ideas, with intuition
1.  **Linearization is Approximation:** The core idea is that any smooth function, when viewed up close, looks like a straight line. The Newton-Raphson method exploits this by replacing the difficult problem of "find where this curve hits the axis" with the easy problem of "find where this tangent line hits the axis," and then repeating the process.

2.  **The Iteration Formula:** The entire algorithm is captured by a single recurrence relation. The next guess, $x_{n+1}$, is found from the current guess, $x_n$.
    $$
    x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}
    $$
    Intuitively, $f(x_n)$ is the current value (height) of the function. $f'(x_n)$ is the slope. The ratio $\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$ is the horizontal distance you must travel along the tangent line to get from height $f(x_n)$ down to zero. We subtract this distance from $x_n$ to get our new x-intercept.

3.  **Quadratic Convergence:** This is the method's superpower. It means that, under good conditions, the number of correct significant figures in the approximation roughly doubles with each iteration. Formally, if $e_n$ is the error at step $n$, then the error at the next step $e_{n+1}$ is proportional to the square of the previous error:
    $$
    e_{n+1} \approx C \cdot e_n^2
    $$
    where $C = \frac{f''(r)}{2f'(r)}$ and $r$ is the true root. If your error is $10^{-2}$, the next error will be on the order of $10^{-4}$, then $10^{-8}$, and so on. This explains the method's incredible speed near a root.

## Worked example
Find the positive root of $f(x) = x^2 - 5 = 0$. Let our initial guess be $x_0 = 2$.

**Step 0: Setup**
The function is $f(x) = x^2 - 5$.
The derivative is $f'(x) = 2x$.
The iteration formula is $x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{x_n^2 - 5}{2x_n}$.

**Step 1: Iteration 1 (n=0)**
Start with $x_0 = 2$.
$$
x_1 = x_0 - \frac{f(x_0)}{f'(x_0)} = 2 - \frac{2^2 - 5}{2(2)} = 2 - \frac{-1}{4} = 2 + 0.25 = 2.25
$$
*Reflection:* Our first guess was 2. The function value was -1 (below the axis). The tangent line at $x=2$ has a positive slope, so its x-intercept must be to the right of 2. The calculation confirms this.

**Step 2: Iteration 2 (n=1)**
Use $x_1 = 2.25$.
$$
x_2 = x_1 - \frac{f(x_1)}{f'(x_1)} = 2.25 - \frac{(2.25)^2 - 5}{2(2.25)} = 2.25 - \frac{5.0625 - 5}{4.5} = 2.25 - \frac{0.0625}{4.5} \approx 2.25 - 0.01388... = 2.23611...
$$
*Reflection:* The new guess is slightly smaller than the last one. This is because $f(2.25)$ was positive (0.0625), so the tangent line needed to go left to find the axis. The correction is much smaller than the first one, indicating we are getting closer.

**Step 3: Iteration 3 (n=2)**
Use $x_2 \approx 2.23611$.
$$
x_3 = x_2 - \frac{f(x_2)}{f'(x_2)} = 2.23611 - \frac{(2.23611)^2 - 5}{2(2.23611)} = 2.23611 - \frac{5.00018... - 5}{4.47222...} \approx 2.23611 - \frac{0.00018}{4.47222} \approx 2.2360679...
$$
*Reflection:* The true value of $\sqrt{5}$ is approximately $2.236067977...$. After just three iterations, our result is correct to six decimal places. This demonstrates the rapid, quadratic convergence.

## Diagrams
Here is the geometric intuition for one step of the method. We start at $x_n$, find the tangent to the curve, and follow it down to the x-axis to find our next, better guess $x_{n+1}$.

```text
      y ^
        |
 f(x_n) +-------(x_n, f(x_n))
        |      /
        |     /  <-- tangent line
        |    /
        |   /
        |  /
--------+----------------------> x
        | /    |         |
        |/     x_n       r (true root)
       /|
 (x_{n+1}, 0)
```

## Memory technique — remember this forever
1.  **Visual Hook:** Picture yourself standing on a curvy hillside ($y=f(x)$) in the fog, trying to get to sea level ($y=0$). You can't see the whole hill, only the ground right under your feet. Your best strategy is to look at the slope where you are standing and ski straight down that slope until you hit sea level. That's your new guess. Then you repeat. **"Slide down the tangent."**

2.  **Must-Know Formulas:**
    *   The iteration: $x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$
    *   The error relationship (for quadratic convergence): $e_{n+1} \approx \frac{f''(r)}{2f'(r)} e_n^2$

3.  **Spaced Repetition Schedule:** Review this material and re-derive the main formula at: 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, 35 days.

4.  **First Principles Pathway:** If you forget the formula, re-derive it. The equation for a line passing through $(x_n, f(x_n))$ with slope $m = f'(x_n)$ is:
    $$
    y - f(x_n) = f'(x_n) (x - x_n)
    $$
    We want the x-intercept, which is the point $(x, y)$ where $y=0$. Let this be our next guess, $x = x_{n+1}$.
    $$
    0 - f(x_n) = f'(x_n) (x_{n+1} - x_n)
    $$
    Now, just solve for $x_{n+1}$.
    $$
    -\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} = x_{n+1} - x_n \implies x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}
    $$

## Common mistakes
1.  **Division by Zero:** Choosing an initial guess $x_0$ where $f'(x_0) = 0$. The tangent line is horizontal and will never intersect the x-axis. Your algorithm will crash.
2.  **Poor Initial Guess:** If $x_0$ is too far from the desired root, the tangent can shoot off to a different root or even diverge to infinity. The method is not globally convergent.
3.  **Ignoring Conditions for Convergence:** Assuming the method will *always* converge quadratically. If the root $r$ is a multiple root (i.e., $f(r)=0$ and $f'(r)=0$), convergence is only linear, not quadratic.
4.  **Sign Errors:** Forgetting the minus sign in the update rule: writing $x_{n+1} = x_n + \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$. This will almost always cause divergence.
