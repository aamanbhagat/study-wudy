## What it is
Efficiency is a dimensionless measure that quantifies how effectively a system converts an input of energy or work into a desired, useful output. It is the ratio of the useful work or energy that comes out of a process to the total work or energy put into it. Because some energy is always lost to non-useful forms like heat or sound due to friction and other dissipative forces, efficiency is always less than 100%.

## Why it matters
In aerospace, the efficiency of a rocket engine (its specific impulse) determines how much payload you can deliver to orbit for a given amount of fuel; a few percentage points can be the difference between mission success and failure. In computer science, algorithmic efficiency determines how much computational work is needed to solve a problem, directly impacting speed and energy consumption in data centers. In physics, the Carnot efficiency sets the unbreakable upper limit for any heat engine, a foundational concept in thermodynamics.

## When to study it
You must have a firm grasp of the following concepts before tackling efficiency. If you are not confident with these, review them first.
*   **Work:** The definition of work as $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}$ and its calculation.
*   **Energy:** The forms of mechanical energy, specifically Kinetic Energy ($KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$) and Gravitational Potential Energy ($PE = mgh$).
*   **Power:** The definition of power as the rate of doing work, $P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v}$.
*   **The Principle of Conservation of Energy:** Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed from one form to another.

## How to study it (step by step)
1.  **Isolate the system.** Clearly define the machine or process you are analyzing. What are its boundaries?
2.  **Identify the input.** Determine the total energy or power being supplied *to* the system. This is your denominator. Examples: electrical energy consumed by a motor, chemical energy in fuel.
3.  **Identify the useful output.** Determine the specific work, energy, or power the system is intended to produce. This is your numerator. Example: the increase in potential energy of a mass being lifted, not the heat generated by the motor.
4.  **Write the ratio.** Form the fraction $\frac{\text{Useful Output}}{\text{Total Input}}$. This is the efficiency. Ensure the units are consistent (Energy/Energy or Power/Power).
5.  **Calculate the loss.** Based on the conservation of energy, any difference between the input and the useful output must be wasted energy ($E_{in} = E_{out, useful} + E_{wasted}$). Calculate this wasted energy and consider where it went (e.g., heat, sound).
6.  **Solve problems.** Work through 5-10 problems of increasing complexity, starting with simple machines (levers, pulleys) and moving to motors and engines. For each, explicitly label $W_{in}$, $W_{out}$, and $W_{lost}$.

## Key ideas, with intuition
1.  **Energy is Conserved, but Utility is Not.** The First Law of Thermodynamics guarantees that $E_{in} = E_{out}$. However, the *total* output $E_{out}$ is composed of both useful energy and wasted energy. Efficiency focuses only on the useful part. Think of spending \$100 (input) to buy a textbook that costs \$80 (useful output). You still spent \$100, but \$20 was "wasted" on tax and shipping (non-useful output). The "book-buying efficiency" was $\frac{\$80}{\$100} = 80\%$.
    $$E_{in} = E_{out, useful} + E_{wasted}$$

2.  **Efficiency is a Ratio of "What You Get" to "What You Pay".** This is the core concept. The Greek letter eta, $\eta$, is used to denote efficiency.
    $$ \eta = \frac{W_{out, useful}}{W_{in, total}} \quad \text{or} \quad \eta = \frac{P_{out, useful}}{P_{in, total}} $$
    Since power is energy per unit time ($P = E/t$), the time component cancels, making the power ratio identical to the energy/work ratio. An efficiency of 0.75 or 75% means that for every 100 Joules of energy you put in, you only get 75 Joules of useful work out.

3.  **Losses are Inevitable.** In any real-world mechanical system, forces like friction, air resistance, and electrical resistance will convert some of the input energy into thermal energy (heat). This energy is typically not the desired output, so it is classified as "wasted". This is a direct consequence of the Second Law of Thermodynamics. Therefore, for any real process, $\eta < 1$.

## Worked example
**Problem:** A 1.5 kW motor is used to lift a 50 kg crate to a height of 12 m. It takes 5 seconds to perform this task. What is the efficiency of the motor? (Use $g \approx 9.8 \ \text{m/s}^2$).

**Solution:**

1.  **Identify the system:** The system is the motor.

2.  **Calculate the total energy input ($E_{in}$):**
    The motor has a power rating of $P_{in} = 1.5 \ \text{kW} = 1500 \ \text{W}$. It runs for $t = 5 \ \text{s}$.
    Energy is power multiplied by time.
    $$ E_{in} = P_{in} \times t = (1500 \ \text{J/s}) \times (5 \ \text{s}) = 7500 \ \text{J} $$
    This is the total electrical energy consumed by the motor.

3.  **Calculate the useful work output ($W_{out}$):**
    The motor's useful job is to lift the crate, increasing its gravitational potential energy.
    The force required to lift the crate is its weight, $F = mg$. The work done is this force times the vertical distance $h$.
    $$ W_{out} = \Delta PE = mgh = (50 \ \text{kg})(9.8 \ \text{m/s}^2)(12 \ \text{m}) = 5880 \ \text{J} $$
    This is the useful work accomplished.

4.  **Calculate the efficiency ($\eta$):**
    Efficiency is the ratio of useful output to total input.
    $$ \eta = \frac{W_{out}}{E_{in}} = \frac{5880 \ \text{J}}{7500 \ \text{J}} = 0.784 $$
    Expressed as a percentage, the efficiency is $78.4\%$.

**Reflection:**
*   Step 1 defined the boundaries.
*   Step 2 calculated "what we paid" in terms of energy. We used the fundamental relationship $E = P \times t$.
*   Step 3 calculated "what we got" in terms of useful work. We identified the useful work as the change in potential energy, $mgh$.
*   Step 4 applied the definition of efficiency, $\eta = \frac{\text{output}}{\text{input}}$, to find the final dimensionless ratio. The remaining $7500 - 5880 = 1620$ J of energy was lost, primarily as heat in the motor windings and sound.

## Diagrams
An energy flow diagram (often called a Sankey diagram) is the best way to visualize efficiency. It shows how the input energy is partitioned into useful and wasted outputs.

```text
                  +-----------------+
Total Energy In   |                 | ----> Useful Energy Out
(7500 J)   -----> |      MOTOR      |       (Work done: 5880 J)
                  |                 |
                  +-----------------+
                        |
                        |
                        v
                  Wasted Energy
                  (Heat, Sound: 1620 J)
```
This diagram makes it clear that $E_{in} = E_{out, useful} + E_{wasted}$, satisfying the conservation of energy.

## Memory technique — remember this forever
1.  **Visual Hook:** Picture a leaky bucket. The water you pour in is $E_{in}$. The water that comes out the spout to water your plant is $E_{out, useful}$. The water that leaks out the bottom onto the floor is $E_{wasted}$. Efficiency is the ratio of water on the plant to the total water you poured in. You can never water the plant with more water than you put in the bucket.

2.  **Must-Know Formulas:** Overlearn these until they are automatic.
    $$ \eta = \frac{W_{out}}{W_{in}} $$
    $$ \eta = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}} $$
    $$ E_{in} = E_{out, useful} + E_{wasted} $$

3.  **Spaced Repetition Schedule:** Review this concept and re-derive the formulas at these intervals:
    *   24 hours
    *   3 days
    *   7 days
    *   16 days
    *   35 days

4.  **First Principles Pathway:** If you forget everything, rebuild it from the **Principle of Conservation of Energy**.
    *   Start with the fact that energy is never destroyed: $Energy_{in} = Energy_{out, total}$.
    *   Recognize that the total output consists of two parts: what you wanted (useful) and what you didn't (wasted). So, $Energy_{in} = Energy_{out, useful} + Energy_{wasted}$.
    *   Define "efficiency" logically as the fraction of the input that became the useful output.
    *   This immediately gives you the formula: $\eta = \frac{Energy_{out, useful}}{Energy_{in}}$.

## Common mistakes
1.  **Inverting the ratio.** Calculating $\eta = \frac{W_{in}}{W_{out}}$. This will give a value greater than 1, which is physically impossible. Efficiency can never exceed 100%. If you get an answer like 1.2, you have inverted the fraction.
2.  **Confusing Power and Energy.** Do not mix units. The ratio must be Joules/Joules or Watts/Watts. Calculating $\eta = \frac{P_{out}}{E_{in}}$ is dimensionally incorrect.
3.  **Ignoring non-obvious inputs.** For a person climbing stairs, the "useful output" is the gain in potential energy. The "input" is not the work done by their legs, but the total chemical energy their body metabolizes, which is a much larger number. Always find the *total* energy consumed.
