## What it is
Roots of unity are the complex solutions to the equation $z^n = 1$, where $n$ is a positive integer. Geometrically, these roots represent the vertices of a regular $n$-sided polygon inscribed within the unit circle on the complex plane, with one vertex anchored at the coordinate $(1, 0)$.

## Why it matters
Roots of unity form the mathematical foundation of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). The FFT is arguably the most important algorithm in applied mathematics, used to process signals in aerospace telemetry, analyze acoustic waves in physics, and extract features for machine learning audio models. Furthermore, they are the key to solving higher-order polynomial equations and understanding quantum phase factors.

## When to study it
Do not attempt this until you have mastered:
1. Basic complex arithmetic and the complex plane (Argand diagram).
2. Polar and exponential forms of complex numbers.
3. Euler’s formula: $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$.
4. De Moivre’s Theorem.

If you cannot instantly convert $z = -1 + i$ into polar form, stop here and review prerequisites. 

## How to study it (step by step)
1. **Understand the periodicity of 1:** Write $1$ in its general exponential form: $1 = e^{i(2\pi k)}$, where $k$ is any integer. This is the engine of the entire topic.
2. **Derive the general formula:** Take the $n$-th root of both sides of $z^n = e^{i(2\pi k)}$ to find the general expression for $z$. 
3. **Generate the cube roots:** Set $n=3$ and evaluate your formula for $k=0, 1, 2$. Convert these back to Cartesian coordinates ($a+bi$) using Euler's formula.
4. **Plot the geometry:** Draw a unit circle and plot your three cube roots. Observe the equilateral triangle they form.
5. **Prove the zero-sum property:** Add the three cube roots together algebraically. Then, prove that the sum of the $n$-th roots of unity is always zero using the formula for a finite geometric series.

## Key ideas, with intuition

**1. The Periodicity Trick**
In real numbers, $1$ is just $1$. In complex numbers, $1$ is a point on the unit circle that you can reach by rotating by $0$, $2\pi$, $4\pi$, or any multiple of $2\pi$. Therefore, $1$ has infinitely many aliases:
$$1 = e^{i 2\pi k} \quad \text{for } k \in \mathbb{Z}$$

**2. The $n$-th Roots Formula**
To solve $z^n = 1$, substitute the periodic form of $1$:
$$z^n = e^{i 2\pi k}$$
Take the $n$-th root (raise to the power of $1/n$):
$$z_k = e^{i \frac{2\pi k}{n}}$$
By plugging in $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n-1$, you generate exactly $n$ unique roots. If you try $k=n$, you get $e^{i 2\pi} = 1$, which just repeats the $k=0$ root.

**3. Geometric Symmetry**
Multiplying by $e^{i \theta}$ rotates a complex number by $\theta$ radians. The roots of unity are generated by repeatedly rotating the number $1$ by an angle of $\frac{2\pi}{n}$. This perfectly slices the unit circle into $n$ equal wedges.

**4. Center of Mass**
Because the roots form a perfectly symmetric regular polygon around the origin, their vector sum (center of mass) is exactly zero. 
$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} z_k = 0$$

## Worked example
**Problem:** Find the cube roots of unity ($z^3 = 1$), express them in Cartesian form, and show their sum is zero.

**Step 1: Set up the general equation.**
$$z^3 = e^{i 2\pi k}$$
$$z_k = e^{i \frac{2\pi k}{3}}$$

**Step 2: Evaluate for $k = 0, 1, 2$.**
For $k=0$:
$$z_0 = e^0 = 1$$

For $k=1$ (often denoted as $\omega$):
$$z_1 = e^{i \frac{2\pi}{3}} = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac{1}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$

For $k=2$ (often denoted as $\omega^2$):
$$z_2 = e^{i \frac{4\pi}{3}} = \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac{1}{2} - i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$

**Step 3: Sum the roots.**
$$z_0 + z_1 + z_2 = 1 + \left(-\frac{1}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) + \left(-\frac{1}{2} - i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$$
$$= 1 - \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} + i\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = 0$$

*Reflection:* The algebraic cancellation of the imaginary parts and the real parts perfectly mirrors the geometric reality that an equilateral triangle centered at the origin has a center of mass at $(0,0)$.

## Memory technique — remember this forever
1. **The Visual Hook:** Imagine slicing a pizza. To find the $n$-th roots of unity, you make your first cut at 3 o'clock (the number $1$). Then, you slice the pizza into $n$ perfectly equal slices. The crust edge of every slice is a root.
2. **Must Overlearn:**
   * $z_k = e^{i \frac{2\pi k}{n}}$ for $k = 0, \dots, n-1$.
   * $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} z_k = 0$ (for $n > 1$).
3. **Spaced-repetition schedule:** Review this derivation at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, and 35 days.
4. **First principles pathway:** If you forget the formula, write $1 = e^{i 2\pi k}$. Then write $z^n = e^{i 2\pi k}$. Isolate $z$ by taking the $1/n$ power. The formula builds itself.

## Common mistakes
1. **Forgetting to bound $k$:** Students often keep plugging in $k = n, n+1, \dots$ and think they are finding new roots. The roots repeat after $n-1$. There are exactly $n$ roots for an $n$-th degree polynomial.
2. **Ignoring the magnitude for $z^n = w$:** When solving $z^3 = 8$, students correctly find the angles but forget to take the real cube root of the magnitude. The solutions are $2z_k$, not just $z_k$.
3. **Confusing $\omega$ notation:** $\omega$ is conventionally used for the first complex cube root $e^{i 2\pi/3}$. Be aware that $\omega^3 = 1$ and $1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0$. This is a specific case, not a general variable.
