## What it is
Chomsky Normal Form (CNF) is a standardized format for a Context-Free Grammar (CFG). In CNF, every production rule must take one of two simple forms: a non-terminal yielding two other non-terminals ($A \to BC$), or a non-terminal yielding a single terminal ($A \to a$). The only exception is for the start symbol, which can yield the empty string ($\epsilon$) if the language contains it.

## Why it matters
CNF is not just a theoretical curiosity; it is the required input format for powerful parsing algorithms, most notably the CYK (Cocke-Younger-Kasami) algorithm. CYK is a cornerstone of computational linguistics and compiler design, used to determine if a string can be generated by a given grammar. In machine learning, probabilistic versions of CNF grammars (PCFGs) are used in natural language processing to find the most likely parse tree for a sentence, a critical step in understanding meaning.

## When to study it
You must have a solid grasp of Context-Free Grammars (CFGs). This includes understanding the definitions of terminals, non-terminals (variables), production rules, the start symbol, and derivations. If you cannot confidently write a simple CFG for a language like $\{a^n b^n \mid n \ge 0\}$, you should review that topic first.

## How to study it (step by step)
1.  **Memorize the Goal:** Write down the two target rule forms, $A \to BC$ and $A \to a$, on a notecard. Look at it until it's second nature. This is the destination for every rule.
2.  **Master Pre-processing (Part 1):** Take a grammar with $\epsilon$-productions (e.g., $A \to \epsilon$) and practice the elimination procedure. For every rule $X \to YAZ$, where $A$ is nullable ($A \Rightarrow^* \epsilon$), you must add a new rule $X \to YZ$. Do this on paper for 3-5 small grammars.
3.  **Master Pre-processing (Part 2):** Take a grammar with unit productions ($A \to B$) and practice their elimination. For each rule $A \to B$, replace it by adding rules $A \to \alpha$ for every non-unit production $B \to \alpha$. Repeat until no unit productions remain.
4.  **Isolate Terminals (TERM):** Practice the terminal-isolation step. For any rule with a terminal on the right-hand side that isn't by itself (e.g., $A \to aB$), create a new non-terminal $T_a$ and two new rules: $T_a \to a$ and $A \to T_a B$.
5.  **Binarize Rules (BIN):** Practice breaking long rules. For a rule $A \to BCD$, introduce a new non-terminal $X_1$ and rewrite the rule as two: $A \to BX_1$ and $X_1 \to CD$. Internalize this "chaining" process.
6.  **Full Conversion:** Combine all steps. Take a complex grammar and convert it fully, writing down the state of the grammar after each of the major phases (START, ELIM-$\epsilon$, ELIM-UNIT, TERM, BIN). This will solidify the full algorithm.

## Key ideas, with intuition
1.  **Binary Trees:** The form $A \to BC$ forces any parse tree to be a binary tree. Each non-terminal node has exactly two non-terminal children. This predictable structure is what makes algorithms like CYK efficient; they can build the parse tree from the bottom up, combining pairs of sub-problems at each step.
    
2.  **Terminals as Leaves:** The form $A \to a$ ensures that terminals (the actual characters in your string) only ever appear as the leaves of the parse tree. This cleanly separates the grammar's internal structure (non-terminals) from the final output (terminals). The parser knows it has reached the end of a branch when it sees a terminal.

3.  **Language Preservation via Substitution:** The conversion process looks complex, but it's just a series of substitutions that don't change the language the grammar generates. When we change $A \to aB$ to $A \to T_a B$ and $T_a \to a$, we've just given the terminal $a$ a non-terminal "name" ($T_a$). The derivation path is slightly longer, but it still produces the exact same strings. Similarly, breaking $A \to BCD$ into $A \to BX_1$ and $X_1 \to CD$ is like factoring in algebra; we've regrouped the sequence without changing the final result.

## Worked example
Let's convert the following grammar $G$ to CNF.
$S \to aSb \mid \epsilon$

This grammar generates the language $\{a^n b^n \mid n \ge 0\}$.

**Step 1: Eliminate $\epsilon$-productions.**
The rule $S \to \epsilon$ is an $\epsilon$-production. We must remove it.
Since $S$ is nullable, for any rule with $S$ on the RHS, we must add a version of that rule where $S$ is replaced by $\epsilon$.
The rule is $S \to aSb$. Replacing $S$ with $\epsilon$ gives us a new rule: $S \to ab$.
Our grammar is now:
$S \to aSb \mid ab$

*Reflection:* We removed the ability to generate $\epsilon$ directly but added a rule ($S \to ab$) that represents the base case of the recursion, which was previously handled by the $\epsilon$-production. Note: because the original language contains $\epsilon$, we need a special fix. The standard algorithm adds a new start symbol $S_0$ and rules $S_0 \to S \mid \epsilon$, then proceeds to convert the grammar for $S$. For this example, let's assume the language is $\{a^n b^n \mid n \ge 1\}$ to keep it simple, which corresponds to the grammar we have now.

**Step 2: Eliminate unit productions.**
There are no unit productions (rules of the form $A \to B$), so we can skip this step.

**Step 3: Isolate terminals (TERM).**
The rules $S \to aSb$ and $S \to ab$ mix terminals ($a, b$) with non-terminals ($S$) or other terminals. We must isolate them.
Introduce new non-terminals $T_a$ and $T_b$ with the following rules:
$T_a \to a$
$T_b \to b$
Now, substitute these back into the $S$ rules:
$S \to T_a S T_b$
$S \to T_a T_b$

Our grammar is now:
$S \to T_a S T_b \mid T_a T_b$
$T_a \to a$
$T_b \to b$

*Reflection:* The new rules are all in valid CNF form except one. We have successfully separated terminals to their own productions.

**Step 4: Binarize long rules (BIN).**
The rule $S \to T_a S T_b$ has three non-terminals on the RHS. We must break it down.
Introduce a new non-terminal, let's call it $X_1$.
Replace $S \to T_a S T_b$ with two new rules:
$S \to T_a X_1$
$X_1 \to S T_b$

*Reflection:* We've replaced one rule of length 3 with two rules of length 2. This preserves the sequence $T_a S T_b$ while conforming to the CNF constraint.

**Final CNF Grammar:**
The complete grammar in Chomsky Normal Form is:
$S \to T_a X_1 \mid T_a T_b$
$X_1 \to S T_b$
$T_a \to a$
$T_b \to b$

This grammar generates the exact same language as the original (excluding $\epsilon$), but every rule is now in one of the two permitted forms.

## Diagrams

The conversion simplifies the structure of parse trees.

**Parse tree for `aabb` BEFORE CNF conversion:**

```text
      S
     /|\
    / | \
   a  S  b
     /|\
    / | \
   a  S  b
      |
      ε
```

**Parse tree for `aabb` AFTER CNF conversion:**

```text
          S
         / \
        /   \
       Ta    X1
       |    /  \
       a   S    Tb
          / \   |
         /   \  b
        Ta    Tb
        |     |
        a     b
```

Notice the second tree is a strict binary tree, which is far easier for an algorithm to process systematically.

## Memory technique — remember this forever
1.  **Mnemonic:** "**S**ome **E**ager **U**ndergrads **T**ry **B**inarization."
    - **S**TART: Create a new start symbol $S_0 \to S$ (if needed).
    - **E**PSILON: Eliminate $\epsilon$-productions.
    - **U**NIT: Eliminate unit productions.
    - **T**ERM: Isolate terminals with new variables ($T_a \to a$).
    - **B**IN: Binarize long rules ($A \to BCD \implies A \to BX_1, X_1 \to CD$).

2.  **Must-overlearn formulas:**
    $$ A \to BC $$
    $$ A \to a $$
    (Where $A, B, C$ are any non-terminals, and $a$ is any terminal).

3.  **Spaced-repetition schedule:** Review this entire mini-lesson and work one new conversion problem on:
    - Day 1
    - Day 3
    - Day 7
    - Day 16
    - Day 35

4.  **First principles pathway:** If you forget the steps, remember the goal: make every rule look like $A \to BC$ or $A \to a$. Look at a rule that violates this. If it's too long ($A \to XYZ$), you can always group it: $A \to X(YZ)$. Give the $(YZ)$ part a new name, say $W_1$. Now you have $A \to XW_1$ and $W_1 \to YZ$. You've made progress. If it mixes terminals and non-terminals ($A \to aB$), you can't have the $a$ there. Give it a name: $T_a \to a$. Now substitute the name back in: $A \to T_a B$. You can always reconstruct the process by fixing one violation at a time.

## Common mistakes
1.  **Incorrect $\epsilon$-elimination:** When removing a nullable variable $A$ from a rule like $X \to YAZAW$, you must add rules for *all combinations* of $A$ being present or absent. This would mean adding $X \to YZAW$, $X \to YAZW$, and $X \to YZW$. Students often only remove one $A$ at a time.
2.  **Stopping unit-production chains too early:** If you have $A \to B$, $B \to C$, and $C \to xyz$, you must replace $A \to B$ with $A \to xyz$. Simply replacing $A \to B$ with $A \to C$ is insufficient, as $A \to C$ is still a unit production that must be eliminated.
3.  **Forgetting the start symbol exception:** If the language contains $\epsilon$, the *only* way to generate it is with a rule $S_0 \to \epsilon$, where $S_0$ is a new start symbol that does not appear on the right-hand side of any rule. Many students forget this and improperly eliminate $S \to \epsilon$ from the original start symbol, removing $\epsilon$ from the language.
4.  **Applying steps out of order:** You must eliminate $\epsilon$ and unit productions *before* binarizing and isolating terminals. Doing it in the wrong order can create new unit or $\epsilon$ productions that you then fail to clean up.
