## What it is
We are calculating the magnetic field ($\vec{B}$) generated by a steady electric current ($I$) flowing through four canonical geometries: an infinitely long straight wire, a single circular loop, a solenoid (a coil of wire), and a toroid (a doughnut-shaped coil). This involves applying fundamental laws of magnetostatics to derive formulas that relate the field's strength and direction to the current and the shape's dimensions.

## Why it matters
These specific geometries are the building blocks of countless electromagnetic devices. Solenoids are the basis for electromagnets, which are used in everything from relays and fuel injectors to MRI machines and particle accelerators. Toroids are essential for creating transformers and inductors that require highly contained magnetic fields, minimizing interference in dense electronics. Understanding these foundational cases is the first step toward designing any system that uses or is affected by magnetic fields.

## When to study it
Before tackling these derivations, you must have a firm grasp of the following prerequisites:
1.  **Vector Calculus:** Specifically, you need to be comfortable with vector cross products ($\vec{A} \times \vec{B}$) and evaluating line integrals ($\int \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{l}$).
2.  **Electric Current:** Understand that current ($I$) is the flow of charge, $I = dQ/dt$, and its direction is defined by the flow of positive charge.
3.  **The Biot-Savart Law:** This law is the fundamental starting point for calculating the magnetic field from a current distribution. It states that a small segment of current-carrying wire $d\vec{l}$ produces a magnetic field element $d\vec{B}$:
    $$d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi} \frac{d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2}$$
4.  **Ampere's Law:** This law relates the line integral of the magnetic field around a closed loop to the total current passing through that loop. For high-symmetry situations, it simplifies calculations immensely.
    $$\oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{enc}$$

If these laws are unfamiliar, pause and study them first. All derivations that follow depend on them.

## How to study it (step by step)
1.  **Master the Right-Hand Rule.** Before any math, get a physical intuition for the field's direction. For a wire, point your thumb along the current; your fingers curl in the direction of the $\vec{B}$-field lines. For a loop or coil, curl your fingers in the direction of the current; your thumb points in the direction of the $\vec{B}$-field inside the coil.
2.  **Derive the field of an infinite wire.** Start with the Biot-Savart law. Set up the integral for a wire of finite length, then take the limit as the length goes to infinity. This is a classic, essential vector calculus exercise.
3.  **Derive the field at the center of a circular loop.** Again, use the Biot-Savart law. Notice how the symmetry makes all components of $d\vec{B}$ cancel out, except for the one along the axis of the loop.
4.  **Master Ampere's Law through the solenoid.** Understand why Ampere's law is superior for the solenoid. Define a rectangular Amperian loop that passes through the solenoid's interior and exterior. Argue from symmetry why the field outside is zero and the field inside is uniform.
5.  **Apply Ampere's Law to the toroid.** This follows directly from the solenoid. Use a circular Amperian loop inside the toroid. See that the field is confined entirely within the toroidal core.
6.  **Solve comparison problems.** For example: "A wire is bent into a circle. Compare the field at the center to the field of a long straight section of the same wire at a distance equal to the radius." This forces you to use and compare the formulas.

## Key ideas, with intuition
1.  **Currents are the source of (static) magnetic fields.** A charge at rest creates an electric field. A charge in motion creates both an electric and a magnetic field. In these problems, we consider a steady, continuous flow of charge (a current) as the source.

2.  **The Biot-Savart Law is universal but computationally intensive.** It's the magnetic equivalent of Coulomb's Law. It tells you to sum up the contributions ($d\vec{B}$) from every tiny piece of wire ($d\vec{l}$). The cross product $d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}$ is crucial: it ensures the resulting field is perpendicular to both the current direction and the line connecting the wire segment to the point of interest. This is why B-fields curl around wires.
    $$d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi} \frac{d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2}$$

3.  **Ampere's Law is a shortcut for high-symmetry cases.** It's the magnetic equivalent of Gauss's Law. Instead of integrating over the source (the current), you integrate the field around a closed "Amperian" loop. If you can choose a loop where $\vec{B}$ is constant and parallel to the path, the integral becomes trivial ($B \cdot L$), making it easy to solve for $B$. This works for infinite wires, infinite solenoids, and toroids because of their translational or rotational symmetry.
    $$\oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = B \oint dl = B \cdot (\text{length of loop}) = \mu_0 I_{enc}$$

4.  **Solenoids and Toroids are "field concentrators".** The geometry of a coil is designed to make the magnetic field contributions from each loop add up constructively inside the coil and cancel out destructively outside. This creates a strong, uniform field inside and a nearly zero field outside, which is extremely useful for building electromagnetic components.

## Worked example
**Problem:** Find the magnetic field $\vec{B}$ at the center of a square loop of wire with side length $L$ carrying a steady current $I$.

**Solution:**
1.  **Decomposition:** We can treat the square loop as four finite straight wire segments. The total field at the center will be the vector sum of the fields from each of the four sides. By symmetry, each side contributes equally to the magnitude of the field, and the Right-Hand Rule shows that each side's contribution points out of the page at the center. So, we can find the field from one side and multiply by 4.
    $\vec{B}_{total} = 4 \cdot \vec{B}_{one\_side}$.

2.  **Setup for one side:** Let's find the field from the bottom segment. Place the center of the square at the origin $(0,0)$. The bottom wire runs from $x = -L/2$ to $x = L/2$ at $y = -L/2$. The point of interest is the origin. The perpendicular distance from the wire to the origin is $r = L/2$.

3.  **Apply Biot-Savart Law:** The formula for the magnetic field from a finite straight wire at a perpendicular distance $r$ is:
    $$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi r} (\sin\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1)$$
    where $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ are the angles to the ends of the wire segment from the point of interest.

4.  **Determine the angles:** For the bottom segment, looking from the origin, the ends are at $(-L/2, -L/2)$ and $(L/2, -L/2)$. The angle $\theta$ is measured from the perpendicular line. Let's define the angle from the positive x-axis. The geometry forms two right triangles. The angle to the right end is $45^\circ$ or $\pi/4$ radians. The angle to the left end is $135^\circ$ or $3\pi/4$ radians. However, the standard formula often defines angles from the line of the wire itself. Let's use a more direct formulation where the angles are measured from the perpendicular bisector. In this case, $\theta_1 = -45^\circ$ and $\theta_2 = 45^\circ$.
    $$B_{one\_side} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi (L/2)} (\sin(45^\circ) - \sin(-45^\circ))$$

5.  **Calculate:**
    $$B_{one\_side} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi L} (\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - (-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}))$$
    $$B_{one\_side} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi L} (\sqrt{2}) = \frac{\sqrt{2}\mu_0 I}{2\pi L}$$

6.  **Sum the contributions:** Multiply by 4 for the four sides.
    $$B_{total} = 4 \cdot B_{one\_side} = 4 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}\mu_0 I}{2\pi L} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}\mu_0 I}{\pi L}$$
    The direction, by the Right-Hand Rule, is perpendicular to the plane of the loop.

**Reflection:** This worked because we broke a complex shape into simpler, known components. We identified the symmetry of the situation to realize we only needed to solve for one side and multiply. The core of the calculation relied on the standard result for a finite wire, which itself comes directly from a Biot-Savart integration.

## Memory technique — remember this forever
1.  **The Story: "Gripping the Source"**
    The Right-Hand Rule is everything. Don't just wave your hand; physically imagine gripping the object creating the field.
    *   **For a Wire:** It's long and thin. Grip the wire with your right hand, thumb pointing in the direction of the current $I$. Your fingers naturally *curl* around the wire in the exact direction of the magnetic field lines.
    *   **For a Solenoid/Loop:** You can't grip the whole thing. Instead, wrap your fingers around the coil, following the direction the current is flowing. Your thumb now points straight through the center, showing the direction of the strong magnetic field *inside*.

2.  **Must-Know Formulas (Overlearn these exactly):**
    *   Infinite Straight Wire: $$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$$ (Field gets weaker as $1/r$)
    *   Center of a Circular Loop: $$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R}$$ (Note: No $\pi$. Field at the center depends only on radius $R$)
    *   Ideal Solenoid (inside): $$B = \mu_0 n I$$ (Field is uniform! $n$ is turns/length)

3.  **Spaced Repetition Schedule:**
    Drill these formulas and the "Gripping the Source" rule at these intervals:
    *   1 day from now.
    *   3 days from now.
    *   7 days from now.
    *   16 days from now.
    *   35 days from now.

4.  **First Principles Pathway:**
    If you forget a formula, you can rebuild it.
    *   **Forgot the wire or loop formula?** Start with the **Biot-Savart Law**: $d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi} \frac{d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2}$. Define your geometry, set up the integral, and solve. It's slower, but it always works.
    *   **Forgot the solenoid or toroid formula?** Start with **Ampere's Law**: $\oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{enc}$. Choose a clever Amperian loop that exploits the symmetry, argue why the integral simplifies, and solve for $B$.

## Common mistakes
1.  **Confusing $n$ and $N$.** In the solenoid formula $B = \mu_0 n I$, $n$ is the number of turns *per unit length* ($N/L$). A common mistake is to plug in the total number of turns $N$.
2.  **Radius vs. Distance.** In the loop formula ($B = \mu_0 I / 2R$), $R$ is the radius of the loop itself. In the wire formula ($B = \mu_0 I / 2\pi r$), $r$ is the perpendicular distance from the wire to the point where you are measuring the field. Do not mix them up.
3.  **Applying Ampere's Law Incorrectly.** Ampere's Law is not a universal tool. It only simplifies the problem for cases with high symmetry (infinite lines, infinite solenoids, toroids). Trying to use it to find the field of a finite wire or a square loop will lead to an integral that is impossible to solve simply.
4.  **Right-Hand Rule Errors.** Forgetting to use it, or using the left hand. Always double-check the direction of the field. In vector problems, this is a frequent source of sign errors.

2.  A single circular loop of wire with a radius of $2 \text{ cm}$ produces a magnetic field of magnitude $B_0$ at its center. If you unwind this wire and use it as a long, straight wire, at what distance from this straight wire would you measure the same magnetic field magnitude $B_0$?

You then take the same wire and wrap it into a toroid with a mean radius $R$, keeping the same total number of turns $N$. Find the ratio of the magnetic field inside the solenoid ($B_{solenoid}$) to the magnetic field at the mean radius of the toroid ($B_{toroid}$), assuming the current $I$ is the same. Your answer should be in terms of $L$ and $R$.
