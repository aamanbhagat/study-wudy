## What it is
Spacecraft thermal control is the engineering discipline of regulating a vehicle's temperature within strict operational limits despite the extreme environment of space. It utilizes passive components—like Multi-Layer Insulation (MLI) to block radiation, heat pipes to transport thermal energy, and radiators to reject it into the void—alongside active components like resistive heaters to warm specific zones during cold phases.

## Why it matters
In the vacuum of space, there is no air to carry away heat via convection. A spacecraft in direct sunlight can be scorched at $150^\circ\text{C}$, while its shadowed side plunges to $-150^\circ\text{C}$. Without a thermal control system (TCS), batteries freeze, electronics fry, and optical sensors warp out of alignment. Mastering TCS is mandatory for aerospace engineering because thermal limits dictate the spacecraft's power budget, orbital trajectory, and physical geometry. 

## When to study it
You must already understand:
1. **Thermodynamics:** The First Law (conservation of energy).
2. **Heat Transfer:** Conduction (Fourier's Law) and, crucially, Radiation (Stefan-Boltzmann Law). 
3. **Basic Orbital Mechanics:** Eclipses and solar flux.

If you do not know the difference between heat conduction and thermal radiation, or if you cannot write down the Stefan-Boltzmann equation from memory, go back and review basic heat transfer.

## How to study it (step by step)
1. **Master the Space Thermal Balance:** Write out the steady-state thermal equilibrium equation for a spacecraft in orbit: Heat In (Solar + Earth Albedo + Earth IR) + Internal Heat = Heat Out (Radiated).
2. **Understand $\alpha$ vs. $\epsilon$:** Differentiate between solar absorptivity ($\alpha$, governs how much visible light from the sun is absorbed) and infrared emissivity ($\epsilon$, governs how much heat is radiated away).
3. **Analyze MLI:** Study how stacking $N$ highly reflective layers separated by low-conductivity netting chokes radiation. Understand the concept of "effective emissivity" ($\epsilon^*$).
4. **Trace the Heat Pipe Cycle:** Draw the thermodynamic cycle of a heat pipe's working fluid: evaporation at the heat source, vapor transport down the pressure gradient, condensation at the heat sink, and liquid return via capillary action in the wick.
5. **Size a Radiator:** Use the Stefan-Boltzmann law to calculate the required surface area to reject a specific internal heat load to deep space.
6. **Calculate Heater Power:** Determine the electrical power required during an orbital eclipse to maintain a battery's minimum survival temperature.

## Key ideas, with intuition

**1. The Vacuum Dictates the Rules**
Because space is a vacuum, convective heat transfer is exactly zero. Heat can only move *through* the spacecraft via conduction, and it can only enter or leave the spacecraft via radiation. 

**2. Multi-Layer Insulation (MLI): The Space Thermos**
Radiation heat transfer between two parallel plates is proportional to the temperature difference to the fourth power. If you place a highly reflective radiation shield between them, you cut the heat transfer in half. MLI uses dozens of layers of aluminized Kapton or Mylar. The heat transfer through $N$ layers scales roughly as $\frac{1}{N+1}$. MLI does not keep a spacecraft warm; it severely restricts the *rate* at which heat enters or leaves. We model this using an effective emissivity, $\epsilon^*$, which is typically around $0.01$ to $0.05$.

**3. Heat Pipes: Passive Superconductors**
Solid metal conductors (like copper straps) are heavy. A heat pipe is a sealed tube containing a working fluid (like ammonia) and a porous wick. Heat enters one end, vaporizing the liquid. The vapor travels to the cold end and condenses, dumping its latent heat of vaporization. The wick pulls the liquid back to the hot end via capillary action. Because it utilizes phase change, a heat pipe can move massive amounts of heat with a near-zero temperature gradient and zero moving parts.

**4. Radiators: The Ultimate Heat Dump**
All heat generated by spacecraft electronics must eventually be dumped into the $3\text{ K}$ heat sink of deep space. Radiators are highly emissive ($\epsilon \approx 1$) plates pointing away from the sun. The power rejected is governed by the Stefan-Boltzmann law:
$$ P_{\text{rad}} = \epsilon \sigma A (T^4 - T_{\text{sink}}^4) $$
Because $T_{\text{sink}} \approx 3\text{ K}$, we usually approximate this as $P_{\text{rad}} \approx \epsilon \sigma A T^4$.

## Worked example
**Problem:** A satellite generates $500\text{ W}$ of internal electrical power. It is entirely covered in MLI ($\epsilon^* \approx 0$), except for a single flat radiator plate facing deep space. The radiator has an emissivity of $\epsilon = 0.8$. The maximum allowable temperature of the electronics is $300\text{ K}$. Assuming deep space is $0\text{ K}$ and no sunlight hits the radiator, what is the minimum required radiator area?

**Step 1: Set up the energy balance.**
In steady state, energy in equals energy out. 
$$ P_{\text{in}} + P_{\text{internal}} = P_{\text{out}} $$
Since the MLI blocks external heat and sunlight doesn't hit the radiator, $P_{\text{in}} = 0$.
$$ P_{\text{internal}} = P_{\text{out}} $$

**Step 2: Apply the radiation law.**
The heat out is purely radiation from the radiator plate.
$$ P_{\text{internal}} = \epsilon \sigma A T^4 $$

**Step 3: Isolate the target variable ($A$).**
$$ A = \frac{P_{\text{internal}}}{\epsilon \sigma T^4} $$

**Step 4: Substitute known values.**
*   $P_{\text{internal}} = 500\text{ W}$
*   $\epsilon = 0.8$
*   $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\text{ W}/(\text{m}^2\text{K}^4)$ (Stefan-Boltzmann constant)
*   $T = 300\text{ K}$

$$ A = \frac{500}{0.8 \times (5.67 \times 10^{-8}) \times (300)^4} $$

**Step 5: Calculate.**
First, calculate $T^4$: $300^4 = 81 \times 10^8$.
$$ A = \frac{500}{0.8 \times 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 81 \times 10^8} $$
The $10^{-8}$ and $10^8$ cancel out nicely.
$$ A = \frac{500}{0.8 \times 5.67 \times 81} = \frac{500}{367.4} \approx 1.36\text{ m}^2 $$

*Reflection:* Notice the $T^4$ dependence in the denominator. If the electronics required a cooler temperature of $250\text{ K}$, the required area would more than double to $2.82\text{ m}^2$. Lower temperatures are extremely "expensive" in terms of radiator mass and area.

## Memory technique — remember this forever
**1. The Hook:** "Block, Move, Dump, Warm."
*   **Block:** MLI (stops radiation).
*   **Move:** Heat Pipes (transports heat via phase change).
*   **Dump:** Radiators (rejects heat via $T^4$).
*   **Warm:** Heaters (adds $I^2R$ heat when in eclipse).

**2. Must-Know Formulas:**
*   Stefan-Boltzmann: $Q = \epsilon \sigma A T^4$
*   Energy Balance: $\dot{E}_{\text{in}} + \dot{E}_{\text{internal}} = \dot{E}_{\text{out}}$

**4. First Principles Pathway:**
If you forget how to size a radiator, fall back to the First Law of Thermodynamics: $\Delta E = Q - W$. In steady state, $\Delta E = 0$. No work is being done on the environment, so $W = 0$. Therefore, $Q_{\text{in}} = Q_{\text{out}}$. Since space is a vacuum, $Q_{\text{out}}$ must be radiation. Write $Q_{\text{out}} = \epsilon \sigma A T^4$ and solve for what you need.

## Common mistakes
*   **Linearizing radiation:** Students often intuitively treat radiation like conduction ($\Delta T$). It is not. It is $T_{\text{hot}}^4 - T_{\text{cold}}^4$. A small change in absolute temperature drastically changes the heat rejected.
*   **Confusing $\alpha$ and $\epsilon$:** $\alpha$ (absorptivity) dictates how much energy you take in from the *sun* (visible spectrum, $\approx 5800\text{ K}$). $\epsilon$ (emissivity) dictates how much energy you radiate away (infrared spectrum, $\approx 300\text{ K}$). White paint has low $\alpha$ and high $\epsilon$, making it perfect for radiators.
*   **Assuming internal convection:** Unless dealing with a pressurized crew cabin, the inside of a satellite is a vacuum. Fans do not work. You must use conduction paths (metal straps, heat pipes) to get heat from an internal PCB to the external radiator.
