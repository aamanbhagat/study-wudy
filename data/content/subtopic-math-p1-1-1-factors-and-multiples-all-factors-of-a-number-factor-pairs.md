## What it is
Factors are the integer building blocks that multiply together to create a target number, leaving no remainder when divided. Multiples are the infinitely expanding sequence of numbers generated by multiplying a target number by any integer. A factor pair consists of two specific integers that multiply together to yield your original number.

## Why it matters
Understanding factors and multiples is the bedrock of fraction arithmetic, modular arithmetic, and prime factorization. In computer science, factoring algorithms form the basis of RSA encryption and public-key cryptography. In physics and aerospace engineering, multiples dictate harmonic frequencies in vibrating structures (like rocket airframes), and factors are used to calculate gear ratios and mesh frequencies in mechanical transmissions. 

## When to study it
You must have a rock-solid grasp of:
* Basic whole-number multiplication and division.
* The concept of a remainder (the division algorithm).
If you cannot reliably divide a two-digit number by a single-digit number in your head, stop and drill basic division first.

## How to study it (step by step)
1. **Define the relationship:** Write down the equation $a \times b = n$. Verbally confirm: "$a$ and $b$ are factors of $n$. $n$ is a multiple of $a$ and $b$."
2. **Find factor pairs systematically:** Pick a number $n$. Start testing integers $i = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ to see if $n / i$ yields a whole number. If it does, $(i, n/i)$ is a factor pair.
3. **Apply the square root limit:** Stop testing integers when $i > \sqrt{n}$. You have now found all factor pairs. 
4. **List factors in order:** Take your pairs and write them in a single, ascending list. 
5. **Generate multiples:** Pick a number $x$. Multiply it by $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ to generate its first five multiples. Notice how this list can continue infinitely.

## Key ideas, with intuition

**1. The Divisibility Rule (Zero Remainder)**
An integer $a$ is a factor of $b$ if and only if dividing $b$ by $a$ leaves a remainder of exactly zero. Formally, there exists some integer $c$ such that:
$$b = a \times c$$
Because multiplication is commutative, finding $a$ automatically gives you $c$. They are bound together as a *factor pair*.

**2. The Square Root Cutoff (Algorithmic Efficiency)**
You never need to check for factors greater than the square root of your target number $n$. Why? Suppose $a \times b = n$. If *both* $a$ and $b$ were strictly greater than $\sqrt{n}$, their product would be greater than $\sqrt{n} \times \sqrt{n} = n$. Therefore, at least one factor in every pair must be less than or equal to $\sqrt{n}$. Once you check up to $\sqrt{n}$, you have found the smaller half of every pair, which means you have already found the larger half too.

**3. Finite vs. Infinite**
A number has a strictly limited, finite set of factors (its building blocks). However, a number has an infinitely large set of multiples (its footprint). 

## Worked example
**Problem:** Find all factors of $36$.

**Step 1:** Calculate the stopping point. $\sqrt{36} = 6$. We only need to test integers from $1$ to $6$.
**Step 2:** Test $1$. $36 / 1 = 36$. Pair: $(1, 36)$.
**Step 3:** Test $2$. $36 / 2 = 18$. Pair: $(2, 18)$.
**Step 4:** Test $3$. $36 / 3 = 12$. Pair: $(3, 12)$.
**Step 5:** Test $4$. $36 / 4 = 9$. Pair: $(4, 9)$.
**Step 6:** Test $5$. $36 / 5 = 7.2$. Not an integer. Skip.
**Step 7:** Test $6$. $36 / 6 = 6$. Pair: $(6, 6)$.
**Step 8:** Compile the ascending list by reading down the left side of the pairs, then up the right side: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36$.

*Reflection:* By stopping at $\sqrt{36}$, we avoided testing $7$ through $35$. The systematic $1$-to-$\sqrt{n}$ approach guarantees no factors are missed and no redundant work is performed.

## Memory technique — remember this forever
**1. The Mnemonic:**
"Factors **F**it inside (finite). Multiples **M**ultiply outward (many)."

**2. Must-Overlearn Facts:**
* If $a \times b = n$, then $a, b$ are factors of $n$.
* If $a \times b = n$, then $n$ is a multiple of both $a$ and $b$.
* The maximum integer you must check to find all factor pairs of $n$ is $\lfloor\sqrt{n}\rfloor$.

**3. Spaced-Repetition Schedule:**
Review the definitions and the square-root cutoff concept at: 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, and 35 days.

**4. First Principles Pathway:**
If you forget the $\sqrt{n}$ rule, write out the factors of $100$. Notice the pairs: $(1, 100), (2, 50), (4, 25), (5, 20), (10, 10)$. Notice how after $10$, the pairs simply reverse: $(20, 5)$. The turning point is always the square root.

## Common mistakes
* **Forgetting $1$ and the number itself:** Every integer $n > 1$ has at least $1$ and $n$ as factors. Students frequently omit these.
* **Confusing the vocabulary:** Being asked for the factors of $5$ and giving $5, 10, 15, 20$ (those are multiples).
* **Brute-forcing past the square root:** Testing if $17$ goes into $100$ when you have already checked up to $10$. It is a massive waste of computational time.

## Self-check
1. Find all factor pairs of $48$ using the square root cutoff method. State the cutoff number.
2. Is $14$ a factor of $112$? Prove it using the formal definition $b = a \times c$.
3. If $x$ is a factor of $y$, and $y$ is a factor of $z$, prove algebraically that $x$ is a factor of $z$.
