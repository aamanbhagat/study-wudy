## What it is

Sigma ($\Sigma$) notation is a compact mathematical syntax used to write the sum of a sequence of numbers generated by a specific rule. A "telescoping sum" is a special type of series where nearly all intermediate terms cancel each other out in pairs, collapsing the massive expression down to just the first and last few terms.

## Why it matters

You cannot read advanced literature in Physics, Computer Science, or Machine Learning without fluency in Sigma notation. In physics, you will constantly approximate complex real-world phenomena using infinite series (like Taylor series) to make orbital mechanics or quantum states computable. In computer science, telescoping sums are the primary tool used to solve linear recurrence relations, which allow you to prove the time complexity (Big-O) of recursive algorithms. 

## When to study it

Do not attempt this until you have mastered:
1. Basic algebraic manipulation and factoring.
2. Partial fraction decomposition (specifically splitting rational expressions).
3. The concept of limits ($N \to \infty$).
If you cannot instantly factor $n^2 + 3n + 2$ or split $\frac{1}{x(x+1)}$ into two separate fractions, stop here and review partial fractions. 

## How to study it (step by step)

1. **Unpack the notation manually:** Take a small sum like $\sum_{i=1}^4 (2i)$ and write it out longhand: $2(1) + 2(2) + 2(3) + 2(4)$. Do this until the notation stops looking like a hieroglyph and starts looking like a simple `for` loop.
2. **Prove linearity:** Write out $\sum_{i=1}^3 (ca_i + b_i)$ longhand, rearrange the terms using basic algebra, and factor out the $c$ to prove to yourself that it equals $c\sum_{i=1}^3 a_i + \sum_{i=1}^3 b_i$. 
3. **Master index shifting:** Practice rewriting sums to start at different indices. Rewrite $\sum_{i=1}^5 i^2$ as a sum starting at $i=0$. 
4. **Learn the partial fraction trigger:** Recognize that denominators with multiplied factors (like $\frac{1}{n(n+k)}$) are begging to be split into subtracted fractions. This subtraction is what causes the telescoping collapse.
5. **Write the partial sum ($S_N$):** For telescoping sums, always write out the first three terms and the last two terms up to an arbitrary integer $N$. Physically draw lines through the terms that cancel.
6. **Take the limit:** Once the sum is collapsed, take $\lim_{N \to \infty} S_N$ to find the value of the infinite series.

## Key ideas, with intuition

**The Anatomy of Sigma**
In the expression $\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(i)$, $i$ is a "dummy variable." It only exists inside the sum to keep track of the current step. $1$ is the lower bound, $N$ is the upper bound, and $f(i)$ is the rule. 

**Linearity of Summation**
Because a sum is just a string of additions, the associative and commutative properties apply. You can pull constants out and split sums apart:
$$ \sum_{k=1}^n (c \cdot a_k + b_k) = c \sum_{k=1}^n a_k + \sum_{k=1}^n b_k $$

**The Telescoping Mechanism**
If a sequence can be written as the difference of consecutive terms of another sequence, $a_k = b_k - b_{k+1}$, the sum collapses entirely. Watch the interior terms vanish:
$$ \sum_{k=1}^n (b_k - b_{k+1}) = (b_1 - b_2) + (b_2 - b_3) + \dots + (b_n - b_{n+1}) $$
Because addition is commutative, $-b_2 + b_2 = 0$, and so on, leaving only:
$$ b_1 - b_{n+1} $$

## Worked example

Evaluate the infinite series exactly: 
$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2 + n} $$

**Step 1: Factor the denominator.**
$$ \frac{1}{n^2 + n} = \frac{1}{n(n+1)} $$

**Step 2: Partial fraction decomposition.**
We want $\frac{1}{n(n+1)} = \frac{A}{n} + \frac{B}{n+1}$. 
Multiplying by the denominator gives $1 = A(n+1) + Bn$.
Setting $n=0 \implies A=1$. Setting $n=-1 \implies B=-1$.
So, $\frac{1}{n(n+1)} = \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1}$.

**Step 3: Define the $N$th partial sum.**
Never evaluate to infinity directly. Evaluate up to $N$.
$$ S_N = \sum_{n=1}^N \left( \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1} \right) $$

**Step 4: Expand and cancel.**
$$ S_N = \left(\frac{1}{1} - \frac{1}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) + \dots + \left(\frac{1}{N} - \frac{1}{N+1}\right) $$
The $-\frac{1}{2}$ cancels with $+\frac{1}{2}$, the $-\frac{1}{3}$ cancels with $+\frac{1}{3}$, all the way down.
$$ S_N = 1 - \frac{1}{N+1} $$

**Step 5: Take the limit to infinity.**
$$ \lim_{N \to \infty} S_N = \lim_{N \to \infty} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{N+1} \right) = 1 - 0 = 1 $$

*Reflection:* Writing out the partial sum $S_N$ prevents you from making assumptions about how terms behave at infinity. The partial fraction step was the engine that transformed a standard fraction into a collapsing difference.

## Memory technique — remember this forever

1. **The Hook:** Think of an old pirate's brass "spyglass" telescope. It has many interlocking rings, but when you smash it closed, all the middle rings slide inside one another and disappear. Only the wide glass at the front and the small glass at the eye remain. 
2. **Overlearn this fact:** 
   $$ \frac{1}{n(n+k)} = \frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+k}\right) $$
   This specific partial fraction identity accounts for 90% of the telescoping sums you will face in introductory calculus and physics.
3. **Spaced Repetition Schedule:** Review this concept and re-derive the worked example at 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 16 days, and 35 days.
4. **First Principles Pathway:** Never memorize the final result of a telescoping sum. If you forget what survives, write out $n=1, 2, 3$ and $n=N-1, N$. Physically cross out the terms. The geometry of the cancellation will always reveal the surviving terms.

## Common mistakes

1. **Assuming only one term survives at each end.** If your partial fractions yield a gap of 2 (e.g., $\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+2}$), the first *two* positive terms and the last *two* negative terms will survive. Always write out enough terms to verify the pattern.
2. **Plugging in infinity directly.** Writing $\infty - \infty$ is mathematical nonsense and will lead to incorrect conclusions. Always define a finite upper bound $N$, collapse the sum, and *then* take the limit.
3. **Off-by-one errors.** Blindly applying a formula without checking the starting index of the Sigma notation. If the sum starts at $n=2$, your first surviving term is $\frac{1}{2}$, not $1$.

## Self-check

2. Evaluate exactly: $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2}{n^2 + 2n}$. *(Hint: Write out the first four terms. Watch the gap.)*
